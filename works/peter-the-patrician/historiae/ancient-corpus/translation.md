# Peter the Patrician, Historiae: our English translation

Translated from Karl Müller's text of the fragments (Fragmenta Historicorum Graecorum IV, 1851), one section per fragment, with his numbers. Made with Claude Opus 5.5 on 18 November 2026 (docs/adr/0003). Most fragments come from the Excerpta de legationibus, which open each excerpt with "that"; the English leaves it out. Where Müller adds a parallel passage from Dio or Zosimus, it is translated too, after his Latin heading. Words in round brackets are Müller's, as he prints them; words in square brackets are his supplements.

## 1

Lexicon Seguerianum, in Bekker's Anecdota, p. 117: Thōpeuō, "to flatter", takes the accusative. Peter, in the part on Antony: "So, having flattered him much."

Ibid. p. 130: Apeipe, "forbade", takes the dative. Peter, in the part on the monarchy of Caesar: "He forbade the senators to travel outside Italy."

## 2

The Parthians sent envoys (so Niebuhr, for "went") to Tiberius, asking him to give them a king from among the hostages. And he gave them Phraates, the son of Phraates. When he died on the way (so Dio; "on the road", the manuscript), he sent Tiridates, of the royal family; and so that he might take over the kingdom more easily, he wrote to Mithridates, king of the Iberians, to invade Armenia, so that Artabanus, going to help his son, would leave his own country (read "would depart"). And Tiridates entered and took the kingdom. Yet he did not reign long; for Artabanus, taking on Scythian allies, drove him out without difficulty. And this is what happened among the Parthians.

## 3

Mithridates, king of the Iberians, rebelled and was preparing for war against the Romans. When his mother spoke against it, and, since she could not persuade him, wished to flee, he, wishing to hide what he was about, went on preparing himself, but sent his brother Cotys as envoy, carrying friendly words to Claudius. But Cotys betrayed his embassy and revealed everything to Claudius, and became king of Iberia in place of Mithridates.

## 4

Decebalus, king of the Dacians, sent heralds to Domitian, promising peace; whereupon Domitian sent Fuscus with a great force. Learning this, Decebalus sent an embassy to him again, in mockery, saying that if each of the Romans chose to pay Decebalus two obols every year, he would make peace with him; but if they did not choose this, he would make war and inflict great evils on them.

## 5

Decebalus sent envoys to Trajan from the cap-wearers; for these are the more honoured among them. Before, he had sent long-haired men, who are thought of less account among them. They came before Trajan, threw down their arms, and with their hands bound behind them like captives begged Trajan (supply "to allow") Decebalus to come to talks. Again Decebalus sent an embassy to Trajan, seeking a truce for the present (seeking, N.). He promised to hand over to Trajan all his arms and engines, and the Roman engineers who were with him, and the deserters, and to pull down all the forts he had built, and to give up the land that Trajan had conquered, and to count Trajan's enemies and the Romans' as his own, and not to receive deserters, nor to keep any Roman soldier near him. So Trajan accepted the embassy on these terms. And Decebalus threw down his arms before him, fell to the ground and did obeisance, and sent envoys to Rome, who likewise bound their hands as captives.

The same is read in the Excerpts on embassies from Dio Cassius's history (68.14), as Valesius notes: He had sent envoys even before his defeat, no longer from the long-haired men, as before, but the best of the cap-wearers. And they threw down their arms, cast themselves on the ground, and begged Trajan above all that Decebalus himself be allowed to come into his sight and to talks with him, since he would do all that was ordered; or if not, that someone at least be sent to make terms with him. And Sura was sent, and Claudius Livianus the prefect. But nothing was done. For Decebalus did not dare to meet even them. And he sent some again then. Trajan took fortified mountains, and found in them the arms and the engines and the captives, and the standard that had been taken in Fuscus's time. For these reasons Decebalus, ... readily agreed to everything that was ordered; not that he meant to keep to it, but so as to get breathing space from his present troubles.

## 6

When six thousand Langobardi and Obii had crossed the Danube, and the forces under Vindex had got there first, the barbarians were put to complete flight. At this outcome the barbarians, afraid after their first attempt, sent envoys to Aelius Bassus, who governed Pannonia: Ballomarius, the king of the Marcomanni, and ten others, choosing one from each people. And the envoys, having confirmed the peace with oaths, went home.

## 7

The Quadi sent envoys to Marcus asking for peace, and got it. And they gave many horses and many cattle, and released captives, thirteen thousand then, and very many others later. The Astingi and the Lacringi also came to help Marcus.

On the second passage compare Dio 71.12 (Excerpts on embassies): The Astingi ... came into Dacia to settle, in the hope of getting both money and land in return for an alliance, and so on.

## 8

The Carpi, a people jealous of the yearly payments made to the Goths, sent an embassy to Tullius (Julius? N.) Menophilus, arrogantly demanding money. He was duke of Moesia and drilled the army every day; and having learned beforehand of their arrogance, for many days he did not receive them, giving them leave to watch the soldiers drilling. And to bring down their pride by the delay, he sat on a high platform, with the highest officers of the camp standing by, and received them, taking no account of them, but while they were stating their embassy he kept talking with others, as if he had other, weightier business. They, thoroughly frightened, said nothing else but this: "Why do the Goths receive so much money from you, and we do not?" He said: "The emperor is master of much money, and gives it to those who ask him." They went on: "Then let him count us among those who ask, and give us as much; for we are better than they are." And Menophilus laughed and said: "I must report this too to the emperor. Come back to this place in four months, and you will have your answer." And he moved on there and again drilled the soldiers. The Carpi came after the four months, and putting on the same show for them he found another delay of three months. And again he received them in another camp in the same way, and gave them the answer: "As for any promise, the emperor gives you nothing at all; but if you need help, go and throw yourselves on your faces and beg him; and it is likely you will be helped." They went away indignant, and for the three years that Menophilus's command lasted they kept quiet.

## 9

Valerian, wary of the Persians' attack (for plague had struck his army, and the Moors most of all), gathered untold gold and sent envoys to Shapur, wishing to end the war with great gifts. Shapur, learning about the plague and the more elated by Valerian's plea, kept the envoys waiting, sent them back with nothing done, and at once followed after them.

I think that in the part after Dio Peter used the same source (Dexippus, it seems) as Zosimus, who has this (1.36): When a plague fell on the camps and destroyed the greater part of them, Shapur, attacking the East, subdued everything; and as Valerian, through softness and slackness of life, despaired of helping matters that had come to the last extremity, and wished to end the war by giving money, Shapur sent back with nothing done the envoys sent for this purpose, and so on.

## 10

Odaenathus courted Shapur greatly, as one who had far surpassed the Romans. Wishing to win him over, he sent magnificent gifts and other goods, which Persia did not produce in plenty, loaded on camels. And he sent letters with the force of a petition, saying that he had done nothing against the Persians. Shapur ordered his servants to take the gifts and throw them into the river, tore the letters to pieces, and declared: "Who is he, and from where, that he dared to write to his master? And now, if he wants a lighter punishment, let him bind his hands behind him and fall at my feet. If not, let him know that I shall destroy him, his family and his country."

## 11

Shapur, king of the Persians, having crossed the Euphrates with his own army, .... [the soldiers, supplied by Niebuhr] greeted one another and rejoiced as having escaped a danger that had been driven off. And he sent to the soldiers at Edessa, promising to give them all the Syrian money he had with him, if they would let him pass undisturbed, and not choose a risk that would bring them into uncertainty and cause him trouble and delay. For he said that he offered this not because he feared them, but because he was hurrying to hold the festival at home, and did not want his journey to be worn out and put off. And the soldiers chose to take the gold without risk and to let them pass.

## 12

The Vandals, defeated, sent an embassy to Aurelian, asking for peace. Aurelian received them gladly, made a treaty with them and withdrew.

## 13

Apharban, who was as close a friend as anyone to Narses, king of the Persians, was sent on an embassy and met Galerius as a suppliant. Given leave to speak, he said: "It is plain to the human race that the Roman and the Persian empires are like two lamps; and that, like eyes, each should be adorned by the brightness of the other, and not be angry with each other forever, to their mutual destruction. For this is thought not virtue, but rather levity or weakness; for thinking that those who come after them will not be able to help themselves, they are eager to destroy their opponents. Yet Narses ought not to be thought weaker than the other kings; but Galerius so surpasses the other kings that Narses has justly been defeated by him alone, though he has not fallen short of the worth of his own ancestors." Besides this, Apharban said that he had been instructed by Narses, since they were (read "he was", N.) reasonable, to entrust the rights of his own kingdom to the Romans' humanity. So he did not even bring the oaths on which the peace should be made, but gave the whole matter to the emperor's judgement; except that he asked only that his children and wives be given back to him, saying that by their return he would be more bound by kindnesses than if he prevailed by arms; nor could he even now give thanks as they deserved, that in captivity they had suffered no outrage, but had been treated as if they were about to be restored to their own rank. In all this he also called to mind how changeable human affairs are. Galerius, seeming angry at this, stirred his whole body and answered that it ill became the Persians to ask others to remember the changes of human affairs, when they themselves, whenever they seize a chance, never stop pressing on men's misfortunes. "For you kept a fine measure in victory in Valerian's time, you who deceived him with tricks, seized him, and did not release him until extreme old age and a dishonoured death; and then after his death, preserving his skin by some foul art, you brought undying outrage on a mortal body." Having gone through this, the emperor said that he was swayed not by what the Persians had reminded him of through the embassy, that one must look to human fortune (for that was rather a reason to be moved to anger, if one looked at what the Persians had done), but by following in the steps of his own ancestors, whose custom it was to spare the subject and to fight down those who resisted; and he ordered the envoy to report to his own king the goodness of the Romans, whose valour he had experienced, and to expect before long that they would come to him at the emperor's pleasure.

## 14

Galerius and Diocletian met at Nisibis, where, after taking counsel together, they sent to Persia as envoy Sicorius Probus, secretary of the memoria. Narses received him kindly, in hope of what had been promised. And Narses used a certain delay. For as if wishing to let Sicorius and his fellow envoys recover from their weariness, he drew Sicorius on as far as the river Asproudis in Media, though Sicorius knew what was happening, until those who had been scattered here and there by the war were gathered. And then, in the inner rooms of the palace, he sent away all the others and, content with the presence of Apharban, Archapetes and Barsaborses, of whom one was praetorian prefect and the other held the command of Symius, he allowed Probus to set out the embassy. The heads of the embassy were these: that in the eastern region the Romans should have Intelene with Sophene, and Arzanene with the Carduenes and Zabdicene; that the river Tigris should be the boundary of each state; that the fort of Zintha, lying on the border of Media, should be the frontier of Armenia; that the king of Iberia should owe the insignia of his kingdom to the Romans; and that the place for trade should be the city of Nisibis, which lies by the Tigris. Narses, hearing this, since his present fortune allowed him to refuse none of it, agreed to it all; except that, so as not to seem to do everything under compulsion, he refused only that Nisibis be the place of trade. But Sicorius said: "You must give way on this; for the embassy has no full powers, and nothing about this was entrusted to it by the emperors." These terms being agreed, his wives and children were given back to Narses, their chastity having been kept pure, to the emperors' honour.

## 15

Licinius sent Count Mestrianus as envoy to Constantine. When Mestrianus arrived, the emperor put him off for some time. After this, looking to the uncertainty of the war, and also because Licinius's men, in a surprise attack, had seized his baggage along with the imperial retinue, he received him. Mestrianus asked peace for the two emperors, saying that the one who had conquered his own kin ought not to be harsh against them. For whatever was lost would be lost thereafter to the victor, not to the defeated; and one who refuses peace to one man becomes the cause of many civil wars. The emperor, showing the greatness of his anger by his face and the movement of his body, and barely letting out his voice, said: "Not to this end have we come through to the present (have we come through? B.), nor have we come here fighting and conquering from the Ocean, that we should refuse to have our own brother-in-law as partner, because of his crimes, and renounce our kinship with him, and yet receive a worthless slave with him into the palace." So he ordered Mestrianus to leave this part of the embassy alone and say whether he wanted to ask anything else; and it was decided that Valens should be put out of the imperial office.

## 16

Magnentius and Vetranio sent envoys to Constantius. Those sent were Rufinus and Marcellinus, the one praetorian prefect, the other a general, and Nunechius, prefect of the senate, and Maximus besides, to urge Constantius to keep from arms and to hold the first rank in the empire. Through them Magnentius promised to give Constantius his own daughter as wife, on condition that he himself took Constantia, Constantius's sister. So the emperor received the envoys of Vetranio and Magnentius, among whom Nunechius at once said in his opening that Magnentius was asking for peace, and many times he reminded Constantius of the present state of affairs, saying that he ought not rashly, already worn out by wars, to challenge to war two emperors against him (against himself, B.), experienced in soldiering, of one mind with each other and still fresh; who, if they did not agree on peace, he would not wish to learn from anywhere else what sort and how great they would be when drawn up in civil war, whether one alone came or both together, than from what they had already done, when through their battle lines triumphs were won for him and his family. Hearing this embassy, the emperor was full of cares. And falling asleep he saw a vision: his father seemed to come down as if from on high, holding Constans, whom Magnentius had killed, by the hand, and bringing him to him to speak these words: "Constantius, see Constans, descendant of many emperors, my son and your brother, destroyed (destroyed, B.) by a tyrant. Do not then stand by and see the empire divided or the state overturned, nor fear threats, but put the glory that will come to you from this before any outcome, and do not see your brother unavenged." After this vision Constantius woke, and the envoys except Rufinus were put under guard ....

## 17

In the time of Constantius Persian envoys came. The leader of the embassy, Narses, tempering the harshness of the letter he carried with the mildness of his own manners, gave it to be read.

## 18

The barbarians asked Julian the Apostate for peace; he came to make a treaty with them, and sought to take hostages. The barbarians said that he had very many [captives of theirs. And he answered that he held these, supplied by Niebuhr] by war, not given by them; but that now he sought pledges of peace, if there were any among them fit for this. They denied nothing, but agreed to give whomever he chose, and he asked to take the king's son, whom he held as a captive, as alone enough in place of many. The barbarians, supposing that he was already dead, wailing and groaning with their king, begged the Caesar not to ask the impossible, demanding as hostages those already dead; and said this was proof that he did not wish to make a treaty.
