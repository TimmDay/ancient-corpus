# Dexippus, Chronica: our English translation

Translated from Karl Müller's text of the fragments (Fragmenta Historicorum Graecorum III, 1849), one section per fragment, with his numbers. Made with Claude Opus 5.5 on 13 November 2026 (docs/adr/0003). Most fragments are other writers reporting what Dexippus said; Müller gives the Historia Augusta's in Latin, and those are translated from the Latin. Words in round brackets are Müller's explanations, as he prints them.

## 3

The return of the Heraclidae, led by Hyllus, the elder son of Heracles, in the war against the Peloponnesians, which went on for a good many years between the Peloponnesians and the Heraclidae. At that time the island of Rhodes was settled by the Lacedaemonians, as Dexippus (Zeuxippus, Goar) relates, when they moved out of the Peloponnese because of the attack of the Heraclidae.

## 4

So Alexander, in his twentieth year, according to Dexippus, trained in every bodily exercise and the truest pupil of the inspired Aristotle, came to his father's throne.

## 5

In these times (those of the emperor Claudius) the bird called the phoenix appeared, as the Egyptians relate, having appeared 654 (650, manuscripts A and B) years before, and Dexippus agrees.

## 6

Dexippus, in the tenth book of the Chronica, says this: "And the Macedonians took by storm Epidamnus, which was later renamed Dyrrachium, a large and prosperous city of Macedonia."

## 7

The Succhaei, a Moorish people, as Dexippus says in the tenth book of the Chronica.

## 8

The Heluri, a Scythian people, about whom Dexippus writes in the twelfth book of the Chronica.

## 9

Dexippus says that he (Alexander Severus) married the daughter of a certain Martianus, and gave her father the title of Caesar; but when Martianus wanted to kill Alexander by treachery, the plot was found out, and he was put to death and his wife cast off. He also says that Antoninus Heliogabalus was Alexander's uncle, not the son of his mother's sister.

## 10

When Gordian had been hailed as emperor, the young men who had begun the deed threw down Maximinus's statues, smashed his portraits and erased his name in public; and they called Gordian himself Africanus. Some add that the name Africanus was given to Gordian not because he began to reign in Africa, but because he traced his descent from the family of the Scipios. In most books, however, I find that both this Gordian and his son were hailed as emperors together, and given the surname Antoninus; others say Antonius. After this they came to Carthage with royal pomp and with the fasces wreathed in laurel; and the son, his father's legate, was girded, after the example of the Scipios, as Dexippus, the writer of Greek history, says, with the power of the sword.

## 11

Cordus said that he (the younger Gordian) never wanted to have a wife. Against this, Dexippus thinks that Gordian the Third, who after this, still a little boy, gained the empire with Balbinus and Pupienus or Maximus, was his son.

*Chapter 23*: Dexippus indeed declares that Gordian the Third was born to Gordian's son.

*Chapter 2*: There were not two Gordians, as some ignorant writers say, but three; and they could have learned this from the teaching of Arrianus, a writer of Greek history, and likewise of Dexippus, a Greek author, who, though briefly, still went through everything faithfully.

## 12

When, then, two men of consular rank and of great distinction, Maximus and Balbinus (of whom Maximus is passed over in silence in most histories, and the name of Pupienus put in his place, although both Dexippus and Arabianus say that Maximus and Balbinus were chosen against Maximinus after the Gordians; of these two the one was famous for his goodness, the other for his courage and strictness), had entered the senate house, etc.

*Chapter 15*: Dexippus, a Greek writer, says that Maximus and Balbinus were made emperors against Maximinus after the two Gordians, and that Maximinus was defeated by Maximus, not by Pupienus.

*Chapter 7*: One thing certainly must nowhere be passed over in silence: Dexippus and Arrianus and many other Greeks wrote that Maximus and Balbinus were made emperors against Maximinus, that Maximus was sent out with an army and prepared the war at Ravenna, but did not see Aquileia until he had already won; while the Latin writers said that it was not Maximus but Pupienus who fought against Maximinus at Aquileia, and that he won. How this error arose I cannot tell, unless perhaps Pupienus and Maximus are the same man. (He is M. Clodius Pupienus Maximus.)

## 13

Then, when Maximinus's head was carried round on a pike and there was great rejoicing at it, it was almost matched by the son's head being carried round as well. Dexippus adds that Maximinus was so hated that, when the Gordians had been killed, the senate created twenty men to set against Maximinus, and among them were Balbinus and Maximus, whom they made emperors against him. He also adds that in sight of Maximinus, already deserted by his soldiers, Anolinus, his praetorian prefect, and his son were killed.

## 14

Under these emperors the Carpi fought against the Moesians, and at that time came the beginning of the Scythian war and the destruction of Histria, or, as Dexippus says, of the city of Histria. Dexippus praises Balbinus highly, and says that he met the soldiers with a brave spirit and was killed, so that he did not fear death; he says that he was trained in every branch of learning. But he denies that Maximinus was such a man as most of the Greeks have said. He adds, moreover, that the people of Aquileia so hated Maximus that they made bowstrings from their women's hair and shot arrows with them. Dexippus and Herodian, who went through the history of these emperors, say that Maximus and Balbinus were the emperors chosen by the senate against Maximinus after the death of the two Gordians in Africa, and with them the boy, the third Gordian, was also chosen.

## 15

Dexippus tells that Titus, a tribune of the Moors, who had been left a private citizen by Maximinus, took the empire, from fear of a violent death, as some say, but, as most declare, unwillingly and forced to it by the soldiers; and that a few days after the revolt which Magnus, a man of consular rank, had prepared against Maximinus had been punished, he was killed by his own soldiers. He reigned, they say, for six months.

## 16

The Scythians who are called Goths crossed the river Ister in the time of Decius in very great numbers and overran the Roman dominion. They surrounded the Moesians, who had fled to Nicopolis. Decius came against them, as Dexippus relates, and killed thirty thousand, but was worsted in the battle, so that he even lost Philippopolis, which they took, and many Thracians were slain. As the Scythians were going back to their own lands, the same Decius attacked them and was killed at Abrytus, the place called the Forum of Thembronius, together with his son, by night, the fighter against God. And the Scythians went back with very many captives and much booty. And the armies proclaimed as emperor Gallus, who had once been consul, together with Volusianus, the son of Decius; and they reigned, according to Dexippus, eighteen months, doing nothing worth mention; according to some others, three years, and according to others, two years.

## 16a

Up to this point (that is, up to Praxidamas (Prasidamas, the manuscript) of Alexandria, who won the stadium race in the 249th Olympiad) we have found the record of the Olympiads. So much for Eusebius. But other chroniclers, and Dexippus of Athens, also record the Olympiads that followed and those who won in them. Dexippus, at any rate, who wrote his chronicle history down to the two hundred and sixty-second Olympiad (AD 269), says that Dionysius of Alexandria won in that one.
