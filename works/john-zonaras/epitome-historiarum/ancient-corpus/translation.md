# Zonaras, Epitome Historiarum: our English translation

Translated from Dindorf's Greek (Teubner, 1868–75), one section per chapter, with his chapter numbers. Made with Claude Opus 5.5 on 2 and 3 November 2026 (docs/adr/0003). Books 12 and 13 so far. Where a chapter begins or ends in the middle of a sentence, the English does too, following the Greek.

## 12.1

When Antoninus, who had been adopted by Hadrian and made Caesar and emperor, became sole ruler, the senate would not vote Hadrian the honours due to a hero, because of the murders of very distinguished men that he had committed. Antoninus therefore spoke to them at length, with tears, and said among other things: "Then I shall not rule you either; for everything he did, and my own appointment is one of those things, will be annulled, as the acts of a man most hateful and wicked to you." The senate, out of respect for him, and no less out of caution towards the soldiers, voted the honours to the dead man. He was named Pius because, just after he had taken the sole rule, many men were accused and some were demanded by name for execution, but he punished no one, saying: "I must not begin my care of you with deeds like these."

Through the whole of his reign he was just and good and no burden to any of his subjects, the Christians included: he not only did them no harm but even held them worthy of respect. He is said to have been inquisitive and exacting about small things, so that those who mocked him called him "the cumin-splitter". Yet he did not make money out of his subjects for all that. Once, when he was short of silver with wars pressing, he neither devised a new tax nor brought himself to ask anyone for money, but set out in the market-place all the treasures of the palace, and whatever ornaments his wife had, and invited anyone who wished to buy them. From this he raised money and paid it out to the soldiers. When he had won the war he gained many times as much, and made a proclamation that any of those who had bought the imperial possessions might give back what they had bought and receive the price. Some did so, but most declined, and he forced no one to give back what he had acquired.

He is said to have made the law that when children die without a will their parents are declared their heirs, and that children who make a will and are childless must leave the parents their lawful share.

In his reign a terrible earthquake happened about Bithynia and the Hellespont, by which several cities fell, and Cyzicus suffered most. At that time the famous temple there also fell, whose columns are said to have been four fathoms thick and to have risen to a height of fifty cubits, each made of a single stone, if anyone does not find this hard to believe; and all the rest of that building was wonderful.

It is told of this emperor that he also burned the senate's decree, made at the command of Julius Caesar, which laid down that no one might make a will unless he left a fixed share to the public treasury. Hence, it is thought, it is still written in wills to this day: "and to the imperial treasury I leave such-and-such." Antoninus Pius died an old man, by a death like a gentle sleep, having reigned alone for twenty-four years.

In his first year Telesphorus, bishop of Rome, having completed eleven years in his ministry and crowned with martyrdom, as Irenaeus has recorded, came to a most blessed end; and Hyginus was set on the throne of Rome in his place. Under him Valentinus became a heresiarch, and Cerdo the founder of the heresy of Marcion. When Hyginus died after the fourth year of his high-priesthood at Rome, Pius presided over the church of the Romans. Mark led that of the Alexandrians, after Eumenes had completed his thirteenth year in the high-priesthood; and when Mark passed to the other life after ten years, Celadion received the ministry. At Rome, when Pius died in the fifteenth year of his episcopate, Anicetus was appointed in his place. At this time also Justin, philosopher and martyr, flourished, contending for the right doctrine; he set out a treatise against Marcion and wrote a defence of the Christians addressed to Antoninus Pius. Moved by it, this emperor sent a decree to the council of Asia that no Christian should be punished for his religion, but that if anyone were accused by someone on that account, the accused should be acquitted and the accuser become liable to penalty. These things happened under Pius.

## 12.2

After his death Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, who had been adopted by him, as has been recorded, received the sole rule; and at once he made Lucius Verus Commodus, the son of Lucius Commodus, partner in his power. For Marcus was weak in body and so given to study that even as emperor he was not ashamed to go to a teacher: he studied philosophy with Sextus the Boeotian and attended Hermogenes the rhetorician, and held most to the Stoics. Lucius, being younger, was strong for hard work and suited by nature to handling military affairs. So Marcus married him to his daughter Lucilla and entrusted to him the war against the Parthians, since Vologaeses had shot down many Romans with arrows, destroyed a whole camp, declared himself an enemy of the Romans and was advancing, a terror, on the cities of Syria. Lucius came to Antioch and assigned the armies to Cassius, who engaged Vologaeses, defeated him and pursued him as far as Seleucia, burned that city and razed his palace at Ctesiphon. These things gave Lucius pride and arrogance, so that afterwards he even plotted against his father-in-law and emperor; but before he could do anything he perished by poison.

After this Cassius was appointed governor of all Asia. The emperor made war for a long time on the barbarians about the Ister, the Iazyges and the Marcomanni and others at other times, using Pannonia as his base. The Celts beyond the Rhine also drove as far as Italy; among their dead were found the bodies of women in armour. He ruled with such restraint and self-command that in such great wars he neither said anything to flatter the soldiers nor did anything out of fear. When he was not occupied with wars he sat in judgement, and he ordered more water to be poured for the orators who spoke by the water-clock, so that he might examine the justice of a case from every side. He was so hard-working that he judged even at night. He did, wrote and said nothing carelessly, thinking that an emperor ought not to do even the smallest thing carelessly, and this though his body was weak.

This emperor subdued the Marcomanni and the Iazyges in many great struggles and dangers. Against the Quadi too he waged a heavy war, and God granted him an unexpected victory. The Quadi had surrounded the Romans, who locked their shields together and defended themselves stoutly. The barbarians held off from fighting but blocked the country round about, so that the Romans could draw water from nowhere, thinking to overcome them more easily, since they would not be able to hold out against thirst and heat. When the Romans were worn down by toil, heat, thirst and wounds, clouds suddenly covered the sky and it rained heavily. This was not, as Dio has recorded, because an Egyptian magician called on Hermes and by his spells was able to bring the rain through him, but because God was called upon by the prayers of Christians and by a miracle rescued both them and the whole army. For there was in the Roman army at that time a legion all of whose men were Christians. The story is that when the emperor was at a loss and afraid for the whole army, the prefect of the guard told him that there was nothing the race called Christians could not do, and that there was a whole unit of such men in the army. On hearing this he begged them to call on their own God. When they had prayed, the enemy were struck by lightning and a rainstorm broke over the Romans. Marcus was astonished and honoured the Christians by a decree, and named that unit the Thunderbolt legion. That the legion was so named is agreed by the other Greeks and by Dio, but they do not add the reason. Eusebius also mentions this miracle in his Ecclesiastical History. Dio, however, says that while the rain was falling the Romans, busy with drinking, would almost have perished, since the barbarians then attacked them, had not violent hail fallen and the enemy been struck by many thunderbolts.

## 12.3

Cassius, while staying in Syria, rebelled. He was a Syrian, but an excellent man, such as one would pray to have as emperor. He was moved to revolt when a false report was brought to him that Marcus had died; and having once set out on the venture he did not draw back, though he learned soon after that the report was false, but prepared to hold the power by war. Marcus called the soldiers together, addressed them and encouraged them for the war. While he was making ready, news came of very many victories over various barbarians, and of the killing of Cassius: a centurion came up to him as he walked and suddenly wounded him, though not mortally, and a decurion finished the killing. So he perished, having dreamed of rule for three months and six days; and his son, who was elsewhere, was also killed. The emperor put to death none of those who had revolted with him, but treated them all with mildness. He wrote to the senate that none of Cassius's accomplices should die: "Heaven forbid," he said, "that any one of you should be executed on my account, by my vote or yours. If I do not obtain this, I shall hasten to die." So in everything he was both pious and good. A law was made at that time that no one should govern the province he came from, because Cassius had rebelled while governing Syria, where his own home was.

Marcus came to Athens and bestowed honours on the Athenians and appointed teachers in every branch of learning, who received a yearly salary from the treasury. On coming to Rome he remitted their debts to all who owed money to the imperial treasury and the public treasury. He gave money to cities, and ordered Smyrna, which had suffered from an earthquake, to be rebuilt. When the Scythian peoples stirred again, he married his son to Crispina sooner than he wished, and marched out against them. He asked for money from the public treasury, for he said that everything belonged to the senate and the people. When the barbarians had been defeated he was hailed imperator for the tenth time. Had he lived longer he would have brought everything there under the Romans; but he died, the doctors having made away with him, it is said, to please Commodus. As he was dying he commended Commodus to the soldiers, so that he should not seem to die by his doing; and to the tribune who asked for the watchword he said, "Go to the rising sun; for I am already setting." So, having ruled excellently, he died, having lived fifty-nine years less thirty-eight days and reigned nineteen years and eleven days. He was a good man by nature, and was made better still by education. Yet he did not have good fortune worthy of his virtue. One might admire him the more for this, that though he met with strange and extraordinary troubles, he himself came through them and kept the empire safe.

During his reign the holy Polycarp, high priest of Smyrna, put on the martyr's crown; and Justin, great in words and greater in confession, contended to the end, many of whose writings survive to this day. In the eighth year of the reign of this Marcus Verus Antoninus, Anicetus died, having presided over the church of the Romans for eleven years, and Soter succeeded him. When Celadion had completed his life after fourteen years, Agrippinus was entrusted with the episcopate of the Alexandrians. In the church of the Antiochenes, after Hero, Cornelius presided fourth; Eros succeeded him fifth, and Theophilus succeeded Eros sixth, who also armed himself in writing against Marcion; and Maximinus succeeded him seventh. When Soter, high priest of the Romans, departed from the things of earth, Eleutherus was brought in in his place.

## 12.4

Commodus, the son of Marcus Antoninus, though brought up and trained by his father in the best way, did not turn out according to the education he had received. He was not wicked, and was by nature far from villainy; but being cowardly, and from simplicity obeying those about him, his character was corrupted and he was led into licentiousness and bloodshed. He was nineteen years old when he took power. So he thrust aside the counsel of the best men of the senate, whom his father had set over him as guardians, made a treaty with the barbarians and went back to Rome, unwilling to toil and in love with an easy life.

For this reason many plots were made against him, and he killed a great many people, not only men but women too, and nearly all who were prominent then, except Pompeianus, Pertinax and Victorinus, so that those who wrote of these things could not account for how those men escaped death at that time. As he was entering the hunting theatre Claudius Pompeianus made an attempt on him: holding up a sword in the narrow entrance, he said, "The senate has sent you this." This man was betrothed to the daughter of Lucilla, Commodus's sister, and Lucilla herself shared his ruin, being no more reasonable or chaste than her brother. It was she who persuaded Pompeianus to attack Commodus, and she destroyed both him and herself. Commodus also put to death his wife Crispina, charging her with adultery. If anyone wished to set down exactly in writing each of those he killed, whether through false accusations, or idle suspicions, or great wealth, or brilliant birth, or eminence in learning, or a name for virtue, he would weary those who were going to read the work.

There were also wars while he was sole ruler, against the barbarians beyond Dacia, and the greatest, the British war, to which he sent Ulpius Marcellus, a man who lived with restraint and like a soldier, wholly incorruptible and sleepless by nature and still more by self-discipline. So that he should not even have his fill of bread, he ate it many days old and very stale, so that he could not eat anything beyond what was quite necessary. He did great harm to the barbarians in Britain, and for his excellence Commodus very nearly killed the man.

Perennis, the commander of the guard, was compelled to manage both military and public affairs, since Commodus was taken up with chariot-driving and debauchery. So if anything unpleasant befell the soldiers anywhere, they laid it at his door and were angry with him. The soldiers in Britain, who had mutinied and been reprimanded for it, sent fifteen hundred of their number into Italy. Commodus went out to meet them as they neared Rome and said, "Fellow soldiers, what do you want, that you are here?" They said, "We have come because Perennis is plotting against you." He believed them and handed the man over; and the soldiers maltreated him and cut him down, though he had conducted his office most incorruptibly and with great restraint.

Rid of him, the imperial freedmen, of whom Cleander was the chief, did every worst thing without fear: they sold everything, and were insolent and debauched. Cleander, raised high by fortune, gave away and sold seats in the senate, military commands, procuratorships, governorships and, in a word, everything. Some bought the rank of senator with all they had, so that it was said of Julius Solon, a very obscure man, that he had been stripped of his property and exiled into the senate. He appointed twenty-five consuls for one year, which never happened before or after. Raising money from every quarter, he gained a very great deal, and gave most of it to Commodus and his concubines.

Yet raised so high by fortune, he perished in disgrace. When a famine happened in Rome, Papirius Dionysius, who was in charge of the grain, made it worse, so that the Romans would hate Cleander and destroy him as the cause of the trouble through his thefts. A horse race was being held; and as the horses were about to compete, a crowd of children ran into the hippodrome, led by a tall and grim-looking girl, who was afterwards thought to have been a spirit. The children cried out together, and at this the people shouted and rushed to Commodus, who was in a suburb, praying for him and cursing Cleander. He sent soldiers against them, who wounded and killed some but could not hold the people back; they pressed on, trusting in their numbers and in the strength of the guards. As they drew near, Commodus, being a great coward, was so frightened that he at once ordered Cleander and his child to be killed. The child was dashed to the ground and killed; the Romans took Cleander's body, dragged it about and abused it, and his head was carried round on a spear. They also killed some others of those who had power with the emperor.

## 12.5

Commodus kept killing and doing away with the distinguished men, and was most oppressive to all the Romans, compelling them to vote him under compulsion all that they had voted his father willingly from goodwill. Among very many other honours voted to him was a golden statue weighing a thousand pounds. He decreed that all the months should be named after him, and they were counted thus: Amazonius, Invictus, Felix, Pius, Lucius, Aelius, Aurelius, Commodus, Augustus, Herculeus, Romanus, Exsuperatorius. For he gave himself all these names, and wrote to the senate thus: "The emperor Caesar Lucius Aelius Aurelius Commodus Augustus Pius Felix Invictus Romanus Hercules, high priest, holding the tribunician power for the eighteenth time, imperator for the eighth time, consul for the seventh time, father of his country, to the consuls, praetors, tribunes and the fortunate Commodian senate, greeting." Many statues were set up to him in the guise of Hercules. It was voted that the age of his reign should be called and written "golden" after him; for among his other names he was called both "golden" and "god".

Spending a great deal, he ran out of money; so he brought charges against men and women, and some he killed, while to others he sold their lives for their property. He drove chariots at home, for he was ashamed to do it in public, and fought as a gladiator: at home to the death, but in the theatre without steel and without human blood. At home he shaved the hair of some men, and cut off the noses of some, the ears of others, and something else from others. When he entered the theatre he wore the dress of Hermes, carrying a golden herald's staff. He was proud too of being left-handed. When he grew tired in his contests he drank, and the senators and knights present cried out "Long may you live!", and they were ordered to shout continually "You are lord!" and "You are first!" and "You will win the most fortunate of all victories!" and "Amazonius, you conquer!" Once he gathered together those who had lost their feet or been crippled in them by disease or accident, wound the likeness of serpents about their knees, and killed them by striking them with a club, as if he were Hercules killing the Giants.

When he was about to fight as a gladiator again, he ordered the knights and the senate to come into the theatre in the knights' dress and in cloaks, which was not the custom unless an emperor had died; and on the last day of the contests his helmet was carried out through the gate by which the dead were carried out. All thought these signs that they would soon be rid of his tyranny. And this happened shortly after. Aemilius Laetus the prefect and Eclectus the chamberlain, vexed at what he was doing, urged him not to do these things. He threatened them and was angry; and they, afraid, plotted against him and gave him poison in beef. When he was not quickly overcome by the poison, but vomited it up, suspected what had happened and began to threaten, they had him strangled by a certain Narcissus while he was bathing. He had been sole ruler twelve years, nine months and fourteen days, and had lived thirty-one years and four months. With him the house of the true Aurelii ceased to rule.

In the first year of the sole reign of Commodus, Julian was entrusted with the episcopate of Alexandria, after Agrippinus had managed it for twelve years and departed from this life. At that time Clement, the author of the Stromateis, was known, and Pantaenus the philosopher, a student and herald of our mystery. Earlier the narrative recorded that Mark led the church of Jerusalem after the bishops of the circumcision, when the city had been rebuilt by Hadrian and settled with gentiles. Cassian succeeded him, Publius succeeded Cassian, Julian Publius, Gaius Julian and Symmachus Gaius; and Symmachus departed this life in the time of another Gaius. Then another Julian succeeded to the episcopate, and his successor was Capito, and Valens followed Capito, Dolichianus Valens, and after them all Narcissus became the thirtieth bishop of Jerusalem from the apostles. In the tenth year of the reign of Commodus, Eleutherus, who had been bishop of the church of the Romans for thirteen years, departed this life, and Victor presided over the faithful there. When Julian had governed the church of the Alexandrians to the tenth year and paid his debt to nature, Demetrius was entrusted with the ministry of the episcopate. In the church of the Antiochenes, after Maximinus, Serapion oversaw those who bear Christ's name, the eighth high priest from the apostles. At that time Apollonius also, a man famous for learning and philosophy, by a martyr's end in Rome, at a decree of the senate, passed to the eternal dwellings.

## 12.6

So Commodus, having ruined the affairs of the empire, as has been said, was ruined himself; and Eclectus and Laetus chose Pertinax for the sole rule because of his virtue and rank. He, having made sure that Commodus was dead, accepted the rule, went into the camp, and won over the soldiers by Laetus's presence and by lavish promises. Then he went to the senate and said: "I have been named emperor by the army, but I resign the rule because of my age and ill health and the difficulty of affairs." When the senate praised him and chose him, he was declared emperor, and Commodus was voted a public enemy. The senate and people heaped insults on him and pulled down his images. They also wanted to drag his body about and tear it apart; but when Pertinax said that it had already been hidden in the earth, they held back from that, but called him accursed and tyrant and charioteer and gladiator and much else.

Pertinax was a Libyan from Alba Pompeia, of a father not nobly born, and had served as a tribune among the knights. He was easy to approach, listened readily, and answered as he thought fit. So Pertinax took the rule, and besides his other titles was named leader of the senate, after the ancient custom. Whatever had been in disorder was put back in good order by the emperor's management and forethought, and the disgrace of those unjustly put to death was removed by him. There was such a lack of money in the palace that only two hundred and fifty thousand drachmas were found. With difficulty Pertinax gathered silver from statues, arms, furniture and Commodus's favourites, and gave the guards what he had promised and the people a hundred drachmas each. For everything Commodus had used for luxury, gladiatorial fighting and chariot-driving he put up for sale.

Laetus praised Pertinax and insulted Commodus. Some barbarians who had received much gold from Commodus for peace, and were still on the road, he sent for and took the gold from them, saying, "Tell those at home that Pertinax rules"; for they knew the man's name from what they had suffered. Laetus did other things of this kind to discredit Commodus. But he did not stay loyal to Pertinax for long. Not getting what he longed for, he stirred up the soldiers against him. They, prevented by him from plundering, hated him and plotted with Laetus. So they agreed to make the consul Falco emperor, a man distinguished for birth and wealth. Pertinax learned of this while he was staying by the sea to arrange for the grain supply, hurried to the city, came into the senate and addressed it. When the senators were about to condemn Falco, Pertinax cried out: "Heaven forbid that any senator should be put to death while I rule, even justly."

Laetus put some of the soldiers to death as though at Pertinax's command, and the rest, afraid, were in uproar. Two hundred of the boldest drew their swords and rushed to the palace. When they were inside, his wife told him they were there. Though he could have resisted and destroyed them with the night watch and the cavalry, he did neither this nor choose to hide and escape, but, hoping perhaps to overawe them when they saw him and persuade them by speaking, he went to meet them as they came. At first they shrank back out of respect and put their swords in their sheaths; but when one of them leapt forward and said, showing his sword, "The soldiers have sent you this," and struck him, the others joined in and cut down the emperor himself and Eclectus. Then the soldiers stuck Pertinax's head on a spear and carried it round, glorying in the deed. The man had lived sixty-seven years less four months, and ruled only eighty-seven days.

## 12.7

When the murder of Pertinax was reported, Sulpicianus, who had been sent by him to the camp to settle matters there, stayed in it and worked to be declared emperor. Meanwhile Didius Julianus, a money-maker and insatiable, always eager for revolution, who for that reason had been banished by Commodus to Mediolanum, his native city, learned of the death of Pertinax and hurried to the camp. Standing at the gates, he outside and Sulpicianus inside, they bid for Rome and its rule from the soldiers, while some carried the messages and said to each, "He offers so much, and he so much; what will you add?" Since Julianus had promised the soldiers a great deal, they preferred him, fearing above all that Sulpicianus would avenge Pertinax, as his father-in-law.

Julianus was received into the camp and declared emperor, and from there went to the forum and the senate house, bringing a great many guards, so as to frighten both the senate and the people by them. He said to the senate: "You need a ruler, and I, if anyone, am the most worthy to lead you." Though he was hated for his boasting and for the soldiers who surrounded the senate house, out of fear the senate too confirmed his sole rule, and he went up to the palace.

Next day the senators came to him, so as not to be caught resenting him, but the people openly scowled. When the emperor came to the senate house they all shouted out as if at a signal, calling him parricide and robber of the empire. When he pretended not to be angry and promised them some silver, they were indignant at being bribed, and all cried out together, "We do not want it, we will not take it." Julianus ordered those who said this to be killed. The people were roused, remembered Pertinax and jeered at Julianus. Many in many parts of the city held out though wounded and killed, and taking arms they ran together into the hippodrome, where they spent a night and a day without food, calling on the rest of the soldiers, and above all on Pescennius Niger and those with him in Syria. Then, worn down by hunger and sleeplessness, they broke up and kept quiet. Julianus, having seized the rule in this way, used it slavishly, flattering the senate and anyone with influence, granting some things and promising others; he did everything to court the powerful, but was not trusted.

These things were done in Rome. The commanders of the armies, three in number, Severus governor of Pannonia, Niger of Syria and Albinus of Britain, learning about Julianus, were roused to bring him down. Severus, the cleverest of them, reckoned that if they overthrew Julianus the three would dispute the rule among themselves. He decided, therefore, to win one of them over. Since Niger was farther away from him, and he guessed that he was proud at having been called on by the people, as has been said, he turned to Albinus, and wrote to him secretly that he would make him Caesar. Albinus stayed where he was, confident that he would share the rule with Severus. Severus hurried towards Rome.

Learning of this, Julianus had the senate vote Severus a public enemy, prepared for war, and strengthened the palace with lattices and strong doors; for he thought the soldiers would never have killed Pertinax so easily had he been shut in, and he believed that if he were defeated he could survive by shutting himself up there. He also killed Laetus, and sent many men against Severus to murder him by treachery. But when Severus invaded Italy and won over Ravenna, and those sent against him went over to him, and the guards lost heart at Severus's coming, Julianus called the senators together and told them to vote Severus his partner in the rule. Severus wrote to the soldiers that they would suffer no harm if they handed over the murderers of Pertinax; they were persuaded, arrested them, and reported this to Silius Messala, the consul. He gathered the senate in the Athenaeum, so called because those being educated did their exercises in it, and told it what the soldiers had done. The senate condemned Julianus to death and at once proclaimed Severus emperor. Then Julianus was killed in the palace, saying, "What harm have I done? Whom have I killed?" He had lived sixty years, four months and as many days, and ruled sixty days.

## 12.8

Severus, having taken the sole rule, punished with death those soldiers who had killed Pertinax with their own hands; the rest he reproached most bitterly, stripped of their arms, took away their horses and drove out of Rome. Then he entered Rome on foot; the army followed him in arms, the whole city was garlanded with flowers and laurel, and the people, dressed in white, acclaimed him and were eager in the extreme to hear and see him. On entering he made a bold promise, as the good emperors of old had done, that he would kill none of the senators, swore an oath on it, and ordered it to be confirmed by a public decree. But before long he broke this law. For he killed not much later that very Julius Solon who had drafted the decree at his command, and did away with many others; and he did things not to the senate's liking. It had been the custom for the bodyguards to come from Italy and other peoples more seemly in appearance and simpler in character; but he, against custom, filled the city with soldiers most savage to look at, most terrifying to hear and most boorish to talk to.

Signs had come to him foretelling his rule. Once he dreamed that a she-wolf suckled him, as is recorded of Romulus; water welled up, as from a spring, while he slept; and in his sleep the whole senate and people of the Romans came to him and greeted him. Again he dreamed that Pertinax, riding in the Roman forum, was thrown from his horse, and that he himself mounted it as it stood quietly. As a youth he sat down, waking and not in a dream, on the imperial seat, in ignorance and not by design.

Having become sole ruler he honoured Pertinax in many ways, and gave him a most splendid funeral, though he had died long before. Then he marched against Niger, who was an Italian of the knights, though not of the very distinguished ones. After various battles had been fought, at last at Issus in Cilicia, near the place called the Gates, which have this name because of the narrowness of the place (on one side sheer mountains rise up, and on the other deep cliffs go down to the sea), a hard battle was fought and Niger was defeated. Most of his force perished there; he himself was caught in flight and killed. Severus cut off his head, sent it to Byzantium and set it up on a stake, so that the Byzantines might see it and come over.

When Severus was punishing those who had taken Niger's side, a senator, Cassius Clemens, on trial before him, spoke out freely, saying: "I knew neither you nor Niger. I was caught on his side, and I did not wish to fight you but to overthrow Julianus. In this I strove for the same things as you, and did no wrong. Nor did I wrong you by not changing over to you later, for you would not have wanted any of your companions to desert to him either. So examine not our persons or our names, but the facts themselves; for whatever you condemn in us, you will condemn in yourself and your companions too." Severus admired his boldness and let him keep half his property.

The Byzantines did many remarkable things both while Niger lived and after his death, being besieged for three years. They captured ships sailing by, and captured even triremes of the enemy lying in the harbour: divers cut their anchors under water, drove nails into their hulls, tied ropes to these and drew them in, so that the ships seemed to sail towards them of their own accord. When everything they had was used up they still held out, and used timber from their houses for ships and ropes they plaited from their women's hair, which they cut off. When the enemy attacked the walls they hurled stone and bronze statues and horses at them. When every customary food failed them, they soaked hides and ate them; and when these were spent they turned on one another and tasted human flesh. Then, against their will, they surrendered the city, and the Romans put to death all the soldiers and magistrates. Severus was greatly pleased at the capture of Byzantium. He took away the city's freedom and civic rank, made it tributary, confiscated the property of the citizens and gave the city and its territory to the Perinthians. By pulling down the city's walls he grieved the Byzantines no more than by robbing them of the fame they enjoyed from their display of them; but he pulled down a great base for the Romans against the barbarians from Pontus and Asia, and a refuge too, for the walls of Byzantium were very strong.

## 12.9

Severus, mad for glory, marched against the barbarians of Osroene and Adiabene and the Arabians, and coming to Nisibis sent generals and legions from there against those peoples, who ravaged their country and took their cities.

Again a civil war broke out for Severus. He would not give Albinus even the rank of Caesar, while Albinus was asking for that of emperor too. When the world was in turmoil over this, the people, gathered in great numbers at a horse race, lamented most openly, shouting, "How long shall we suffer such things?" and "How long shall we be at war?", and they cried out other such things as if inspired by some divine breath. For how else could so many tens of thousands have said what they said with one voice and without a slip, as if under a chorus-master? At length a battle was fought, and after the war had taken many forms, turns and changes of fortune, Albinus was defeated, Romans having fallen on both sides almost beyond counting, and Albinus killed himself. Severus mocked him as he lay dead and abused him with his tongue, ordered the rest of his body to be thrown away, and sent the head to Rome and set it up on a stake. He killed many of the senators, but released some.

After this he marched against the Parthians, who had taken Mesopotamia. He built boats on the Euphrates, and took Seleucia and Babylon, which had been abandoned. He took Ctesiphon and let the soldiers plunder it, and killed many and took more alive. But he neither pursued Vologaeses, the Parthian ruler, who had withdrawn home, nor held Ctesiphon, but went away as though he had marched only to plunder it.

While he was at war he killed two distinguished men: Julius Crispus, a tribune of the guard, because he was vexed at the hardships of the war and had let slip some remark, and he appointed the soldier who had accused him tribune in his place; and Laetus, because he was proud and loved by the soldiers, who said they would not march unless Laetus led them. He blamed his murder on the soldiers, since, having killed him from envy, he could give no reason.

Then he marched against Hatra. This is an Arabian city in which many other treasures were stored and the offerings to the Sun, to whom the city was dedicated. He achieved nothing, though he had pulled down the outer wall, and after losing many of his own men he withdrew. He came to Palestine and to Egypt, saw all of it and pried into its hidden things; for he was a man to leave nothing divine or human unexamined. He took away from all the sanctuaries the books that held anything secret, as many as he could find.

So much for that. Let me say in passing what I found Dio saying about the Nile, where it rises and why it floods in summer. He says that it rises from Atlas, and that Atlas is a mountain in the Macennite country beside the Ocean itself towards the west, and rises above all mountains, so that because of its height the poets have called it the pillar of heaven. No one has ever climbed to its top or seen its peaks, which are always full of snow, from which much water runs down in the summer, and for this reason the Nile then rises. He says that the Romans learned this exactly from the people of lower Mauretania, who are neighbours of the Macennite country, and that many of the Romans who served there reached Atlas. That is what Dio says about the Nile, though others have not described it so.

## 12.10

Plautianus, the power behind Severus, who held the prefect's authority and had the greatest influence, put to death many men of note and of his own rank. Being insatiable, he demanded from everyone, and left neither province nor city unplundered, and everyone gave more to him than to Severus. He castrated a hundred Romans of good birth, not only boys and youths but also grown men with wives, so that the same men were seen to be eunuchs and husbands, fathers without testicles, castrates with beards. Before this a huge sea monster ran aground in the harbour called Augustus's and was caught; a model of it was made and brought into the hunting show, and it is said to have held fifty bears inside it. When Severus married Plautianus's daughter Plautilla to his son Antoninus, Plautianus gave his daughter as much as would have been enough for many queens. This man, having grown enormously great and very powerful, even more than Severus himself, was plotted against by his son-in-law Antoninus, killed and thrown down into a street. Severus's sons, Antoninus and Geta, having shaken off Plautianus like a tutor, went in for every kind of wickedness.

At that time also Bulla Felix, an Italian, formed a band of six hundred robbers and plundered Italy for two years. Many strove to catch him, but he was not taken, skilfully outwitting his pursuers in many ways; we shall tell one of them as an example of the rest. When a centurion had been ordered by Severus to ambush him, he came to him himself, as if someone else, and assured him that if he followed he would hand the robber over. The centurion was persuaded, and he led him to a thickly wooded place and easily seized him. Then, seating himself on a tribunal as if a magistrate, the robber chief sent for the centurion, shaved the hair of his head and let him go, saying, "Tell your master: feed your slaves, so that they do not turn robber." For he had many of the imperial freedmen who had come over to him. Later, however, he was caught and given to the beasts.

Severus marched into Britain, so that his sons should not go astray and the armies should not grow slack from idleness. There are two very great peoples of the Britons, the Caledonians and the Maeatae. Both inhabit wild mountains and desert and marshy plains, having neither cities nor farming the land, but getting their living from flocks and hunting and nuts; for though there are very many fish about the island they do not eat them. They live in tents, naked and unshod, have their women in common and rear all the children born. They are governed by the people and live by plunder. They fight from chariots, having small swift horses, and on foot, and they run very fast. For arms they use a shield, a short spear and a dagger. They endure hunger and cold and other hardships, and plunge into the marshes and hold out for many days with only their heads above the water, and in the forests they live on roots and bark. They also prepare a food of which, if they eat as much as a bean, they feel neither hunger nor thirst.

Such is the island of Britain, and the part of it not then subject to the Romans was inhabited by such people; for the Romans held a share of it not much less than half. The length of the whole island is said to extend to seven thousand one hundred and thirty-two stades, and its breadth at most about two thousand three hundred, and in places less. Severus, meaning to conquer the whole island, invaded Caledonia, and had much trouble, neither fighting a battle nor seeing an enemy in battle order, but cutting down forests and levelling the heights. His soldiers, scattering, were killed in ambushes, and many thousands perished. At length, however, he forced the Britons to come to terms.

His son Antoninus caused him anxiety, living licentiously and plainly intending to make away with his brother if he could. He plotted against his father himself too, and was caught twice. But Antoninus suffered nothing at his father's hands. Severus called him, with Papinian and Castor (Castor was his freedman, an excellent and most trustworthy man), set a sword between them and said to his son: "If you want to kill me, make an end of me here, and not with everyone watching. If you shrink from doing it with your own hand, order Papinian the prefect, who is here, to finish me off; for he will not disobey you, since you are emperor."

When the Britons revolted again he made ready to make war on them. In the meantime, while staying in the island, he died of illness, Antoninus, it is said, helping it along. Before he breathed his last, the story goes, he said to his sons: "Agree with each other, make the soldiers rich, and scorn everyone else." He ruled seventeen years, eight months and three days, and lived in all sixty-five years, nine months and twenty-nine days.

His way of life in time of peace was this. He did some business at night towards dawn, then walked, speaking and hearing about the affairs of the empire, and then judged cases until midday, unless there was some great festival, and gave his fellow judges freedom of speech. After this he rode, then bathed, lunched and slept, and on rising managed the rest of his business, and conversed while walking in Latin and Greek; towards evening he bathed again and dined. Only on days when it could not be avoided did he give lavish dinners.

## 12.11

Under him a persecution was raised against the pious, and many showed courage and won the crowns of martyrdom. Then also Leonides, the father of Origen, was arrested, and after other punishments was at last beheaded, leaving his son quite young. The boy, still a child, was eager for martyrdom. His mother first tried with words to turn her son from the attempt; when she could not persuade him, she took away all his clothes and so made him stay at home against his will. Unable to go out, he wrote to his father in prison, urging him on to martyrdom, and among other things wrote these very words: "Take care not to change your mind on our account." While still a child, pursuing the ordinary course of education, he also busied himself with the divine scriptures, as his father bade him. He did not read the holy words superficially, as a child would, but reached for deeper meaning, and by questioning his father constantly gave him trouble. His father outwardly rebuked the child for his curiosity, but in truth was amazed at his understanding; often he stood over his son as he slept, bared his breast and kissed it as the dwelling of the divine Spirit, and counted himself blessed in his child.

When his father had been made perfect by martyrdom, Origen lacked the necessities of life. He was taken in by one of the most distinguished women in Alexandria, who had abundant wealth. She had adopted as her son a man of Antioch named Paul, one of the heretics in Alexandria, round whom a crowd gathered every day, since he was thought able in speaking. Origen, living with him from necessity, never joined him in prayer, as has been recorded, loathing the man as not orthodox. At eighteen he became head of the catechetical school in Alexandria, encouraged those then contending to the contest, accompanied them as they went to their deaths and gave them courage, so that the mobs often raged against him; but by divine help he escaped them.

The man was great not only in word but also lived a life of philosophy, in harmony with his teaching. He is recorded to have had no second tunic, to have gone unshod for many years, used no wine and taken no food beyond what was necessary. Eusebius records that for love of chastity he cut off his own organs of generation; but others say he did not cut them off but applied a certain herb to them, by which they became so numb as to be like the dead. He also went to Rome, when Zephyrinus presided over the church there, and then returned to Alexandria. He expounded the Hebrew scriptures, having learned the Hebrew language, acquired and studied the scriptures the Jews had in Hebrew letters, and gathered together the works of those who had translated them, that is, the Seventy-two, Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion and two other anonymous translators (for their names are unknown); and he laboured on the works called the Hexapla. In the Psalms he also mentions a seventh translation, found at Jericho. And setting side by side, in particular, the version of Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion with that of the Seventy-two, he handed down what is called the Tetrapla.

When Demetrius died, whom the narrative recorded above as heading the church in Alexandria, Heraclas, who had been a pupil of Origen, was thought worthy of the episcopate. Narcissus, a worker of wonders, headed the church in Jerusalem. Falsely accused by some, and being a lover of the solitary life, he slipped away unnoticed and lived in the wilderness unknown. In place of him who had withdrawn Dius was brought in, after whom Germanio was appointed, then Gordius; in whose time Narcissus came back and was called by the faithful to the leadership again. When Narcissus could not perform the ministry because of his great age, Alexander, who had earlier been distinguished for his confession of Christ and had then been made bishop in the country of the Cappadocians, was taken on by the faithful in Jerusalem, after a revelation, to administer the church together with Narcissus. In the church of the Antiochenes, when Serapion went to his rest, Asclepiades received the episcopate; Eusebius says that his writings too survive.

## 12.12

So much for these things. Antoninus seemed to make his brother Geta partner in the rule, but in truth he was sole ruler. He at once made peace with the British enemy, withdrawing from their country and the forts, and removed Papinian the prefect from his office. He killed others, including his tutor Euodus and Castor, his own wife Plautilla and her brother Plautius, and, on top of these, his own brother. He had been plotting against Geta for a long time, but could not kill him, as he was guarded by soldiers and many others. Then he persuaded their mother to send for the two of them alone, for a reconciliation. Geta trusted it and went into the room with Antoninus; and centurions prepared for this by Antoninus pushed their way in behind them and cut Geta down there as he clung to his mother's neck and breasts and bosom, so that she was covered with his blood and was wounded in the hand. In her fear she did not even feel the wound. Nor was she allowed even to mourn her son, who had perished so pitiably and untimely (he was twenty-two years and nine months old), for fear that she too would perish with him.

Antoninus at once went to the camp and said: "Rejoice, fellow soldiers; for now I am able to do you good, since I am one of you, and I wish to live with you and for you, so that I may give you many gifts. If not, I pray at least to die with you." The next day he spoke at length to the senate, and at the end said, "Hear me: so that the whole world may rejoice, let all the exiles return." He killed about twenty thousand of the imperial freedmen and the soldiers who had been with Geta, destroyed a great many distinguished men, and robbed Rome of many good men. He dedicated the sword with which his brother had been slaughtered. Passing from murders to games, he killed in these too. To the soldiers he was most generous, but he made it his business to strip, despoil and wear down everyone else, and not least the senators. Through the whole time of his reign the land subject to him was so plundered that once, when Julia said, "We have no source of money left, just or unjust," he answered, drawing his sword, "Take heart, mother; as long as we have this, nothing will fail us."

He was faithless above all. He invited Abgar, the king of Osroene, to come to him as a friend, put him in chains when he came, and so took Osroene. The king of the Armenians, who was quarrelling with his own sons, he summoned with friendly letters as though to make peace between them, and did to them the same as to Abgar. But he did not master the Armenians; they took up arms, and no one trusted him any more. In emergencies and urgent campaigns, however, he was frugal and plain, marched and ran with the soldiers, without bathing or changing his clothes, sharing in every task and eating the same food as they. Sometimes he even challenged the leading men of the enemy to single combat. Generalship, however, he did not handle well. Everything about him was counterfeit, even his coinage.

He suffered from illnesses, both visible and secret, and often fancied that his father and his brother were pursuing him with swords. For this reason he is said to have called up by spells the spirits of his father and of Commodus and others; only Commodus spoke to him, saying "Come nearer to justice", and at the end, "having in secret places a disease hard to heal." He also had eavesdroppers and spies, and everything, even the smallest things, was reported to him. He would give notice that he would judge cases early, or do some other public business, but put it off past midday or even to evening, not even admitting the assembled senators into the vestibule. He killed and broke the laws and spent money freely, and did not listen to his mother, though she gave him much good advice on these and other matters. He took pleasure in magicians and sorcerers.

He marched against the Parthians, and while at Antioch, living in luxury and holding gladiatorial shows, he lamented as if he were in danger and enduring great hardships, reproached the senate for living at ease, and finally wrote to it: "I know that my doings do not please you; but that is why I have arms and soldiers, so that I need not heed the tale-tellers."

He marched against the Parthians again because Artabanus, though he had betrothed his daughter to him, did not give her to him; for he knew that Antoninus had made the marriage a pretext to seize the Parthian kingdom. He ravaged much of their country, took Arbela, broke open the tombs of the Parthian kings and scattered the bones; but no war with that people was fought. He broke the laws in other ways on his campaigns, and invented a dress of his own, cut out and stitched together in a barbarian fashion like a cloak, and wore it constantly, from which he was called Caracallus; and he ordered the soldiers to dress in the same way.

He was killed, however, by soldiers. Macrinus, the prefect, had been told beforehand by a seer that he would become sole ruler, and fearing that for this reason he would be destroyed by Antoninus, he did not delay, but won over two tribunes of the guard and plotted against him. As Antoninus was going from Edessa to Carrhae and had got off his horse to relieve himself, a soldier sent by the tribunes came up to him as if wanting to say something, and, coming close, struck him with a small sword. So he lived and so he perished, having lived twenty-nine years, and of these he was sole ruler six years, two months and some days. It is said that at Antioch, at the last, he dreamed that his father stood over him with a sword, saying, "You killed your brother, and I shall kill you." The seers also told him to be on his guard that day, and they say other signs of his ruin came to him as well.

## 12.13

When Antoninus had been destroyed (he was called Caracallus, as has been said, and also Tarautas, after a most hideous and bloodthirsty gladiator), Macrinus received the rule from the army after the fourth day. By race he was a Moor from Sicily, of most obscure parents, so that one of his ears was pierced after the custom of the Moors; but he was reasonable and a most faithful guardian of the laws. As prefect he had managed the affairs of his office excellently, but as sole ruler he did not do everything well. For he set unworthy men over offices, which is the greatest part of imperial government, and lived a rather luxurious life, and an arrogant one too.

Julia, the mother of Antoninus, who was staying in Antioch, on learning of her son's death set out to kill herself, not for her son's sake but fearing for herself, that she would become a private person. But when nothing about her was changed, and she kept her attendants and guards, she clung to life. Then, when Macrinus heard that she was abusing him and perceived that she was reaching for the sole rule, she was ordered to leave Antioch and go wherever she wished. Then she destroyed herself.

When Artabanus advanced against the Romans with great forces, Macrinus engaged him near Nisibis, was defeated twice and was compelled to buy peace for much money. So the war with Artabanus was laid to rest, but another, civil, war broke out for the Romans. Eutychianus, one of the imperial freedmen, saw the soldiers' hatred of Macrinus, because he did not give them boundless gifts as Antoninus had, and, persuaded by oracles, attempted a rising against Macrinus. Maesa, the sister of the empress Julia, had two daughters, Soaemias and Mamaea, and two grandsons by them. Eutychianus took one of these, pretended that he was a bastard son of Caracallus, brought him into the camp by night, and persuaded the soldiers, who were looking for a pretext for revolt, to rise. They named him Antoninus and at once proclaimed him emperor, though he was a child. Taking him with them they marched against Macrinus, who was staying in Antioch. Engaging him at a place far from Antioch, they were at first defeated; but then, seeing them rally again, Macrinus turned to flight, and went off to Antioch as if he had won, so as not to be shut out of the city. He sent his son to Artabanus.

When his defeat was reported and many killings were taking place, he fled by night, having shaved his head and beard and put on a dark cloak so as not to be recognised. With a few men he reached Aegae in Cilicia, and came through Cappadocia, Galatia and Bithynia as far as Eriboulos, the port lying opposite Nicomedia, and crossed to Chalcedon. He sent to one of the procurators and asked for money; through this he was recognised and arrested by the partisans of the False Antoninus, and taken as far as Cappadocia. There, learning that his son had been caught, he threw himself from the carriage and broke his shoulder. Not long after he was killed, having lived fifty-four years and ruled the Romans alone one year and two months less three days, overthrown by a little boy. It is said that this verse was spoken to him in an oracle: "Old man, the young fighters press you hard indeed; your strength is loosed, and grievous old age comes upon you."

## 12.14

Avitus, the False Antoninus, or Assyrian, or Sardanapalus, on coming to Rome did one act of a good emperor. Though he had been much insulted, privately and publicly, in word and deed, by those in Rome when Macrinus wrote to them about him, he punished no one. In everything else he was most shameful, lawless and bloodthirsty. Eutychianus commanded the guards and after this was consul twice and three times; but the other distinguished men were killed, either on invented pretexts or with no pretext at all, because they did not like what he was doing; for he was not ashamed to write this to the senate. He played both man and woman, and did and suffered both in the most licentious way. He killed not only others but even some of his very dearest, who urged him to live with restraint.

He brought into Rome a foreign god called Elagabalus, and honoured him above Jupiter; for which reason he himself was also named Elagabalus. He was circumcised, abstained from pork, and wore in public a barbarian dress such as the priests of the Syrians use; for which reason he was also called the Assyrian. Among his other wives he married a virgin consecrated to Vesta, impiously and most shamelessly violating ancestral custom. He said he had married the priestess of Vesta so that from himself, the high priest of Elagabalus, and the priestess, godlike children might be born. Not only did he sing barbarian songs to his foreign god with his mother and grandmother, and offer secret sacrifices, slaughtering boys and using sorcery and countless amulets every time, but he also betrothed a wife to that god of his, as if he needed children, installed the wife in the palace and exacted wedding gifts for her from his subjects.

He himself lived most licentiously. To list each of his deeds would be most unpleasant both for those who tell it and those who hear; let a few be told. He went into taverns at night, wearing false hair on his head, and mixed and worked with the tavern women. He frequented the notorious brothels, drove out the courtesans and played the whore himself. Setting aside a room in the palace, he stood naked at its door like the harlots, shaking the curtain, and with a womanish, soft and broken voice solicited those who passed, and collected money from them. He quarrelled with those who shared his shame, boasting that he had more lovers than they and made more money. Not only was he debauched in this way, and drove chariots and danced, but he also wanted to have a man as if a lawful husband, and wished to make him Caesar; he was called mistress and queen, wore a hair-net, spun wool and painted his eyes. Once he shaved his beard, and afterwards had the hair plucked so as to look like a woman. His husband was a Carian slave called Hierocles. He even wanted to seem to commit adultery, to imitate the most licentious women; he let himself be caught in the act on purpose, was abused by his husband and beaten so that he had black eyes.

A certain Aurelius, handsome in his whole body and with enormous private parts, was reported to him, and at once brought to him in a magnificent procession. When he greeted him, "Hail, lord emperor," Elagabalus bent his neck with a womanish affectation, half closed his eyes and said: "Do not call me lord; for I am a lady." When, bathing with him, he found the report not false, he lay on his breast and dined in his lap like a mistress. Hierocles, afraid of being outdone by Aurelius, unmanned and numbed him with spells or some drug, so that he stayed incapable of intercourse the whole night; for which reason he was driven from the palace and from Rome and from Italy itself. He was driven to such licentiousness that he even asked the doctors to contrive a woman's parts for him by surgery, offering them great fees for it.

For these things Sardanapalus was hated by all, who could not bear his foulness and shameful practices. Then he brought Bassianus, the son of his mother's sister Mamaea, into the senate, adopted him as his son and ordered him to be called Alexander. Not long after, however, suspecting everyone, and learning that most men were well disposed to Alexander, he changed his mind, and at last tried to kill him. But Alexander was carefully guarded by the soldiers, and the guards were in a terrible uproar over this. Elagabalus went into the camp with Alexander and tried to calm the uproar. When he perceived that the soldiers were plotting against him he tried to run away, but was caught and killed, and his mother, who clung to him, perished with him. Their bodies were stripped and dragged through the middle of the city; then that of Sardanapalus was thrown into the Tiber, for which reason, besides his other names, he was called Tiberinus. Hierocles and many others were slaughtered with him. He had ruled three years, nine months and four days from the time he won the sole rule by defeating Macrinus in battle.

In his reign Zephyrinus, bishop of the church of the Romans, departed this life, having governed it for eighteen years; after him Callistus presided over the faithful there for five years, and Urban succeeded him.

## 12.15

When the False Antoninus had been killed, Alexander, the son of Mamaea, his anepsios (for so the ancients called cousins), obtained the sole rule. He at once proclaimed his own mother Mamaea Augusta, and she took over the management of affairs. She gathered wise men about her son, so that his character might be formed by them, and chose the better men of the senate as counsellors, sharing with them everything to be done. When Domitius Ulpianus had been entrusted with command of the guards and the administration of public affairs, many of the things done by Sardanapalus were set right by him. He killed Flavianus and Chrestus so as to succeed them, and was himself not long afterwards slaughtered by the guards, who attacked him by night. While Ulpianus was still alive, a quarrel arose from some slight cause between the people and the guards, and they fought one another for three days. When the soldiers were being beaten and set fire to the houses, the people, fearing for the city, came to terms with them, though unwillingly. Other risings that happened were also put down.

Alexander's mother, being a slave to money, raised it from every quarter. She also took a wife for her son, but did not allow her to be proclaimed Augusta, and after some time even tore her from her son and drove her off to Libya, though he loved her. He could not oppose his mother, who ruled him.

Artaxerxes the Persian, who was of obscure and inglorious birth, won the kingdom of the Parthians for the Persians and became their king. From him the family of Chosroes is said to descend. After the death of Alexander the Macedonian, his Macedonian successors ruled for a very long time over the Persians and Parthians and the other peoples, but going to war with one another, they destroyed one another. When they had been weakened in this way, Arsaces first led the Parthians in revolt from them, mastered the Parthians and left the rule to his descendants; the last of these was Artabanus. The Artaxerxes I have named defeated him in three battles, captured him and killed him. Then he marched against Armenia too, and was defeated when the Armenians and Medes attacked him with the sons of Artabanus. But he recovered, and with a greater force lay in wait against Mesopotamia and Syria, and threatened to recover everything as belonging to the Persians from their ancestors. Then this Artaxerxes overran Cappadocia with the Persians and besieged Nisibis.

This Alexander sent envoys to him asking for peace. The barbarian did not receive the embassy, but dressed four hundred very tall men in costly robes, mounted them on picked horses, adorned them with splendid arms and sent them to Alexander, thinking in this way to overawe him and the Romans. They arrived, came into Alexander's presence and said: "The great king Artaxerxes orders the Romans to withdraw from Syria and from all of Asia that lies opposite Europe, and to yield to the Persians the rule as far as the sea." Alexander arrested them, stripped them of their arms and robes, took away their horses, scattered them among a great many villages and made them farm; for he did not judge it right to kill them. He himself divided his own armies into three parts and attacked the Persians on three sides. There was great destruction of the Persians, but very many of the Romans also perished, not so much at the enemy's hands as on the way back through the mountains of Armenia. For these are bitterly wintry, and the feet of those on the march, and in some cases the hands too, were lost to the cold, blackened and dead. For this reason Alexander was blamed by the Romans. Then, from despondency or from the change of climate, he fell seriously ill.

When he had recovered he invaded the Germans and harassed them with javelin-men and archers. He also sent envoys to them about terms, for money. At this the soldiers were angry and turned to revolt. They took a certain Maximinus, a Thracian by race, who had been a shepherd from boyhood and afterwards became a soldier, and, against his will as he made out, named him emperor. Taking those who had proclaimed him, he set off at once to where Alexander was staying. Learning of this, Alexander called on the soldiers with him to defend him, and they promised to. When Maximinus came near, Alexander assembled the army and told them to engage Maximinus's men. But they abused his mother and taunted her for greed, and insulted him as a coward; and they left him and went away. Seeing himself bereft of help, he went back into his tent, embraced his mother and lamented. Maximinus sent a centurion and killed him and his mother and those who were with them, and became master of the empire.

Mamaea, Alexander's mother, laid claim to virtue and a dignified life. While she was staying in Antioch with her son she heard of Origen and sent for him from Alexandria; and being instructed by him in the doctrine of the faith she became most God-fearing, as Eusebius records and some other writers also say. For this reason not only did the persecution of the Christians then cease, but those who worshipped Christ were held in the highest honour.

At that time, when Urban presided over the episcopate of the Romans, Hippolytus also flourished, a most holy and most wise man, who became bishop of Portus near Rome; he wrote many works, expounding various parts of the divine scripture. At Antioch at that time Asclepiades presided, governing the church of the faithful there; and

## 12.16

Sardianus of Jerusalem.

So Alexander, having led the Romans for ten years, was killed in the way that has been told, and Maximinus succeeded to the rule. He at once raised a persecution against those who bear Christ's name, and ordered the heads of the churches to be put to death as teachers and heralds of the mystery of Christ. He is said to have begun the persecution from anger against Alexander, because that emperor had honoured the worshippers of Christ. For he was furious with that emperor, because, appointed general by him and sent on campaign against the Persians, he had been most shamefully defeated and had felt the emperor's anger. There was a second cause of the persecution too: many in Alexander's household had recognised Christ as God. At that time Ambrose also, a lover of learning, who spurred Origen on to expound the divine scripture, supplied him with ample funds, and gave him seven shorthand writers, who took turns writing at fixed times, and no fewer copyists, and girls trained in fine writing, is said to have been adorned with the crown of martyrdom, together with Protoctetus the presbyter.

As soon as he became sole ruler this Maximinus wrote to the senate, informing it of his proclamation by the armies. He was harsh and cruel not to Christians alone but to all his subjects. He was insolent and greedy for money, and therefore most unjust, a murderer and an outright tyrant, turning to plunder and the killing of men without any reasonable pretext. He ran aground so far in bloodshed as not even to spare his own wife; for he killed her too. As if to cover up his own low birth, he dishonoured the nobles and made friends of men of obscure origin. For these reasons he was hated by all. He marched against the Germans and plundered their country, no barbarian showing himself. Then they appeared about the marshes and the dense forests; and when the Romans pushed in there, great numbers of them were killed. Having won in this way, Maximinus came back, bringing a multitude of captives. Appropriating everything of his subjects' and raising money from every quarter, he did not keep his hands even from the temples.

When everyone was blaming the soldiers who had made him emperor, those serving in Libya, learning of this, turned to revolt, roused also by another cause. The procurators of affairs in Libya were taking away the property of the wealthy there without any reasonable pretext, and destroying the owners as well. Indignant at this, the people there rose up, seized an old man of the senate called Gordian, against his will, put a diadem on him, clothed him in purple and proclaimed him emperor and Augustus. He came to Carthage, and all received him gladly from hatred of Maximinus. He wrote to the senate and sent men to announce his proclamation to those in Rome. Those sent were delayed on the voyage, and meanwhile the people of Rome, unable to bear Maximinus's tyranny, were moved to revolt, pulled down his images and abused the tyrant. Then, coming to their senses, and seeing that if Maximinus survived they would have no hope of safety, they put forward two generals, Maximus and Albinus, both members of the senate. Some have recorded that they were proclaimed Caesars by the senate, which had not yet learned of Gordian's proclamation.

Maximinus, learning of this, set out for Italy, uttering many threats against the senate. Learning that Maximus was advancing against him (for Albinus had stayed in Rome to guard it), he turned aside to Aquileia, with Moors in his company, hurrying to seize it first. Aquileia is said to be what is now Venetia. But he was driven off, those within resisting him rather bravely. Driven off from Aquileia, he engaged Maximus's men and was defeated, and withdrew to his own tent. When the soldiers and his own guards mutinied, he came out of his tent with his son, intending to speak to them. They at once attacked and killed them both. Maximinus was sixty-five years old, and had reigned for six of them. The heads of the slain were cut off, shown to those in Aquileia and sent to Rome. In Rome they set Maximinus's head on a stake in the forum,

## 12.17

so that it might be seen by all. Then Maximus returned to Rome, and Albinus went to meet him, and the people and the senate received him with acclamations and applause. The two ruled together, and ruled well. But the soldiers were stung that not they but the senate and the people had put forward the emperors. Then the emperors themselves fell out with each other, and this was the cause of their destruction. For the soldiers, learning that they were at odds, attacked them, bound them both and led them through the whole city, abused, mocked and maltreated. Then, learning that the Germans meant to rescue them and save them, they killed them, so that this should not happen. Of these, Maximus was seventy-four years old and Albinus sixty; and they reigned, according to some, twenty-two days, according to others not quite three months.

After these, some have written that a certain Pompeianus held the rule of the Romans but very quickly fell from it, enjoying power as in a dream, for not two months had passed; and that besides the sole rule he was deprived of his life too, being killed. By whom and for what cause I have not found, and so I too have passed it over in silence; others ... After him, they have recorded, Publius Balbinus was brought in; he too had only a small taste of the sole rule, for they limit his reign to three months, and he too was killed, Gordian having just arrived from Libya, who, as I have already said, had been proclaimed before. They say that Gordian, on reaching Rome, fell ill, partly because he was very old (he lived seventy-nine years), and partly because he had been greatly worn out by delays at sea on the voyage; that he died of this illness, having spent only twenty-two days in the city; and that he had as successor to the empire his own son, also called Gordian, of the same name as his father.

Some have recorded that these things followed in this way. But others say that after the elder Gordian had been proclaimed emperor in Libya, some men rose against him, that a battle was fought and Gordian's men were defeated, and many of them were killed, and his son too; and that he, grieving deeply at this, hanged himself and so took himself out of life.

Others, who give the rule of the Romans to his son, the young Gordian, after the elder Gordian's death from illness, have written that he marched against the Persians and engaged them; and that in the battle, as he rode his horse, encouraging his men and urging them to courage, the horse slipped and fell on him, and his thigh was broken; and so he was carried back to Rome and died of the fracture, having been sole ruler for six years.

Urban, the high priest of Rome, died in the reign of Maximinus after being high priest for eight years; Pontianus succeeded him. Zebinus presided over the church of the Antiochenes after Philetus. Under the young Gordian, Pontianus, who presided over the faithful in Rome, departed this life after serving in the ministry for six years, and had Anteros as his successor. Anteros governed the church for a very short time and passed to the other life. After him Fabian obtained the high-priesthood by divine election, as Eusebius records. For it is said that when the faithful had gathered to choose the one who was to preside over the episcopate, Fabian was present, having just come in from the country. No one spoke of him as the one to preside over the church; there were others about whom the gathering was concerned, which of them should be preferred. Meanwhile a dove flew down and settled on Fabian's head; and at this all those gathered there spoke with one voice, as if at a signal, and shouted "Worthy!" at him, and without any delay brought the man to the throne of the high-priesthood. At that time also Zebinus, who presided over the church of the faithful in Antioch, left this life and was succeeded by Babylas. Origen, who was then living at Caesarea in Palestine, had gained many pupils from many places, and had among the hearers of his teaching the great Gregory the Wonderworker and his brother Athenodorus. At that time too Africanus the historian was known.

## 12.18

After the young Gordian another Gordian again took the rule, related by birth, as the story goes, to the Gordians who had passed away. He marched against the Persians and made war on them, when Sapor, the son of Artaxerxes, was leading that people. He defeated the enemy and recovered for the Romans Nisibis and Carrhae, which had been snatched away by the Persians under Maximinus. Then, having reached Ctesiphon, he was killed by the plotting of Philip, the prefect of the guard. For this Gordian, when he began to rule, had appointed as prefect his own father-in-law, called Timesicles. So long as he lived, the emperor's power stood firm and his affairs went with the current; but when Timesicles died, Philip was appointed prefect. Wishing to make the soldiers mutiny, he cut their rations, as though the emperor had ordered it. Others say that he held back the grain that was being brought to the camp, so that the soldiers were pressed by want, and were roused to mutiny by this. Mutinying, they rose against the emperor as the cause of their hunger, attacked him and killed him, when he had led the empire six years. And Philip at once leapt upon the rule.

When Gordian's murder was announced to the senate, it was roused to appoint another emperor, and at once proclaimed as Caesar a certain Marcus, a philosopher. But before he could set his foot firmly in the sole rule he died suddenly while staying in the palace. On his death Severus Hostilianus took the rule of the Romans. But he too, having scarcely taken it, paid his debt to nature; for he fell ill, was bled, and died.

## 12.19

So Philip returned and became master of the rule of the Romans. On his way back he took his son Philip as partner in the empire. He made a treaty with Sapor, king of the Persians, and ended the war with the Persians, ceding them Mesopotamia and Armenia. But learning that the Romans were vexed at the cession of these lands, he soon broke the agreement and seized the lands again. Sapor, as has been recorded, was enormous in the bulk of his body, a man such as had never yet been seen.

After returning, Philip was well disposed to the Christians, and, according to some, actually joined the faith of Christ, so that he shared in prayers with Christians in church and gladly confessed the sins he had committed. For the head of the church would not receive him into communion otherwise than if he confessed and enrolled himself among those seeking penance; and he is said to have obeyed. Some say, however, that he was the father of the martyr Eugenia; but they are mistaken in this opinion. That man too is recorded to have been a prefect, but of Egypt, not of the guard; and after coming to faith in Christ he resigned his office, stood bravely for his confession of Christ, and was adorned with martyrdom.

This emperor Philip took up war against the Scythians and returned to Rome. In Moesia a certain Marinus, a commander, was chosen emperor by the soldiers. Philip was alarmed at this and spoke to the senate about the revolt. While the others were silent, Decius told him that he need not worry about Marinus, as he would be killed by the soldiers themselves, being unworthy of the empire. This happened soon after, as he had foretold. Philip, admiring Decius for this, urged him to go to Moesia and punish those responsible for the revolt. He begged to be excused the mission, saying that it would benefit neither himself nor the one who sent him for him to go there. Philip still insisted, and he went, though unwillingly; and as soon as he arrived the soldiers acclaimed him emperor. When he refused, they drew their swords and forced him to accept the rule. From there he wrote to Philip not to be alarmed, for if he reached Rome he would lay aside the insignia of the empire. But Philip did not believe him and marched against him; engaging Decius's men, he fell fighting in the front rank, and his son Philip was killed with him. When they were dead, all went over to Decius. He reigned, according to some, five years, according to others six years and as many months. He came from Bostra, where as emperor he also built a city named after himself, calling it Philippopolis.

## 12.20

Decius, all the armies having come over to him, as has been said, returned to Rome and became master of the empire. Looking to the weight of power and the management of affairs, as some say, he took Valerian to help in the administration of affairs. At once they spurred each other on to war against God, and raised a most violent persecution against those who bear Christ's name. Some say that Decius attacked the Christians from hatred of Philip, since they had been honoured by him; at any rate he raged against the faithful. At that time Fabian, who presided over the church in Rome, met a martyr's end, and Babylas, who administered the church of the faithful in Antioch, and Alexander, the bishop of Jerusalem, who then contended for the faith in the Saviour and Lord not for the first time but earlier too, as has already been said, and at this time was shut up in prison and died there. The great Cyprian also, who was then bishop of Carthage, contended for the faith in Christ. When these high priests had departed this life, Cornelius became bishop in Rome in place of Fabian, Flavian in Antioch in place of Babylas, Dionysius in Alexandria, and Mazabanes in Jerusalem in place of Alexander; and very many others were then thought worthy of martyrdom.

At that time Origen too was brought before a tyrant's tribunal as a worshipper of Christ, but did not meet a martyr's end; God, I think, did not judge him worthy of it because of the wretched man's perversity concerning orthodox doctrine. For he deserted his post, and that after experience of torture. This man, as has been said before, having become great in learning and therefore proud and boastful, did not follow the doctrines of the holy fathers before him, but, trusting in himself, made himself an introducer of new doctrines, and belched out blasphemies against the holy Trinity and the divine incarnation from the evil treasure of his heart, and became the founder of almost every heresy. For he taught that the only-begotten Son of God was created, and estranged him from the glory and essence of the Father; and he ranked the Holy Spirit below the dignity of the Father and of the Son, saying that the Son cannot see the Father, nor the Spirit the Son, just as the angels cannot see the Spirit, nor men the angels. These are Origen's blasphemies about the holy and consubstantial Trinity. About the incarnation of the Son of God he blasphemed, impiously teaching that Christ did not take ensouled flesh from the holy Virgin. For he told the tale that the only-begotten Son of God was united before the foundation of the world to a mind, which he imagined as chosen and never having suffered disturbance, and that with this, in the last times, he became man and took on flesh without a rational and intelligent soul. He taught too that the Lord laid the flesh aside again, and that his kingdom will have an end. He introduced the doctrine that there would be a restoration of the demons, and that punishment is for a time and not eternal, and will serve to purge sins, and that after all have been purged both men and demons will be restored to the unity. What concerns this unity, needing a long discourse to make its nonsense plain, has been passed over, like others of his blasphemies.

So much for Origen, who was also called Adamantius. At that time also Novatus, a presbyter of the church in Rome, became a heresiarch and leader of those who call themselves the Pure. He refused penance to those who had given way in the persecution and offered sacrifice to the idols, and then turned back and confessed, and would not receive them when they fell at his feet and sought healing for their fall with remorse and contrition of heart. A synod was held against him in Rome, with Cornelius presiding. At it, it was decided that those who had lapsed in the time of the persecution should be received when they turned back, and healed with the medicines of penance; and when Novatus would not obey the decisions of the synod, those divine fathers cut him off from the church and condemned him as a hater of his brethren.

Eusebius records a story from a letter of Dionysius, who was bishop of the church of the Alexandrians, as follows. He says that Dionysius wrote these very words: "There was among us a faithful old man named Serapion, who had lived blamelessly, but had fallen in the trial. He often begged, and no one heeded him, for he had sacrificed. Falling ill, he remained for three days in a row speechless and senseless. Recovering a little on the fourth, he called his grandson and said, 'Hurry, call me one of the presbyters'; and again he was speechless. The boy ran to the presbyter. He could not come, for he was ill; but since I had given an order that those departing this life, if they asked, and above all if they had pleaded earlier, should be absolved, so that they might depart in good hope, he gave the boy a little of the eucharist, telling him to soak it and drop it into the old man's mouth. The boy came back carrying it. When he was near, before he went in, Serapion revived again and said, 'Have you come, child? The presbyter could not come, but you, do quickly what you were told and release me.' The boy soaked it, and at the same time poured it into the old man's mouth; and he swallowed a little and at once gave up the ghost. Was he not plainly preserved, and did he not remain until he was released, and, his sin blotted out, could be acknowledged for the many good things he had done?" So runs the letter of Dionysius.

Decius, being so disposed towards the worshippers of Christ, perished most shamefully, having completed not even two whole years in the rule of the Romans. When barbarians were plundering the Bosporus, Decius engaged them and killed many. When they were hard pressed and asked to give up all their plunder if they were allowed to withdraw, Decius would not yield, but set Gallus, one of the senate, over the barbarians' line of passage, ordering him not to let them pass. But Gallus, plotting against Decius, advised the barbarians to draw up for battle there, near a deep marsh. The barbarians drew up in this way and turned their backs, and Decius pursued; and he himself with his son and a multitude of the Romans fell into the marsh, and all perished there, so that not even their bodies were found, buried in the mud of the marsh.

## 12.21

So Gallus took power, who, some of the writers say, was also called Volusianus, as having two names; but others have written that Volusianus was his son and ruled with him. Gallus, then, having taken the rule of the Romans, made a treaty with the barbarians on terms that they should receive a yearly tribute from the Romans and not plunder Roman territory. Having made the treaty in this way he returned to Rome, and proclaimed his son Volusianus Caesar. He too was harsh to the Christians, no less than Decius, raising a persecution against them and killing many.

In his reign the stirring of the Persians began again, and Armenia was seized by them, Tiridates its king fleeing, while his sons went over to the Persians. Scythians invaded Italy too, in a multitude almost beyond counting, and overran Macedonia, Thessaly and Greece. It is said that part of them went through the Bosporus, crossed the Maeotic lake, reached the Euxine sea and ravaged many lands. Many other peoples too had then set out against the Roman dominion. A plague also struck the lands at that time, beginning in Ethiopia and spreading over almost every land east and west; it emptied many of the cities of their inhabitants, and lasted fifteen years.

The Scythians, receiving the sums fixed by the Romans each year under the treaty, came to receive them, and, saying that what was given them was less than had been promised, went away with threats. Aemilianus, a Libyan, commander of the army in Moesia, promised the soldiers to give them all that was given to the Scythians if they would make war on the barbarians. They fell on the Scythians unexpectedly and killed all but a few, and gathered very much spoil from them, overrunning their country. Then Aemilianus, grown proud at this success, courted the soldiers under him, and they proclaimed him emperor of the Romans. He at once gathered his forces and hurried to seize Italy. When this came to the knowledge of Gallus, he too made ready on his side and drew up against Aemilianus. When the armies clashed, Gallus's men were defeated, and in defeat they turned on their own emperor; and having killed him and his son, who had reigned two years and eight months, they went over to Aemilianus, and themselves voted him the empire.

## 12.22

Proclaimed emperor in this way, Aemilianus wrote to the senate, promising that he would rid Thrace of barbarians, march against the Persians, and do everything and fight as their general, leaving the empire to the senate. But he did none of these things in time, for Valerian rose against him. He commanded the forces beyond the Alps, and on learning of Aemilianus's doings, he too seized power, gathered the forces under him and hurried towards Rome. Those serving with Aemilianus knew they were no match for Valerian's army, and judged it unholy for Romans to destroy and be destroyed by one another and for wars to be fought between kinsmen. Besides, they reckoned Aemilianus unworthy of the empire, as inglorious and low, and thought Valerian better suited to the rule, as one who would take affairs in hand in a more kingly way. So they killed Aemilianus, who had not yet led the empire four months and was in the fortieth year of his age; and they went over to Valerian and handed him the rule of the Romans without a battle.

After Fabian had been martyred under Decius, as has been said, Cornelius succeeded to the pastorate of Rome, and having shone in it for three years he completed his life; and Lucius was raised to the throne of the high-priesthood, who died without completing even an eighth year in the episcopate of Rome. Stephen succeeded to the ministry of the episcopate. His is the ordinance that Christians returning from heresies should not be baptised, but purified by prayer with the laying on of hands; and a letter of his on this to Cyprian, the holy martyr, is on record. When Stephen fell asleep after two years, Xystus sat on the high-priestly throne of Rome. At that time too the heresy of Sabellius was stirred up in Ptolemais of the Pentapolis. But the affairs of the high priests of Rome

## 12.23

were thus. Valerian, having seized the rule of the Romans with his son Gallienus, also raised a most violent persecution against the Christians; and many in various lands became martyrs, contending in many ways for the faith in Christ. Since in his reign too there was a rising of peoples, the affairs of the Romans went badly. The Scythians crossed the Ister and again enslaved the Thracian country, and besieged the famous city of Thessalonica, though they did not take it. They brought everyone to such fear that the Athenians rebuilt the wall of their city, which had been pulled down in Sulla's time, and the Peloponnesians walled off the Isthmus from sea to sea. The Persians too, under their king Sapor, overran Syria, ravaged Cappadocia and besieged Edessa.

Valerian shrank from engaging the enemy. But learning that the soldiers in Edessa were going out of the city, engaging the barbarians, killing many and taking a great deal of spoil, he took heart, set out with the army he had and engaged the Persians. They, being many times more numerous, surrounded the Romans; most of them fell, though some escaped, and Valerian with those about him was captured by the enemy and taken to Sapor. Sapor, having mastered the emperor, thought he already mastered everything; and cruel as he was before, he became much worse afterwards.

Some have recorded that Valerian was taken prisoner by the Persians in this way. But there are those who say that Valerian went over to the Persians of his own will, because while he was staying in Edessa famine came upon the soldiers, and so they were roused to mutiny and sought to kill the emperor. Fearing the soldiers' rising, he fled to Sapor, so as not to perish at the hands of his own men, betraying himself to the enemy, and, so far as it lay with him, the armies of the Romans too. The soldiers did not perish, however, but learning of the betrayal escaped, only a few being killed. Whether the emperor was taken by the Persians as a prisoner of war, or handed himself over to them of his own will, he was led about in dishonour by Sapor.

The Persians, attacking the cities with complete impunity, took Antioch on the Orontes, Tarsus, the most famous of the cities of Cilicia, and Caesarea in Cappadocia. They gathered a multitude of captives and gave them no food beyond the very least to live on, nor let them have water to their fill, but once a day their guards drove them to water like cattle. Caesarea, which was populous (about four hundred thousand people are said to have lived in it), they did not take at first, since those within resisted the enemy bravely, led by a certain Demosthenes, a brave and intelligent man; until a doctor taken prisoner, unable to bear the tortures put on him, showed them a place, through which the Persians got in by night and killed everyone. Their general Demosthenes, surrounded by many Persians who had been ordered to take him alive, mounted his horse, drew his sword, thrust himself into the midst of the enemy, and having struck down very many broke out of the city and managed to escape.

With affairs going so for the Persians, they scattered over all the eastern land subject to the Romans and plundered it without fear. But the Romans who had escaped, as has been said, set over themselves as general a certain Callistus. Seeing the Persians scattered and overrunning the lands without precaution, since they thought no one would oppose them, he fell on them suddenly, made a very great slaughter of the barbarians, and captured Sapor's concubines with much wealth. Deeply grieved at this, Sapor hurried back home, taking Valerian with him; and Valerian ended his life in Persia, as a prisoner, reviled and mocked.

Not only Callistus distinguished himself against the Persians then, but also a man of Palmyra called Odaenathus, an ally of the Romans, destroyed many of the Persians, attacking them as they returned through the land of the Euphrates; and Gallienus, rewarding him for this feat, appointed him general of the East. Among the fallen of the Persian army, when they were being stripped, women are said to have been found dressed and armed like men, and others of the same kind taken alive by the Romans. On his way back Sapor came upon a deep ravine which the baggage animals could not cross, and ordered captives to be killed and thrown into the ravine, so that its depth might be filled with the dead bodies and his baggage animals might cross; and so, it is recorded, he crossed the ravine.

So much for Valerian. Xystus led the church of the Romans; Demetrianus that of the Antiochenes, having succeeded Flavian; Hymenaeus that of Jerusalem, Mazabanes having died; and Dionysius presided over that in Alexandria.

## 12.24

After Valerian, Gallienus his son became master of the rule of the Romans. His father, on campaign against the Persians, had left him in the West to oppose those who were threatening Italy and those plundering Thrace. He engaged the Alamanni, who numbered about three hundred thousand, near Mediolanum, with ten thousand men, and won; then he attacked the Heruli, a Scythian and Gothic people, and overcame them. He also fought the Franks.

Aureolus came from the Getic country, later called Dacia, and was born of an obscure family, for he had earlier been a shepherd; but fortune chose to raise him high, and he became a soldier, and being very capable was put in charge of the imperial horses. Distinguishing himself in this, he was thought to be in the ruler's favour. When the soldiers in Moesia mutinied and proclaimed Ingenuus emperor, and Gallienus drew up against him near Sirmium, bringing Moors among the rest, who are said to descend from the Medes, Aureolus, commanding the cavalry and fighting bravely with the horsemen, destroyed many of the partisans of Ingenuus and put the rest to flight, so that Ingenuus himself, despairing, fled and was killed in flight by his own guards.

Then Postumus rose against Gallienus. This emperor had a son of the same name, capable and handsome, whom he held as successor to the empire; he had left him in the city of Agrippina to help the Gauls, who were being plundered by Scythians, and had set over him, because of his son's youth, a man called Albanus. Postumus, left to guard the river Rhine, so as to prevent the barbarians living on the far side from crossing into Roman territory, attacked some who had crossed the river unnoticed and were bringing back much plunder on their return; he killed many, took away all the plunder, and at once distributed it to the soldiers. Learning of this, Albanus sent and demanded that the plunder be brought to him and the young Gallienus. Postumus called the soldiers together and exacted the plunder from them, contriving to drive them to revolt; and this happened. With them he attacked the city of Agrippina, and the people of the city handed over to him the emperor's son and Albanus, and he killed them both.

Learning this, Gallienus went against Postumus, and engaging him was at first defeated, but then prevailed, so that Postumus fled. Aureolus was sent to pursue him. But though he could have caught him, he would not pursue him far, and came back and said he had been unable to catch him. Postumus, having escaped in this way, gathered an army again. Gallienus marched against him again, shut the usurper up in a city of Gaul and besieged him. During the siege the emperor was struck in the back by an arrow, and falling ill from this he broke off the siege.

Another war was also stirred up against Gallienus by Macrinus, who had two sons, Macrianus and Quintus, and attempted usurpation. Since one of his legs was crippled, he did not put on the imperial robe himself, but put it on his sons. The people in Asia received him gladly. He spent a little time against the Persians and then prepared against Gallienus, and set against the Persians Ballista, whom he had appointed commander of horse, and left with him his son Quintus. The emperor sent Aureolus, with other generals, against Macrinus and his son Macrianus. Engaging them, they surrounded them and killed some; for they spared them as kinsmen and hoped they would come over to the emperor. But they would not give in. By a chance occurrence, however, all went over to the emperor. As the usurpers' men marched they held their standards upright; one of the standard-bearers stumbled as he marched and fell, and as he fell his standard was brought down. The rest who carried standards saw the lowered standard and, not knowing how it had come to be lowered, supposed that the man holding it had lowered it on purpose to the emperor, having changed sides. At once they too lowered all theirs to the ground and acclaimed Gallienus, only the Pannonians being left to Macrinus. Then, when they too wished to change sides, Macrinus and his son begged them not to hand them over, but to kill them first and so go over to the emperor; the Pannonians did this and surrendered.

Quintus, the younger son of Macrinus, was in the East with Ballista, and had brought almost all of it under himself. Gallienus sent against them Odaenathus, the leader of the Palmyrenes. When the defeat of the Macrini in Pannonia was reported to Quintus and Ballista, many of the cities under them revolted. They were staying in Emesa. Odaenathus came there, engaged them and won; he himself killed Ballista, and the people of the city killed Quintus. The emperor, rewarding Odaenathus for his valour, appointed him general of the whole East.

This Odaenathus became great and faithful to the Romans, and distinguished himself in many wars against various peoples and against the Persians themselves, but at last was killed by his own nephew. This man was with his uncle at a hunt, and when a beast leapt out he struck first and killed it. Odaenathus was angry and threatened his nephew. He did not stop, but did it a second and a third time. In anger Odaenathus took away his horse, which among the barbarians is counted a great insult. The young man, aggrieved, threatened his uncle, and for this Odaenathus put him in chains. Then the elder of Odaenathus's sons asked his father to free the prisoner; and when freed he came upon Odaenathus at a drinking party with a sword and killed him and his son, through whom he had been freed. He too was killed, some men attacking him.

## 12.25

Then another rising against Gallienus took place, set on foot by Aureolus, who commanded all the cavalry and was very powerful. He seized the city of Mediolanum and prepared to engage the emperor. The emperor came with a force, drew up against the usurper and destroyed many of those with him. At that time Aureolus was wounded and shut up in Mediolanum, besieged there by the emperor.

While Gallienus was making sallies against some of the enemy, the empress, who was with him, was once in danger. When the emperor had sallied out with most of the soldiers, very few were left about the camp. Seeing this, the enemy attacked the emperor's tent, meaning to seize the empress. But one of the soldiers of no account, sitting in front of the tent, had taken off his shoe and was stitching it. When he saw the enemy coming he snatched up a shield and a dagger and charged them furiously. Striking one and then a second, he checked the rest, who lost heart at his onslaught; and so, more soldiers running up, the emperor's wife was saved.

While the emperor was still besieging Mediolanum, Aurelian came to him with cavalry; with him the leading men had planned beforehand to kill Gallienus, but put off the design until Mediolanum should be taken. Learning that their plan had become known, they hastened the plot. They sent men to report that enemies were coming against Gallienus. He at once set out against them, though it was already the hour for lunch, with few men following him. As he went, horsemen met him; when they were not far off, and neither dismounted nor did any of the other things customarily done before emperors, he asked those with him, "What do these men want?" They answered, "To end your rule." At once he gave his horse its head and turned to flight. He would have escaped the plotters by the speed of his horse had he not come upon a channel of water; for the horse took fright at crossing it and stopped, and so his pursuers caught him. One of them hurled his spear at him; struck, he fell from his horse, and after lasting a little while died from loss of blood, having reigned fifteen years, counting those with his father. He was generous in his disposition and wished to please everyone, and no one who asked him anything was refused. Nor did he punish those who had opposed him or joined the usurpers.

Some have recorded that Gallienus was killed in this way; but others say that he was slaughtered by Heraclianus the prefect. When Aureolus, who was general among the Celts, had risen against him and was coming to Italy with his forces, Gallienus set out against him. By night Heraclianus, who had shared the plot with Claudius, a most skilful general, came to him as he slept in his tent and reported that Aureolus was already coming on with a heavy force. Alarmed at the suddenness of the news, he leapt from his bed and, half naked, called for his arms. And Heraclianus dealt him a mortal blow and killed him.

In his time Xystus, who had presided over the church of the Romans for eleven years, died, and Dionysius succeeded him. And when Demetrianus, who shepherded the flock of Christ in Antioch, had completed his life, Paul of Samosata received the church; he taught low things about Christ, that he was by nature an ordinary man and not God. The shepherds of the other churches gathered a synod against him, at which Gregory the Wonderworker and his brother Athenodorus were present; they convicted him of thinking wrongly about Christ, and condemned him. When he would not agree to leave the church, Aurelian, who was then ruling, received a petition about this from the orthodox and issued an ordinance that the church should be assigned to those to whom the bishops in Rome and Italy should give their support. So Paul was driven from the church in disgrace, and Domnus was brought in in his place.

## 12.26

When Gallienus had been killed, Claudius was proclaimed Caesar. Aureolus laid down his arms and submitted to him; when he tried to usurp power again he was killed by the soldiers. Claudius, being a good man and walking in justice, forbade everyone to seek other people's property from the emperor. For it had been the custom that emperors could give away even what belonged to others; and from this the laws still in force in the state took their origin. A woman came to him, whose estate he himself had received before his reign as an imperial gift, and said, "Claudius the commander of horse wronged me." He said: "What Claudius took away as a private citizen, when he cared nothing for the laws, he gives back now that he is emperor."

In Rome the senate, learning of the killing of Gallienus, put his brother and his son to death. While Postumus was still a usurper, and barbarians had crossed the Maeotic lake into Asia and Europe and were plundering them, and the question was put which war should be undertaken first, Claudius said: "The war against the usurper concerns me; that against the barbarians concerns the state; and the state's must be preferred."

The barbarians overran many lands and besieged Thessalonica, which is said to have been called Emathia in old times and renamed Thessalonica after Thessalonice, the daughter of Philip and wife of Cassander. They were driven off from that city, but attacked Athens and took it. They gathered all the books in the city and wanted to burn them. But one of them who was thought wise held his fellow tribesmen back from the attempt, saying that because the Greeks are busy with these they neglect the works of war and so become easy to conquer. Cleodemus, an Athenian who had managed to escape, gathered a crowd and attacked them from the sea with ships, and killed many, so that those left fled from there. Claudius went against them, scattered as they were in many lands, and defeated them, sometimes in sea battles and sometimes in battles fought on land. Storms also harmed them, and hunger pressed and destroyed them.

While staying at Sirmium Claudius fell ill, and calling together the most notable men of the army he spoke to them about an emperor, and said that Aurelian was worthy of the empire. Some say he even proclaimed him emperor at once. Others say that the senate in Rome, learning of Claudius's death, from their love for Claudius thought his brother Quintillus worthy of the empire, but the soldiers proclaimed Aurelian. Quintillus, who was simple and unfit by nature to handle affairs, learning of Aurelian's proclamation, killed himself, cutting a vein of his own arm and dying from the flow of blood there, having dreamed, as it were, of the empire for only seventeen days. Nor do the writers agree with one another about the length of Claudius's reign: some record that he ruled for one year, others for two, of whom Eusebius is one. Constantius Chlorus, the father of Constantine the Great, was a grandson of this emperor Claudius through his daughter.

## 12.27

Aurelian, having taken the rule of the Romans, asked those in authority how one ought to reign. One of them told him: "If you wish to reign well, you must fence yourself about with gold and iron, using iron against those who trouble you, and rewarding with gold those who serve you." This man was the first, it is said, to reap the fruit of his own advice, tasting the iron not long after.

This emperor at first treated the worshippers of Christ mildly, but as the time of his sole rule went on he changed, and he too planned to raise a persecution against the faithful, and edicts were already being drafted. But divine justice checked the onrush of his wickedness against the worshippers of Jesus Christ by cutting short his life.

But not yet of his end: the things done in his reign must be told. Being a most skilful general, he won many wars. He conquered the Palmyrenes, and their queen Zenobia, who had made herself mistress of Egypt and captured Probus, the general there at the time, he marched against himself, defeated and subdued. Some say she was taken to Rome and married to one of the more distinguished men; others say she died on the way, grieving at the change of her fortune, and that Aurelian took one of her daughters as wife and married the others to distinguished Romans. He also recovered for the rule of the Romans the Gauls, which had been held for many years by certain usurpers, and having set governors over them returned to Rome himself and celebrated a triumph in a car drawn by four elephants. He also defeated the Gauls who had then risen.

When he had set out on a campaign against the Scythians he was killed, at Heraclea in Thrace. A certain man called Eros, who reported the answers brought from outside (or, as some record, an eavesdropper who reported to the emperor what some people said about him), plotted against him when Aurelian grew angry with him. Imitating his handwriting, he drew up a list containing the names of some powerful men, ordering that they be led to death; and by showing it to them he spurred the men on to murder the emperor. Fearing for their lives, they attacked Aurelian and killed him, when he had completed six years in the empire less a few months.

## 12.28

Tacitus succeeded him, an old man; for he is recorded to have been seventy-five years old when he was chosen for the sole rule. The army proclaimed him in his absence, for he was then staying in Campania. Having received the decree there, he entered Rome in the dress of a private citizen, and by the judgement of the senate and the people put on the imperial robe. Scythians crossed the Maeotic lake and the river Phasis and attacked Pontus, Cappadocia, Galatia and Cilicia. Tacitus, and Florianus, who was prefect, engaged them and killed many, and the rest sought safety in flight. Tacitus appointed a certain Maximinus, a kinsman of his, governor of Syria; but he used his office badly and was killed by soldiers. Those who had killed him, fearing that the emperor would not let them go unpunished, pursued him too and killed him, when he had not yet completed a seventh month in the empire, or according to some not quite two years.

## 12.29

When he had been slaughtered, two emperors were proclaimed at the same time: Probus in the East by the soldiers, and Florianus in Rome by the senate. Both ruled, Probus in Egypt, Syria, Phoenicia and Palestine, and Florianus from Cilicia as far as Italy and the West. But Florianus, having completed not even three whole months in the rule, lost his life and his power together, killed by soldiers said to have been sent by Probus. When he had died, as has been said, Probus girded himself with the whole power. He is recorded to have been most distinguished, to have set up trophies over many peoples, and to have gathered the soldiers who had killed Aurelian and Tacitus, reproached them at length and put them to death.

When Saturninus the Moor, who was very dear to him, attempted usurpation, he punished the man who had informed him of it, not believing the report; but Saturninus was killed by soldiers. Another man planned a revolt in Britain, whom the emperor had put in office at the request of Victorinus the Moor, who was close to him. Learning of this, Probus blamed Victorinus. He asked to be sent to the man, and went off as if fleeing from the emperor, and was gladly received by the usurper. But he killed him in the night and came back to Probus. Probus was loved by all as gentle, kindly and generous.

When the German people were attacking the cities under the Romans, he drew up against them, and as the war dragged on for a long time he was in difficulties, since famine had come upon his army. At that time a violent rainstorm is said to have broken over his camp, and a great quantity of grain to have come down with the rain, if anyone can believe this; and the army gathered it up and was fed by it, escaped the danger and routed the enemy.

Another rising also took place against him. Carus, who governed part of Europe, learned that the soldiers under him were planning to proclaim him emperor, and told Probus of this, asking to be recalled from there. But Probus would not take his office from him. So the soldiers surrounded Carus and forced him, against his will, to accept the rule of the Romans, and at once set out with him for Italy. Learning of this, Probus sent an army with a commander to oppose him. But when those sent were already near Carus, they bound their own commander and handed him and themselves over to Carus. Probus was killed by his own guards when they learned that the soldiers had gone over to Carus. The time of Probus's sole rule was not quite six full years.

## 12.30

Carus, having become master of the empire, crowned his own sons Carinus and Numerian with the imperial diadem. At once he marched against the Persians with one of his sons, Numerian, and seized Ctesiphon and Seleucia. The army of the Romans was almost in danger; for he had camped in a hollow place, and the Persians, seeing this, let the river flowing by there into that hollow place through a canal. But Carus attacked the Persians with success and routed them, and returned to Rome bringing a multitude of captives and much plunder. Then, when the people of the Sarmatians rose, he engaged them too, won and subdued the people. He was a Gaul by race, brave and skilled in war.

The account of his death is not written alike by those who have recorded it. Some say that he marched against the Huns and was killed there; others say that he had pitched his tent by the river Tigris, where his army too had built its camp, and record that his tent was struck by a thunderbolt and he perished with it. When his life had failed, whether in this way or another, Numerian his son was left as sole emperor in the camp. He at once marched against the Persians. When battle was joined and the Persians had the upper hand and the Romans turned their backs, some have recorded that he was caught in the flight, that the skin of his whole body was flayed off like a wineskin, and that so he perished. Others have written that on his way back from Persia he fell ill with a disease of the eyes and was killed by his own father-in-law, the prefect of the camp, who cast his eyes on the sole rule but did not obtain it. For the army chose Diocletian as emperor, who was then present there and had shown many deeds of courage in the war against the Persians. The other of Carus's sons, Carinus, staying in Rome, was harsh to the Romans, being licentious, cruel and vindictive; he was destroyed by Diocletian when he came to Rome. The time of their rule was not quite three full years.

In these times Mani, thrice accursed, slipped in from the Persians into our world, to its ruin, and vomited out his own poison in it; from him the name of the Manichaeans has not died out to this day. He sometimes named himself the Paraclete and Holy Spirit, he in whom the spirit of wickedness plainly dwelt; at other times he called himself Christ, he who had been anointed by the demons for their service. He gathered twelve disciples, partners and heralds of his nonsense, which he had heaped together from many godless doctrines of heresies already extinct.

When Dionysius, who shepherded the faithful in Rome, had completed nine years in that ministry and passed away, Felix succeeded to the priestly throne of Rome. When he died after five years, Eutychianus was thought worthy of the episcopate of the Romans; he too, having been bishop not yet ten months, departed this life, and Gaius was brought in in his place to the pastoral ministry. When he had presided over the church for about fifteen years, Marcellinus became his successor. These lived in the times of the persecutions. In the church of the Antiochenes Timaeus was bishop after Domnus; Cyril succeeded Timaeus, and after Cyril the throne received Tyrannus, in whose time the siege of the churches reached its height and the tyranny became unbearable. Zabdas presided over the church of Jerusalem after Hymenaeus; when he had fallen asleep, not long after Hermon adorned that throne. In Alexandria, when Maximus, who had performed the priestly office for eighteen years after Dionysius, paid his debt to nature, Theonas became bishop. Peter succeeded him, who also won the crown of martyrdom, being beheaded.

## 12.31

Such, then, were the successions of these high priests. Diocletian obtained the rule. He was a Dalmatian by race, of obscure parents (some say he was even a freedman of Anullinus, a senator), and from a common soldier he became duke of Moesia. Others say he became count of the domestics; and some take the domestics to be the cavalry. Addressing the soldiers, he assured them that he had had no part in the murder of Numerian; and as he said this he turned to Aper, the prefect of the army, and said, "This is his murderer," and at once killed him with the sword he held in his hand. When he came to Rome he took charge of the affairs of the empire and set his hand to their administration. Looking to the great size of the empire, in the fourth year of his rule, or according to others the second, he took Maximian Herculius as partner in it, reckoning that he alone was not enough for the administration of so great a realm.

Both, then, acting in concert, raised a persecution against the Christians more violent and savage than all that had gone before. For they strove passionately, or rather madly, to blot out from the whole earth the saving name of our God Jesus Christ. At that time in every city and land so many showed courage for the confession of Christ that it would hardly be easy even to count them; for they held this a task more serious than all others.

When Busiris and Coptos, Egyptian cities lying near Thebes there, turned to revolt, Diocletian marched against them, took them and razed them. Then Alexandria and Egypt again raised their hand against the Romans, a certain Achilleus having led the Egyptians to this. But when the Romans came against them with Diocletian, they did not hold out long, and many of those responsible for the revolt were killed, and Achilleus himself.

Diocletian and Maximian appointed their sons-in-law as Caesars: Diocletian appointed Galerius Maximinus, marrying him to his own daughter Valeria; and Maximian Herculius appointed Constantius, called Chlorus for his paleness, a grandson through his daughter of Claudius, who had reigned a little before, as I have already said; he too betrothed his own daughter Theodora to him. Both these Caesars had wives, but because of their kinship with the emperors they put them away and married the emperors' daughters.

When a certain Amandus started a revolt in the Gauls, Maximian went there and put the revolt down. Crassus, who had held Britain for three years, was killed by the prefect Asclepiodotus; and when five men of the Gentiani seized Africa, Herculius defeated them.

The Caesar Constantius, fighting the Alamanni in the Gauls, was beaten and won on the same day. At first, when the Alamanni charged his army with a violent rush, all turned to flight, and Constantius himself, fleeing with them, was very nearly captured. For coming back he found the gates of the city shut; his pursuers, already close, were ready to seize him, and he would have been led away a prisoner had they not let ropes down from the top of the wall and hauled him up by them. Saved in this way and inside the city, he at once gathered the army, roused it to courage with words of encouragement and as it were breathed boldness into it; he went out at once, engaged the enemy and won a famous victory, so that about sixty thousand of them fell.

Narses was then king of the Persians. He is recorded as the seventh to reign over the Persians from Artaxerxes, whom the history mentioned earlier as having renewed the kingdom for the Persians. For after this Artaxerxes, or Artaxares, as he had two names, Sapor ruled the Persians, and after him Hormisdas, then Vararanes, and after him Vararaces, and again another Vararanes, and after these Narses. When this Narses was then plundering Syria, Diocletian, who was going through Egypt against the Ethiopians, sent his son-in-law Galerius Maximinus with a force fit for battle to engage him. He engaged the Persians, was defeated and fled. Diocletian sent him out again with a larger army. Engaging them once more, he won so great a victory as to make up for the earlier defeat. He killed most of the Persians, pursued Narses, wounded, into the interior of Persia, carried off his wives, children and sisters as captives, and seized all the money Narses had brought with him on campaign, and many of the Persian nobles. When Narses had recovered from his wound he sent an embassy to Diocletian and Galerius, asking that his children and wives be given back to him and offering to make a treaty of peace. He obtained his request, giving up to the Romans as much as they wished.

Diocletian and Maximian also succeeded in many other wars, some themselves or through the Caesars, others through a general, and widened the bounds of the empire. Elated at this and grown proud, Diocletian would no longer allow the senate to address him as before, but decreed that he should be worshipped, decked his clothes and shoes with gold, stones and pearls, and put more costliness into the imperial insignia. For the earlier emperors had been honoured like the consuls, and had as their only badge of empire a purple cloak.

## 12.32

As the persecution grew more intense, and countless men and women died for the confession of Christ, and the faithful still grew in number, these tyrants in their madness, about the [garbled in the Greek] year of Diocletian's reign, sent decrees everywhere ordering the churches of the Christians to be pulled down and razed, their books to be burned, and their priests, as teachers and heralds of the faith, to be destroyed without mercy; and of the rest, those enrolled in ranks or in the service to be driven in disgrace from their rank and from the service, and those of private station to be enslaved.

When Diocletian had already completed his twentieth year in the rule, both emperors by agreement laid down the empire. In public they said they were putting off the burden of affairs; but to those to whom they revealed the secrets of their hearts they confessed that they were laying down the rule from despair, because they had been unable to overcome the Christians or to put out the preaching of Christ's name, and chose not even to enjoy the empire. They laid down the rule on the same day by agreement, one in Nicomedia and Maximian in Mediolanum, and becoming private citizens, Diocletian lived in Salona, a city of Dalmatia, which was also his native town, and Herculius in Lucania. Before laying down the rule they came back to Rome and celebrated the triumph for the victory over the Persians, in which they led in triumph Narses's wives, children and sisters, and the leaders of other peoples, and all the wealth they had plundered from the Persians.

But here it is well to explain where the name of the triumph comes from. They say it was named from the thria, which are the leaves of the fig tree. For before masks were invented, actors covered their faces with fig leaves and made their jests in iambics; and the soldiers too in the victory processions, putting fig leaves over their faces, jeered at those who held the processions; and from this, it is thought, the victory processions were called triumphs. Others say the triumph was so named because those who made up the procession were drawn from three orders, the senate, the people and the soldiers, and from the three orders walking (bainein) together it was called a "triambus", with th in place of t for a better sound.

After this victory procession they gave up their power and declared the Caesars emperors, dividing the rule between them: to Galerius Maximinus they allotted the eastern part with Illyricum, and to Constantius Chlorus they assigned the West with Africa. When this had been done, the soldiers who were called praetorians proclaimed Maxentius, the son of Maximian Herculius, emperor in Rome.

Of these three, Constantius, ruling in the Britains, the Cottian Alps and the Gauls, treated the worshippers of Christ very mildly, and indeed all those under him, and he despised money. Maximinus, ruling the East, set on foot a most oppressive persecution against the Christians and treated the rest of his subjects most harshly. Being mad for women and a great adulterer, he not only took women of private station against their will and debauched them, but also tore the wives of distinguished men from their husbands by force, abused them and so sent them back to their own husbands. He was so given to divination that he did not do even the smallest thing without it, and those who practised the secret arts were held in honour by him. He decreed the utter destruction of the pious, and ordered their property to be confiscated, charging the innocent with nothing but the knowledge of God and faith in Christ.

## 12.33

Maxentius in Rome was in no way short of a tyrant to his subjects, but managed the affairs of his rule just like Maximinus. He too raged against the Christians under his hand and subjected them to every harm, and was most bitterly disposed to all his subjects. He ordered the slaughter of many distinguished men without any just trial, dared daily to commit adultery with noble women and to violate maidens, abusing them most shamelessly, imposed unjust confiscations of property on the wealthy, and oppressed his subjects with new and heavy exactions.

In Rome he conceived a licentious passion for a woman of the noblest birth, but chaste too, married to one of the distinguished men of Rome, and sent those who served him in such matters to bring the woman. When she learned that the procurers were at the house and the reason for their coming, and perceived that going to the tyrant could not be avoided (for her husband, cowering in fear of death, allowed them to take his wife and lead her off, and she had no help from any other quarter), she asked them to give her a short time to adorn herself and then go with them. The woman worshipped Christ and had been initiated into the divine mystery. She went into her bedchamber, and alone there she killed herself, so as to remain unviolated and not betray her chastity, choosing a voluntary death and leaving her dead body to the procurers and the accursed lover.

So these ruled in this way; and Diocletian and Maximian died as private citizens. About their end the writers disagree. Eusebius, in the eighth book of his Ecclesiastical History, says that Diocletian suffered a derangement of mind, and that, his body wasted to a skeleton by a long illness, he violently spewed out his wretched soul, and that Maximian Herculius took himself out of life by hanging. Others do not record that they died so, but that they repented, planned to take the rule again, were found out and were put to death by a decree of the senate.

There are also those who say that Herculius, longing for the empire again, proposed to Diocletian that they should try to take it back, but Diocletian refused. Herculius then went before the senate of the Romans and said that his son was not sufficient for the administration of public affairs. When the soldiers were roused to anger at his words, as though he were appropriating the rule, Herculius, fearing the danger, said that he did not hold this opinion, but had said it to test the soldiers and see how they were disposed towards his son; and so he calmed the soldiers' uproar. Then he went to the Gauls, to Constantine the Great, who was also his son-in-law through his daughter Fausta, plotted against him too and tried to take his empire; but there too he was found out and repulsed in the attempt, and so hanged himself.

These men, then, ended their lives in one or other of the ways described. Constantius, having completed his eleventh year in the rule from the time he was proclaimed Caesar, and having ruled gently and kindly, ended his life while staying in the Britains, leaving his subjects to mourn him for his goodness. Before this he had made the eldest of his sons successor to the rule, that is, Constantine the Great, whom he had begotten by his first wife. He also had other sons by his second wife, Theodora, the daughter of Herculius: Constantine, Hannibalianus and Constantius. Constantine the Great was preferred to these because his father judged them unfit for the empire. The whole was arranged by God for the sake of the divine preaching, or rather for the sake of all who were subject to the rule of the Romans, so that through him the tyrannies might be overthrown. For it is said that when Constantius was ill and despondent at his failure with his other sons, an angel stood by him, ordering him to leave the power to Constantine.

This Constantine his father had given as a hostage to Galerius while he was a youth, so that as a hostage he might at the same time be trained in the practice of the soldier's art. Galerius, seeing him very capable and envying him, plotted against him. First, when he was fighting the Sarmatians, he ordered him to attack their leader, who was conspicuous for his full armour. Constantine attacked him, seized him alive and brought him to Galerius. Then he ordered him to take on a lion, a terrible and murderous beast. He completed this task too, at great risk, but with divine grace preserving him. Learning from this that he was envied and plotted against by Galerius, he fled by night with some men he trusted and came back to his father.

## 12.34

So he escaped the danger and was thought worthy of his father's empire. Maximinus took Licinius as partner in the rule, who drew his line of descent from the Dacians and was a brother-in-law of Constantine the Great, married to his sister. Having made him partner in the empire, he left him in Illyricum to defend the Thracians, who were being plundered by barbarians, while he himself came to Rome to fight Maxentius. Then, suspecting his own soldiers and fearing that they would go over to the enemy, he withdrew from the battle and retired. Regretting that he had taken on Licinius, he plotted against him at first in secret, and then made open war on him. Engaging him, he was routed, and being defeated he fled, and in the flight killed himself.

Some have recorded that Maximinus ended his life by such an end; but others have handed down that divine justice pursued him for raging against the Christians. A most grievous ulcer broke out about his groin and his private parts and ate away the instruments of his licentiousness; from their rotting worms bubbled up, and the affliction was incurable. Of the doctors, those who refused the attempt outright were cruelly slaughtered, and those who attempted it but could not heal him were killed most pitilessly, as if the thing were not impossible. At last the tyrant suspected that he was paying the penalty for the unjust murders of the worshippers of Christ, and published decrees everywhere ordering the persecution of the Christians to stop, and that they should live and worship as they wished and pray for him. Here too two accounts are given. One says that he was freed from the affliction beyond all hope, and then raised the persecution again and remained unreformed, until he drained the dregs of the cup in the hand of the Lord. The other denies the impious man recovery, saying that he died of the illness described, bringing up worms through his mouth. So in one of the ways recorded he broke off his life by violence.

## 13.1

In this way, as has been said, Constantine the Great succeeded to his father's rule, he who is renowned among emperors and most distinguished among the orthodox. He was born to his father of the blessed Helena, about whom the historians disagree, and what they say of her does not agree. Some say she lived with Constantius under the law of marriage, but was sent away when Maximian Herculius, as was said earlier, betrothed his own daughter Theodora to him and declared him Caesar. Others have recorded that she was not Constantius' lawful wife but a by-work of his amorous desires, and that from that union he begot this Constantine the Great. Having succeeded to his father's rule, he governed Britain and the Alps and, besides these, the Gauls. He was still attached to the religion of the Hellenes and opposed to the Christians, being roused to zeal for the worship of idols by his wife Fausta. Fausta was the daughter of Maximian; for he and his father were married to two sisters.

There were three emperors, Constantine himself, Licinius, and Maxentius, who ruled in Rome and in Italy. This Maxentius lived not as an emperor but plainly as a tyrant, bringing very many terrible and outrageous things upon those under his tyranny, as I have already written. The people in Rome, unable to bear this, sent to Constantine begging him to free them from the tyranny of Maxentius. Upon this he rose up to overthrow him, took the field, and marched on Rome. Maxentius for a long time sat within the walls and did not go out against those besieging him, so that some even made jokes about him. At last, late, he drew up his army against him, having resorted to sorcery and divining by cutting open infants and doing other unlawful things, which put fear into Constantine. While he was anxious about this, a figure of a cross appeared to him at midday, formed of stars in the sky, and writing around the cross in Roman letters, which by their own shapes said: "In this conquer." At once, then, he had a cross made of gold in the shape of the figure that had appeared to him, ordered it to go before his army, and engaged Maxentius' men and overcame them, so that most of those serving under him were killed and the rest took to flight. Maxentius himself fled with them, and when he came onto the bridge over the Tiber called the Bulbian, he slipped with his horse into the river and perished. So he was destroyed. The Romans, freed of his tyranny, threw open the gates of Rome and received Constantine splendidly, and acclaimed and extolled him as the liberator of the city, and voted in common to set up a statue of him in the forum of Rome. He gave orders that his statue be fashioned with the sign of the cross engraved on it; and he proclaimed decrees that those who worshipped Christ as God should not be punished for their religion.

When in this way Italy and Rome itself were added to his rule, he and Licinius, his brother-in-law by his sister, were left as emperors. For Licinius killed the son and the daughter of Maximinus, and from then on each of the two eyed the other with suspicion; for each thought that if the one were removed he would himself be sole monarch, with no one to dispute the rule. Some say that Licinius gained control of Valerius' share in this way, having been taken by him as a partner, as has been said. Others say that when Constantine's sister had been married to him by Constantine, the soldiers, to please Constantine, proclaimed him Caesar, and he was sent by him to take the field against Maximinus; and when he had conquered and routed him, Constantine gave up the rule to him, charging him not to trouble the Christians. He did not keep the commands, however, but raged against the Christians no less than those before him, if not more, for he went beyond every excess of cruelty; and this, besides other causes, became one of the causes of their quarrels with each other. Constantine therefore moved his army against Licinius, engaged him many times, and at last won. Then he made peace with him for his sister's sake, and did not take his rule away, but on terms gave it back to him again. But he, being faithless, did not keep the agreements. So Constantine made war on him again, and on winning took Byzantium and Chrysopolis. Licinius fled to Nicomedia, and Constantine's sister came to him and begged that the rule be kept for her husband. When she could not get her brother to agree to that, she turned her plea to his life, and persuaded her brother. Licinius came to him in the dress of a private man and was ordered to live at Thessalonica as a private citizen. He lived there; but the soldiers objected that Licinius was being kept alive, when he had often shown himself faithless and a breaker of agreements. So by the emperor's letter the decision about him was referred to the senate. Some have recorded that the senate left it to the soldiers to do with Licinius what they thought fit, and that they killed him at Thessalonica, or near Serrae as he was going somewhere. Others say that even while living at Thessalonica he did not keep quiet but was planning a usurpation, and that the emperor Constantine, learning of this, sent men to kill him.

It is said that in the battles against him, or those against Maxentius, Constantine saw an armed horseman carrying the figure of the cross in place of a standard and going before his line. And again at Adrianople two young men were seen by him cutting down the enemy's ranks. And near Byzantium, at night when all were asleep, a light was seen by him flashing round the palisade of his own army. From these things he was led to the thought that his good fortune and his victories came to him from God.

## 13.2

Having become sole ruler in this way, Constantine was also named Flavius, and so was styled Flavius Constantine. He lived in Rome, not abandoning the worship of idols, but being instructed in the things of Christ and already accepting them. His body happened to be sickly and broke out in very many eruptions from bad humours and foul matter, so that the doctors called them a disfigurement and likened them to leprosy, and gave up treating them. He found the priests of Jupiter on the Capitol saying that he would obtain a cure in no other way than by bathing in the still-steaming blood of infant children. At once, then, the infants were gathered from all the land under him, and a day was fixed for their slaughter. And the emperor went off to the Capitol to bathe in the blood of the children. But their mothers, as he went along, let out mournful cries and wailed. Hearing them, he asked what the cause of the lamentation was, and learning that the mothers were mourning their babies, he came to himself as if from drunkenness and said: "The wickedness of the deed is plain, but its outcome is uncertain; and even if that were beyond doubt, it is better for me to suffer, afflicted by my illnesses, than to condemn so many babies to death and drive a sword of grief through their mothers' souls." Having said this he went back, ordering that the infants be returned to their mothers and that money be given them as well, so that they might have a joy equal or even double, both because they had got their children back alive and because they had received money besides.

When he had done this, two men seemed to stand by him at night, saying they were Peter and Paul, the apostles of Christ, and they said: "If you wish to obtain health of body and of soul, send for the bishop Silvester, and he will both heal the corruption of your flesh and make you worthy of a life without end." Upon this the emperor, rising from sleep, sent men to summon the bishop with respect. When the holy Silvester arrived, he said: "Tell me, bishop, are gods called Peter and Paul worshipped among you?" He answered: "One God is known by us; Peter and Paul are his servants and ministers." Next the emperor told him the dream, was initiated by him into our mystery, and was baptised. He came up from the all-holy font wholly healed, and at once proclaimed freedom for the Christians, opened their churches to them, and allowed new ones to be built. He closed, on the other hand, the precincts of the falsely named gods, and decreed that those who wished might come freely to the faith of Christ. For he said he wished to force no one, but to receive those who joined Christ willingly. So in this way he came to the faith of Christ; and the preaching spread, and freedom of speech was given to those who bear Christ's name.

Then Jews came to the emperor's mother, saying that the emperor had been deceived and had mixed a work hateful to God with a work pleasing to God: they called the destruction of the idols pleasing to God, and the faith in Christ hateful to God. For, they said, the only true God was the one they worshipped; and Jesus, the wretches called a man, a criminal and a sorcerer. The mother reported this to her son the emperor; and he decided that the Jews who said these things must debate, in his hearing and that of the leading men of the senate, with Silvester the bishop of Rome and those with him, so that he might learn what it was the Jews were bringing forward. The debate took place. The words of the holy Silvester seemed the stronger. The Jews said that Silvester was winning by skill in argument, and asked for a display of signs. Rather, a sorcerer among them called Zambres asked that an ox be brought in, saying that through it he would show the power of his own God. The ox was brought, Zambres went up to it and whispered something into its ear. And it let out a great and mournful bellow, shook and spun round with trembling, fell down, and was dead. The Jews boasted at this, saying that the one who hears the name of their God cannot contain it. "What then," said Silvester, "does the man who says this into the animal's ear not hear what is said? How is it, then, that he does not die?" And he said: "There is no need now of twisting words and persuasion, bishop, but of deeds." "If then," said Silvester, "I myself bring to life this bull that you have killed, by calling on Christ, shall I not be thought to do something greater, and shall I not show the power of Christ to be great?" He agreed, and at once swore by the emperor's safety that if he saw the bull come back to life he would confess Christ as God. And Silvester, gazing up to heaven and calling on the Lord, stood near the bull, raised his voice, and cried: "If Christ, whom I preach, is the true God, rise, bull, and stand on your feet." At once the bull stirred and leapt up. Those present cried out: "Great is the God of Silvester." The Jews, astounded at the miracle, fell at the saint's feet and begged him to make God gracious to them and to grant them divine baptism.

And the emperor's famous mother, who was not yet initiated, asked to be both initiated and baptised. When she had obtained what she longed for and had come to know the true God, she desired to see the places that the beautiful feet of Christ had walked as he brought the good news of peace. Taking the divine Silvester with her, she went to Jerusalem; and having worshipped at the Lord's tomb, and found the divine cross on which our God was nailed in the body, and built costly churches, she returned to her son the emperor.

By Fausta, the daughter of Maximian, the emperor had three sons, Constantine, Constantius, and Constans, and a daughter Helena, who later married Julian. He also had another son by a concubine, called Crispus, older than his other sons, who often distinguished himself in the war against Licinius. His stepmother Fausta fell madly in love with him, and when she found him unwilling, she told his father that he was in love with her and had often tried to force her. For this Crispus was condemned to death by his father, who believed his wife. When the emperor later learned the truth, he punished his wife too, both for her wantonness and for the killing of his son. For Fausta was put into a bath heated very fiercely, and there violently broke off her life.

When the Sarmatians and the Goths moved against the land subject to the Romans and plundered the Thracian region, the very great Constantine rose up against them; and having reached Thrace he engaged the barbarians and set up a most brilliant trophy over them.

## 13.3

Wishing, in accordance with a divine oracle, to build a city that he might call by his own name, he first proposed to found it at Sardica; then at Sigeum, which is a headland of the Troad, where they say he even laid foundations. And again he began to raise the city at Chalcedon. It is said that eagles swooped down and snatched the builders' measuring-lines, and, flying across the strait between, dropped them at Byzantium. When this had happened many times it was reported to the emperor, and it seemed that what happened did not happen by chance, but that the divine was signifying something by it. The emperor himself then went to Byzantium, surveyed the place, was pleased with it, changed his purpose, brought the craftsmen over there from Chalcedon, built the city, called it Constantinople after his own name, and dedicated it to the Virgin, the Mother of God. When the city was now completed, he celebrated its birthday, that is its dedication, on the eleventh of the month of May, at the beginning of the year five thousand eight hundred and thirty-eight.

At that time, as some have recorded, he summoned the astronomer Valens, the most exact of those then practising this art, and ordered him to draw up a horoscope for the city's birthday, so that he might learn how many years it would last. He prophesied that it would endure for six hundred and ninety-six years; and these had already run out long ago. Either, then, we must suppose that Valens' prediction was false and his art went astray, or we must think that he meant those years in which the customs of the state were kept, and its constitution, and the senate was honoured and its citizens flourished, and the government was lawful, an imperial rule and not plainly a tyranny: when the rulers did not count public property their own and use it for their own pleasures, some of them not even decent ones, and give public goods to whomever they wished, and when they treated their subjects in the manner of shepherds, shearing the surplus of the wool and taking the milk sparingly, and not like robbers slaughtering the sheep themselves and gorging on their flesh, or even sucking out their very marrow.

So the city was rebuilt by that pious emperor on the site of the old Byzantium. Byzantium had been a city before too, not one of the nameless or obscure ones, but one that abounded in numbers of citizens, in wealth, in brave men, and in strength of walls; so much so that in the reign of Severus, who ruled in old Rome, it was besieged by the Romans for three years and they suffered much from the besieged, as I have related in the account of Severus. About the power of the Byzantines and the strength of their walls Dio says this in his account of Severus: "Byzantium had very strong walls. Their breastwork was built of thick squared stones, bound together with bronze plates, and the inside was strengthened with mounds and buildings, so that the whole seemed to be one thick wall. There were many large towers, projecting outwards and with openings one above another all round. The walls on the landward side were raised to a great height, those towards the sea less so; both harbours within the wall were closed with chains, and their moles carried towers projecting far out on either side. The Byzantines had five hundred vessels, most of them single-banked, but some two-banked, and some of them were fitted with rudders at both prow and stern and had double crews of helmsmen and sailors, so that they could attack and retire without turning round, and catch the enemy off guard both as they advanced and as they withdrew." Dio adds to this that there were seven towers standing towards the sea from the Thracian gate. If anyone approached any of the others, it was silent; but if anyone shouted something at the first, or threw a stone at it, it both spoke itself by some device and passed on to the second to do the same, and so it went through all of them alike; nor did they interfere with one another, but all in turn, one from the one before it, took up the voice and the echo.

Such then was the old city of Byzantium; but the renowned Constantine made it many times greater. Churches too were founded in it by him, and many things were done to adorn it. Above all there was the round porphyry column which, it is said, was brought from Rome, and which he set up in the forum, which is paved with stone slabs, from which it was named the Plakoton; and on it he set a bronze statue, a wonder to see for its art and its size. For it was huge, yet it showed the precision of an ancient hand fashioning figures all but alive. The statue is said to have been an image of Apollo, brought from the city of Ilium in Phrygia. That most divine emperor set up the statue under his own name, fitting into its head some of the nails that fastened the Lord's body to the saving cross; and it lasted to our own time standing on the column. It fell in the reign of Alexios Komnenos, when a violent and fierce wind blew; it was smashed, and crushed many of those who happened to be there. He also brought the Palladium from Troy and set it up in the forum of the Plakoton.

With many other things, then, as has been said, Constantine the Great adorned the city. Byzantium had formerly been a bishopric under Heraclea in Thrace, having been made subject to the Perinthians by Severus after its capture, as has been related in the account of Severus; he raised it to patriarchal rank, keeping the precedence for elder Rome because of its seniority and because the rule had been moved from there to here. The bishop of Byzantium at that time was the most holy Metrophanes, son of Dometius, who was brother of the emperor Probus. This Dometius, having turned from unbelief to belief, and having left Rome for this reason, came to Byzantium and was raised to the height of the bishopric. After him Probus, another son of his, was installed on this high-priestly throne, and his brother Metrophanes succeeded him.

## 13.4

Under this emperor too Arius, a priest of the church in Alexandria, became known, having dared to call the Son and Word of God a creature, of another substance and not co-eternal with the Father. He was not himself the begetter of the heresy. For Origen first, besides very many other corrupt opinions, introduced the teaching that the only-begotten Son of God was created and foreign to the Father's substance; and he taught that, as the Son cannot see the Father, so the Son himself is invisible to the Spirit, belching out these abominations from the evil treasury of his heart. But they lay only in writings, kept silent and not yet made public; Arius brought them out into the open, proclaimed them from the housetops, tripped many into impiety, and filled the churches with uproar and schisms.

When that most pious emperor learned of this, he ordered the bishops of the provinces to meet at Nicaea in Bithynia. Three hundred and eighteen holy fathers were assembled, among whom were also some priests and deacons, and monks too; the great Athanasius was there at that time, still enrolled in the order of deacons. The most Christian emperor himself came to Nicaea, and sitting with those holy fathers he charged them to examine what Arius was saying and to decide whether it departed in any way from the right belief. They examined it and scrutinised it closely, and decreed that the Son is of one substance with the Father, of equal honour, and co-eternal, and they expelled Arius and those who thought as he did from the assembly of the orthodox. Among those who championed the cause of Arius was also Eusebius son of Pamphilus, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine, who is said afterwards to have abandoned the opinion of Arius, to have agreed with those who decreed the Son co-eternal and of one substance with the Father, and to have been received into communion by the divine fathers.

So these things are found recorded by some. But he himself leaves them in doubt by what is found written in his Ecclesiastical History. For in many places in that work he is caught Arianising. Straightaway, somewhere near the beginning of the book, citing David saying "He spoke, and they came into being; he commanded, and they were created", he says that the Father and Maker is to be thought of as ruler of all, giving commands with a royal nod, and the divine Word, second to him, as ministering to the Father's orders. And a little later he says that he is, as it were, the Father's power and wisdom, entrusted with the second place in the kingship and rule over all. And again a little after, that he is a certain living and subsisting being before the world, which served the Father and God of all in the making of things that come to be; and Solomon says in the person of the wisdom of God, "The Lord created me the beginning of his ways", and so on. And after much else he says: "And above all these, as the Word of God pre-existing and given being before all ages, having received venerable honour from the Father, he is to be worshipped as God." These and other passages show Eusebius thinking as the Arian doctrines do, unless one were to say that the work was composed by him before his conversion. For he is found in the acts of the first council fighting for the right doctrine.

So the holy council, having decreed that the Son is of one substance and co-eternal, at once set out the divine creed of the faith, speaking in it of the Father and the Son, and ending it at "of whose kingdom there shall be no end". For the teaching about the Holy Spirit was added afterwards, at the second council, when the question about it arose; that council was assembled against Macedonius. The emperor equal to the apostles, delighted at the fathers' agreement, welcomed them warmly. And as some of them bore in their bodies the marks of their confession of the Saviour, he kissed their maimed limbs and parts and called them blessed for their mutilations. When petitions against certain bishops were handed to him, he neither read them nor brought them to inquiry, but burned them to ashes in front of everyone, adding: "Even if I had seen with my own eyes a high priest sinning, I would have covered him with my purple." From there he brought those divine fathers to the imperial city he had founded, and had it granted their blessing, and arranged for the holy Alexander to be ordained in it as its second patriarch, for the renowned Metrophanes had departed this life; and he let each of them go back to his own diocese, having treated them kindly with honours and gifts.

The emperor's mother, the blessed Helena, having reached a great old age (she is said to have lived eighty years), departed to the heavenly mansions, and her son laid her to rest in royal state in the church of the Holy Apostles. He himself, setting out on campaign against the Persians, was carried by triremes to Soteropolis, which is now called Pythia; and having used the hot waters there, where he is also said to have drunk a poison mixed for him by his half-brothers, he came to Nicomedia. There he died after a long illness, aged sixty-five, having reigned thirty-two of those years less two months. His son Constantius, arriving from Antioch (for he was there fighting the Persians), found him still alive, buried him magnificently, and laid him in the church of the Holy Apostles, but in a separate portico which he himself built for his father's tomb.

That renowned emperor is recorded to have spent money unsparingly and to have raised it for himself still more unsparingly, so that his love of display in spending was judged not magnificence but plainly... but let me say nothing bad of that divine man. Hence the profane Julian too, in his work on the Caesars, mocking this most pious emperor, as it were, for his great expenditure, makes up a scene in which Hermes converses with him and asks what the mark of a good emperor would be, and he answers the question by saying that an emperor must possess much and spend much. He is said also not to have been unacquainted with letters, but to have been no less zealous about them than about arms; and from this his tongue became ready in debate, and a certain charm was in it that bewitched the ears of his hearers. It is recorded too that he hated wickedness and was accustomed to say that for the sake of the good order of public affairs the ruler must spare no one at all, not even his own limbs. But he was kindly disposed to those who turned away from wickedness, saying that the diseased and rotten limb must be cut off so that it does not harm the healthy ones; not, however, the one that has already recovered or is recovering.

## 13.5

So that thrice-blessed emperor was removed to the everlasting tents. The whole dominion of the Romans was divided among his three sons: according to some writers, apportioned to them by their father; according to others, divided by themselves among themselves after their father's death. The division among them is recorded to have gone as follows. To Constans fell Italy and Rome itself, Africa and Sicily and the other islands, and also Illyricum and Macedonia and, with Achaea, the Peloponnese. To Constantine were assigned the Cottian Alps with the Gauls (they were named Cottian after Cottius, who had been king of these parts) and the Pyrenean region as far as the Moors who are divided off by the strait of the Ocean. Constantius' lot was all that was subject to the Romans in the eastern part, and besides this Thrace with his father's city.

When the division of the rule had gone this way, Constantius went to the east, campaigning against the Persians. For their ruler Sapor, learning of the death of Constantine the Great, did not stop attacking and plundering those subject to the Romans. Constantine attacked Constans, finding fault with the division of the lands, and demanding either that he give up part of his rule to him or that they divide both kingdoms afresh. When Constans held to the division already made, kept a firm hold of what had been allotted to him, and would not yield even the smallest part to his brother, Constantine took up arms against him and invaded Constans' share. Constans, who was away in Dacia, learning of Constantine's move, sent an army and generals against him, promising that he himself would soon arrive with a larger force. Those sent, when they were near Constantine, set ambushes, and on engaging him pretended to flee. When Constantine's men pursued them, the men in ambush came up behind and fell on their backs. And those who were fleeing turned and caught them in the middle, and much of Constantine's army was destroyed, and he himself too. For his horse was wounded, and plunging and leaping because of the wound, it threw Constantine from his seat, and he was killed after receiving many wounds. He did not get what he aimed at, and lost his life besides, because he began a wrong, and he lost his own share of the rule too. And the dominion of the western region came under the one emperor Constans. Then he too, rolling into unnatural loves and a dissolute life, was plotted against by Magnentius and perished wretchedly: Magnentius, whom he had saved when he was in danger from a mutiny of the soldiers, when the soldiers had already raised their swords against him and were rushing to kill him.

Constantius, staying in the east, fought the Persians, Sapor, as has been said, being king of that nation. He was the son of Narses, though not by a wife of rank. For by the chief of his wives three sons were born to Narses, Adanarses, Hormisdas, and a third. When Narses died, the eldest of these three, Adanarses, became heir to the rule. But being very cruel and harsh, and hated by the Persians for this, he fell from the throne. Let me tell one sign of the cruelty of his disposition. A tent was once brought to his father from Babylon, worked very intricately of native skins. Narses, looking at it just after it had been spread out, asked Adanarses, who was still a child, whether he liked the tent. He answered that if he came to the throne he would make a better one than this out of human hides. So from infancy he showed his cruelty. When he had fallen from the throne in this way, Sapor was brought into the rule in his place. He at once blinded one of his brothers, and kept Hormisdas in chains under guard. His mother and his wife, winning over the guards with money, were allowed to go in to visit him. And going in they gave him a file, so that he could cut through his iron chains with it, and advised him what he must do afterwards, having got ready horses for him and men to flee with him. His wife served the guards a lavish dinner; and they, stuffed with food and having drunk the unmixed wine too freely, were taken by a heavy sleep. While they slept Hormisdas cut through his chains with the file, left the prison, went off, and fled to the Romans, and was received with the greatest honour. Sapor seemed pleased at his flight, as having rid himself of fear from him; he not only did not demand that the fugitive be handed over to him, but even sent his wife to him with honour. Hormisdas was very strong and a most skilful javelin-thrower, so that while poising his javelin at someone he would say in advance where he would hit his enemy. He then campaigned with Constantius against his own people, having been appointed to command a large squadron of cavalry. The emperor Constantius engaged them often and had the worse of it and lost many of his own men. But very many of the Persians fell too, and Sapor himself was wounded.

## 13.6

When the wars against the Persians had turned out in this way for the emperor Constantius, Magnentius learned of it. He was born of a British father, served among the protectores, and was then made count of two Roman regiments. He had long desired to usurp, and he set himself to the aim all the more then because he heard that Constantius was faring badly in the Persian war, and he judged that the moment had come to make his attempt at usurpation. Pretending to celebrate his own birthday in the city of Augustulum, he invited the leading men of the city to share his banquet, some of them privy to his aim and some with no part in the plan. And he prolonged the drinking until evening. Then, suddenly rising from the banquet, he ran into his bedchamber, and shortly came out from there in imperial dress with many guards, which alarmed those who were not party to the act. Speaking to those present, he persuaded some to join him and forced others. Taking them with him he went at once to the palace, made distributions of money, and set guards on the gates of the city, ordered to allow entry to those coming in but to let no one go out, so that the deed would not yet be proclaimed. And at once he sent men to kill Constans before he could learn of the deed.

Constans was busy hunting. For he was mad about hunting, and this while struggling with continual arthritis, which he had contracted from immoderate pleasures by living licentiously; or else, on the pretext of hunting, he would plunge into the woods with the lads and young men about him, who were gathered and attached to him for their beauty, and who adorned themselves too elaborately and had lustful eyes, a kindling of licence, and were, it is said, his favourites. Indeed he spent ever more time in the woods, avoiding the company of decent men. So the men sent by Magnentius, coming upon Constans by the river Rhone after the hunt as he slept, killed him, and they also killed the few who were with him. Others say that his killing did not happen this way, but that he learned of the rising against him; and being left alone, when those about him had deserted him, he took refuge in a church. There he took off the insignia of imperial rule, and was dragged out of it and killed, having completed the seventeenth year of his rule and having passed the thirtieth of his age. It is said that just after he was born his father charged the astrologers to draw up a horoscope on his birth, and that they, among other things they foretold about him, said this too, that he would be killed in the arms of his grandmother. When she had died, Constans even made fun of this; but it came to pass, and the astrologers' prediction did not miss, though it was oblique. For Constans was killed in a small town called Helena, after the name of that empress. So he who had lived so licentiously was so pitiably deprived of life.

Magnentius, as the affairs of his usurpation went with the stream for him, was eager to get the most notable of those holding office out of the way. Forging letters to them as if sent to them by Constans, summoning them, supposedly, to himself, he set ambushes on the road and killed a great many, not sparing even his own fellow-conspirators, but destroying these too. And he was engaged in such things, strengthening the usurpation for himself. But when Constantius learned of his brother's death his mind was divided, as he considered whether he should rather choose to stand against the Persians, who were ravaging the lands subject to the Romans, or to neglect them for the present and march against the usurper, so that he might both avenge his brother's murder and win the west for himself.

## 13.7

While Constantius was considering this and delaying, Sapor, since what had happened to Constans had come to his knowledge too, seized the moment, and with a heavy army attacked the lands and cities subject to the Romans. He plundered much country, and also took forts, and at last besieged Nisibis, which of old belonged to the kingdom of the Armenians, but in the time of Mithridates, who was son-in-law of Tigranes, then ruler of the Armenians, and had received the city from him, was taken by the Romans by siege. Coming against it, Sapor set every device in motion so that the city might fall to him: he brought rams up to the walls and made tunnels underground, but the besieged stood up bravely against everything. He also diverted the river that flowed through the middle of the city, so that the people of the city, pressed by thirst, would betray the city to him. But they had plenty of water from wells and springs. When his designs came to nothing effective, he contrived something else. Going up the river which, as has been said, flowed through the city, and coming to the ravines where the ground through which it flowed narrowed, he blocked the place and held back its stream. When the water had flooded up, he suddenly removed what blocked the water's outlet and let the stream loose against the city; and it, having piled up in great volume, struck the wall with tremendous force and broke down part of it. The barbarian did not enter the city at once, but, as though it were already taken, and since the time was towards evening, he put off taking over the city until the next day, with no one to oppose him. The people in the city were thrown into confusion by the breach in the wall, but when they saw the Persians had put off their entry, they spent the night without sleep and with many hands fortified the place, raising another wall inside. When Sapor saw this at dawn he put the misfortune down to his own negligence. Yet even so he did not give up the siege. But after devising many other things against the city and losing very many of his own men (over twenty thousand of the Persian army are said to have perished while Nisibis was besieged), he withdrew in disgrace. For the Massagetae had by now attacked Persia and were ravaging it.

The emperor Constantius fortified Nisibis and restored its citizens, and he himself, since there was now a truce in the east from the Persians, set off for the west. And it was reported to him that Vetranio had made common cause with Magnentius. For he was in command of the armies in Illyricum, and on learning of Magnentius' rising and the murder of Constans he did not submit to the usurper, but himself, for his part, attempted a usurpation. Writing to Constantius, he said he was standing against the usurper, and urged him to come for his overthrow. Then Vetranio and Magnentius, having made a pact with each other on terms, together sent envoys to Constantius asking him to lay down his arms and keep the first rank of honour. The envoys met the emperor near Heraclea in Thrace and delivered their message to him. He was anxious because of this, and when night came he saw this dream. He seemed to see his father standing by him, holding his son Constans by the hand, and saying to him: "Constantius, see Constans your brother, a descendant of many emperors, how he perished at a usurper's hands. You must therefore avenge him, and not look on while the rule is broken up and the state overturned, but hasten to put down the usurpation, and not leave your brother unavenged." Waking at this, Constantius detained the envoys and put them under guard. He himself at once, without any delay, came to Sardica, and Vetranio, cowering at Constantius' unexpected arrival, went to meet him as his master, abandoning his former plans and breaking his agreements with Magnentius. Constantius received him sincerely and made him his companion at table. For Vetranio, having taken off the marks of imperial rule, in the dress of a private man took hold of the emperor's feet. He embraced Vetranio and called him father, and reaching out his hand to him and supporting him, as he was an old man, he made him share his dinner. Then Prusa, a city of the Bithynians, was set apart for him to live in, and estates were assigned for the supply of his needs. There he lived in comfort for six years and measured out his life. So the affairs of Vetranio came to such an end.

## 13.8

The emperor Constantius set out against Magnentius. He was staying in Milan, having proclaimed his brother Decentius Caesar and sent him to guard the Gauls. Meanwhile Sapor, seizing his chance again, ravaged the regions towards the east, and took booty and many captives and went home. So the emperor, hemmed in on both sides by the cares of war, honoured his own cousin Gallus with the rank of Caesar, betrothed his own sister Constantia to him, and sent him off to the east to check the Persian inroads. So Gallus, proclaimed Caesar in this way, went to the east, taking his wife with him. Constantius returned to the war against Magnentius.

So that the Romans should not be defiled by civil battles and slaughter of one another, he judged that he must invite the usurper to terms. He therefore sent him men of rank and wrote him a letter, granting him pardon for his deed if he would lay down his arms, and ceding the Gauls to him, so that he might rule them and be confined within them. But he, thinking nothing moderate, did not accept what Constantius offered him without trouble, but chose war, and marched against him all the faster because Silvanus, one of his commanders, had left him with a body of soldiers and gone over to the emperor Constantius. When they had now come near each other and encamped facing each other, Constantius roused his own soldiers to courage with speeches, and Magnentius urged his to show themselves loyal and brave, promising them much. Drawn up against each other, they spent most of the day doing nothing, neither side advancing against the line opposite. Magnentius even resorted to sorcery. For a woman, a sorceress, advised him to sacrifice a virgin and mix her blood with wine and give it to his soldiers to taste, while she said certain incantations over it and called on the aid of demons. Just as the day was declining, the armies clashed with each other, and after the battle had many turns, at last victory smiled on Constantius, and until deep in the night Magnentius' men were cut down and perished. When the scale of the war had tipped this way for the usurper, he looked to flight. So that he should not be recognised in flight by his imperial insignia, he took them off and put on the dress of a private man, and putting the insignia on the imperial horse he let it run loose, so that those who saw the horse running without its rider would think he had been killed, and he would not be pursued by anyone. So he got away in this manner.

At dawn Constantius went up on a hill, and seeing the plain lying there, and also the river flowing past, full of corpses, he was moved to open tears, not so much pleased by the victory as stung by the destruction of the fallen. For about thirty thousand of his men are said to have fallen, out of eighty thousand in all, and of Magnentius' thirty-six thousand, twenty-four thousand to have been destroyed. At once, then, he ordered that all the dead among the fallen be given burial, with no distinction between his own and the enemy, and that those still breathing receive care and medical treatment.

Magnentius, having escaped, as has been said, gathered those of his own men who had survived, collected others, and tried to recover himself again. He also sent a senator to Constantius as envoy, whom Constantius confined, thinking he had come under the name of an embassy to spy and pry into his affairs. Magnentius again used bishops for an embassy, asking to be pardoned so that he might campaign with the emperor in the rank of a soldier. But Constantius made no answer at all to this embassy, and let the envoys go away without result. And he went on, moving his army, and many of those under Magnentius came over to him, handing over themselves and their forts. Magnentius, despairing of obtaining pardon, prepared for war, and staying in the Gauls gathered great numbers.

To surround the emperor with cares and to draw him away from himself to other matters, he sent one of his own people to Antioch to kill Gallus. The man sent, so as to avoid suspicion, lodged in the hut of an old woman pitched beside the river Orontes, which was formerly called Ophites, as some record, and was later named Orontes when the son of Cambyses the king of the Persians, called Orontes, fell into it. When the man sent by Magnentius had now prepared the plot against Gallus and had won over many of the soldiers there, he dined one evening at the old woman's hut with some of his accomplices, and talked with them rather fearlessly about what they were planning, scorning the old woman as simple and as not understanding what was said because of her age. But she, it seems, being of quite a sharp nature, seemed not even to hear what was said, but kept it all to herself. And when her guest had fallen asleep drunk, she slipped secretly out of the hut, came to the city, and reported everything to the Caesar. Men sent by him seized the man plotting against him, who under compulsion revealed the whole drama. And so Gallus escaped the plot, punishing him and his accomplices.

## 13.9

When things had happened in this way, Magnentius again made ready for battle, and engaging Constantius' men he was defeated and fled. The soldiers who fled with him, since they saw no hope of safety left to them anywhere, and judging it foolish to run risks for a man past hope, decided to hand him over to the emperor. And standing round the house where he was lying, they guarded him in the guise of a bodyguard, so that he should not escape them unnoticed. When Magnentius learned their intention and knew that he was caught in a trap he could not escape, he did, as the story goes, the deed of a man maddened by desperation: he killed all his kinsmen and friends who were with him, and then dealt his brother Desiderius many blows with a sword, though none of them was fatal. Having done this he killed himself too, so as not to be handed over by those guarding him to the emperor Constantius and be punished at greater length. And Decentius his brother, whom he had appointed Caesar, being in the Gauls and preparing to come to his brother as an ally, when he learned of his destruction despaired and hanged himself. Desiderius, his brother, who had received the blows from Magnentius, escaped death, recovered from his wounds, and came over to Constantius of his own accord. When the usurpation of Magnentius had been dissolved in this way, all that he held came under Constantius too, and he became sole master of the whole of his father's empire.

From then on the west was at peace; but the eastern region was in turmoil. For Gallus, elated by his success, when he was in Antioch, behaved oppressively to those under him, who were ill-treated by him in many ways, and he had his wife too egging him on to this. Constantius therefore, fearing that if those ill-treated by him were moved to revolt he would need a civil war, appointed Domitian, a man of rank and advanced age, praetorian prefect and sent him to Antioch, instructing the man in secret to work on Gallus cleverly somehow and persuade him to come to him. But when he arrived in Antioch he handled the matter very clumsily, openly ordering the Caesar to go to the emperor, and threatening, if he did not obey, to stop the rations of those under him. Provoked to anger by this, and being quick to wrath anyway, he arrested the prefect and set soldiers over him as guards. When Montius the quaestor found fault with the deed and said it amounted to open usurpation, the Caesar was still more angered, and being also inflamed to wrath by his wife as though he were being despised, he put the quaestor himself in chains too and handed both to the soldiers. They tied the two men together, dragged them through the market-place and abused them, and at last threw them into the river and killed them. When Constantius learned of this he sent men to bring Gallus to him. Gallus sent his wife ahead to appease her brother; but while she was still on the road the end of her life overtook her through illness. When Constantius learned of his sister's death, he at once sent and stripped Gallus of his rank and put him beyond the frontier. Then he sent men to kill him too, provoked to this by those about him. But changing his mind again, he sent others to stop the killing. These men were persuaded by those hostile to Gallus, and above all by the eunuch Eusebius, who held the office of praepositus and had great power with Constantius, not to announce the emperor's change of mind to those ordered to kill Gallus until they knew the man had been killed. And so he was killed.

When the barbarians beyond the Rhine were pressing on the Gauls, Silvanus was sent out to check their attacks, a man of the greatest generalship and excellent in war. But the emperor, believing slanders against him (for his ears were inclined to slanders), brooded on terrible things against the man. Learning of this, he turned to revolt and put on himself the dress of a Caesar; but he did not enjoy the revolt for long. For Ursicinus was sent there, and having corrupted some of his soldiers with money, he killed Silvanus through them and ended the revolt.

As Constantius was leaving the west and returning to Byzantium, envoys from the Persians met him near Sirmium, sent by Sapor, who demanded that Mesopotamia and Armenia be given back to the Persians, so that in this way they would stop fighting the Romans; for these lands, he said, had belonged to them from their ancestors of old. If he did not comply, he declared to the emperor that he would hold the inquiry into the case with Ares as judge. Constantius wrote back to him in reply that he wondered whether he had forgotten that the Persians had been slaves to the Macedonians, and that when the Macedonians were subjected to the Romans, those who had been their slaves became subjects of the Romans too. Provoked by this, Sapor looked to war. And again he laid siege to Nisibis. When he accomplished nothing against it, he withdrew and made attempts on others. And when he was repulsed from those too, he came to Amida and took it.

## 13.10

Constantius, unable to govern the whole empire alone, so great was it that it reached from almost the farthest ends of the earth to the farthest ends, summoned Julian, the brother of Gallus, from Athens, proclaimed him Caesar, and married him to his own sister Helena. It is said that his mother, when she was carrying him, had a dream and seemed to give birth to Achilles. Waking and telling the dream to her husband, she gave birth to him with scarcely any experience of labour pains, and delivered him before she knew she was about to give birth. From then on his parents had great hopes for him, and handed him to Eusebius of Nicomedia to be instructed by him in the divine scripture.

Having proclaimed him Caesar, the emperor Constantius sent him to the Gauls with very few soldiers, so that a suspicion arose from this that Constantius had chosen Julian not as a partner in rule, but had put the dress of a Caesar on him as a pretext for a plot, so that he would be destroyed by the enemy, having no force adequate to the war against them. But he went, and with good fortune engaged the enemy, and won beyond hope. And when the enemy had recovered, he closed with them again and set up a trophy, many being killed, many perishing in the river that flowed past, and no fewer taken alive. At that time, they say, eleven thousand Roman captives were set free from the bonds of captivity when the enemy was defeated. Then he made war on the Alamanni too, and succeeded against them as well, and when they begged him he made a treaty with the nation once it had freed from slavery the Roman captives among them.

Elated by this and grown arrogant, the Caesar Julian (or, as some have written, because he also feared that Constantius, who envied him his successes, would put him out of life as he had his brother Gallus) turned to revolt. Working on some of the commanders under him, he stirred up the soldiery through them, and they, standing together, proclaimed him Augustus. Then standing over him with drawn swords, as though he were refusing the proclamation to the throne, they threatened to kill him if he did not consent. In this way, perhaps even unwillingly, yielding to the impulse of the mass of soldiers, he accepted the rule. When a diadem was sought so that he might be crowned with it at once, he swore he had none; and when some asked for a woman's ornament so that a diadem could be improvised from it, Julian refused it as an ill omen. But since one of the commanders wore a golden torque set with stones in gold, they took it and fitted it to his head.

He sent Pentadius, the master of the imperial offices, with others to Constantius, writing to him and pleading that he had not been led willingly to be proclaimed emperor, but had been forced by the soldiers, who did not want to serve under a Caesar but under an emperor, so that they could demand from him rewards worthy of their labours. He asked him to accept the partnership in rule, which would be of benefit to the state, and promised to send him every year the racehorses from Spain, as was the custom, and picked men from the Gauls. In writing this he did not style himself emperor in the heading but Caesar, so that Constantius would not be offended by the heading and send the letter back. When it was brought to the emperor Constantius, who was staying at Caesarea in Cappadocia, he made no answer out of anger. But he proclaimed the campaign against the Persians to the soldiers, and sent Leonas the quaestor to Julian, writing to him and finding fault with him for not waiting for his decision, and treating it as an insult rather to Julian himself than to him that he had accepted the title of Augustus not by the judgement of the one who had the authority but by a disorderly uproar of soldiers; and advising him to give up what had been done improperly and return to his former rank, which he had received from him. He also charged the quaestor to remove from office those holding the magistracies there, the praetorian prefect himself among them, and to install in them others, whom he named for each.

So the quaestor went to Julian and delivered Constantius' words to him. They were: "You ought to remember how much you owe me, not only that I proclaimed you Caesar, but that when you were left an orphan at a child's age I brought you up, taking you in myself." He answered the quaestor: "And who, my good man, made me an orphan at such an age? Was it not the murderer of my parents? Does he not know, then, that by reminding me of these things he is scratching open the wound and making it worse?" And when Julian had read the letter to him too, with its advice to put off the imperial dress and take up that of Caesar again, he said to the quaestor: "I will do this, but with the agreement of the armies." The quaestor, afraid that if Julian disclosed this to the soldiers in his presence he would be torn apart by them, begged him not to share any of this with the mass of soldiers. Despairing, however, of being able to accomplish any of what he had been ordered, he went back with letters from the usurper which shamelessly reproached the emperor, rebuked him for having done very many wrongs against his family, and threatened that he would become the avenger of those who had suffered unjustly. And he went off to Constantius.

## 13.11

The usurper, knowing that many of those with him favoured Constantius, drove them all out from there, and himself prepared for civil war. At this time his wife died too, as some say, giving birth in his house, as others say, having already been sent away. Gathering the soldiers, he urged them on to the civil war, and advised that they must march against Constantius and not wait for him to come against them. Having already forsworn the faith in Christ, he was wary of the soldiers on this account, knowing that almost all of them were Christians. So, cloaking his own wickedness, he ordered each to worship as he wished. And he himself, when the day of the Saviour's nativity came, went into the church, and having worshipped, so as to seem of one belief with the soldiers, went away. He also put in office those he wished; and so he set out for the civil war. He said he was not marching against Constantius, but wished the eastern and western armies to come together in one place, so that, being united, they might choose who was to rule them. He boasted too that he knew in advance the day on which Constantius would die, having been initiated into it in a dream by verses saying this:

"When Zeus comes to the broad boundary of glorious Aquarius, and Kronos goes upon the twenty-fifth degree of the Virgin, the emperor Constantius of the Asian land will have a hateful and painful end of his dear life."

Constantius died having left the Persian war, since their king had gone home, and while marching against the usurper. For, beset by many cares, and seized in consequence by a continual fever and bringing up black bile, he died at Mopsucrene (it lies at the foot of the Taurus), blaming himself, it is said, for three things: the murder of his kinsmen (for he killed not only Gallus, as has already been said, but also his own father's brothers), the proclamation of Julian, and his innovation in the faith.

This emperor was kind to his subjects, kept to justice in his judgements, was temperate in his way of life, and in the distribution of governorships and ranks aimed at what was fitting, enrolling no one in the senate who had not had an education and was not trained in speaking and knew how to write in verse and prose. But in the faith he was not sound; for he did not follow his father's piety, but attached himself to the Arianisers, through the influence of Eusebius, the chief of the imperial eunuchs. Hence he tried to force the divine Alexander, who had been ordained patriarch of New Rome after the holy Metrophanes, to receive Arius into communion; but he would not consent. So the emperor ordered a synod to be convened. A day was fixed for the synod; and Alexander, on the evening before, went into the sanctuary and threw himself face down and begged God not to allow the wolf, meaning Arius, to enter his flock, "or release me from life first", he said. He pleaded this passionately, with tears. At dawn, then, just as the synod was gathering, Arius was going along full of pride. And as he went he was struck in the belly with an urge to relieve his bowels and bladder. Turning aside from the road he sat down to void the excrement; and the wretch's entrails poured out with the dung, and he violently broke off his life.

The patriarch Alexander, having held the high priesthood for twenty-three years, departed this life, and Paul the Confessor was installed in his place on the throne of Constantinople by the orthodox. But Constantius, returning from Antioch, removed him from the throne and put in his place Eusebius of Nicomedia, who was a heretic of Arius' party. Saint Paul went to Julius, the pope of Rome, and was installed by him on the throne of Constantinople again, but was driven out again by Constantius. And sent beyond the frontier, he was killed by the Arians. When Eusebius died, Macedonius, the fighter against the Spirit, was raised by the Arians to the throne of New Rome. He, in his arrogance, moved the most holy body of Constantine, who is among the saints, from the church of the Holy Apostles to the divine precinct of the holy martyr Acacius. At this Constantius was enraged, and returning to his father's city he dragged Macedonius from the throne after he had held it one year and banished him, and installed on it Eudoxius, who championed the views of Arius and held the high priesthood for ten years. And he moved his father's body back to the church of the Holy Apostles. This Constantius also brought to his father's city the most holy bodies of the holy apostles Andrew and Luke, through the dux of Alexandria, and later that of the triumphant martyr Artemius; and he laid them up in the church of the Holy Apostles within the sanctuary.

His wife was Eusebia, who became famous for her beauty. But she was unfortunate in her husband, who was soft and rather sluggish in love, from illness and by nature. So, wasting away little by little, she died before Constantius, remaining childless all her life; and, as some say, she died having fallen into a disease of the womb. Constantius is also said to have been very skilful at riding and throwing the javelin, and to have had to do with letters, so that he could compose verse.

## 13.12

When the death of Constantius was announced to Julian, the armies acclaimed him Augustus, but he changed out of his imperial dress, put on mourning, and was seen with a gloomy face. And he held public mourning for the dead emperor according to custom. Then he set off for Byzantium; and the senate and the people came out to meet him, and with acclamations he went on to the palace and took up the administration of public affairs. The army that had been with Constantius and the officials carried his body on a carriage to Constantinople; and Julian went out to meet it and escorted it, having taken the diadem off his head. The body of the dead emperor was laid in the church of the Holy Apostles.

Of the people about the palace, Julian killed many, and banished others and deprived them of their property. Along with the other business of ruling he also took up judging. Once, then, when he was judging a man accused of having stolen public money who denied the theft, the accuser said: "Who, emperor, will ever pay the penalty on a charge, if the accused are to profit from denying it?" He answered: "And who will be innocent, if accusers are to be believed without proofs?" He also gave audience to the envoys sent to Constantius from various nations. He visited and inspected the armies too, and dismissed most of the imperial household. When he asked for a barber and one of Constantius' barbers came to him richly dressed, he said he had asked for a barber, not a senator, and dismissed him. And seeing one of the imperial cooks in clothing more splendid than his service called for, he sent for his own cook, dressed as a cook; and he asked those present which of them they judged to be a cook. When they named the plainly dressed one, he dismissed the other. He did these things hunting for fame from seeming unpretentious and truly a philosopher. Having distributed money to the soldiers, he prepared for the war against the Persians.

Once he had become emperor and had secured the rule for himself, he at once broke out into open Hellenism. He had forsworn the Christian faith earlier too, as has been said, but had not dared to let the birth-pangs of his impiety burst into the open. For it is said that, nursing a passion for the throne and hiding it in his soul as under ashes, he went to seers and sorcerers, asking whether he would obtain power, and was corrupted by them and turned over to Hellenism. Having obtained power by the inscrutable judgements of God, he made many martyrs. For he raged so madly against the Christians that he even prevented them from taking part in Greek learning, saying that those who called these things myths and slandered them should not enjoy the benefit from them and arm themselves with them against them. Hence, when the children of those who bear Christ's name were barred from studying the poets, Apollinarius is said to have set about his paraphrase of the Psalter, and Gregory, great in theology, about composing his verses, so that the young, learning these instead of Greek learning, might both make their speech Greek and be taught the metres. He also allowed the Jews to rebuild the temple in Jerusalem. And when they had begun the building with great zeal and at great expense, and were attempting to dig the earth to lay the foundations, fire is said to have burst up all at once from the trenches and burned up the diggers, so that they were forced to give up the building. He killed Eusebius the eunuch as having brought about the murder of his brother Gallus, and drove all the other eunuchs out of the palace.

Once, when the Apostate was passing near Chalcedon, Maris, its bishop, called him a wretch and a denier of Christ. He, pretending forbearance, said: "Go away, poor wretch, and weep for the loss of your eyes"; for Maris suffered from blindness through a cataract. He retorted: "I thank my Saviour Christ, because he took care beforehand that I should not see your shameless and most impious face."

Moving his army against the Persians, he came to Tarsus, a famous city of Cilicia. When he was there Artemius, the priest of Asclepius, came to him. For there was at Aegae, which is also a city of Cilicia, a famous temple of Asclepius. He asked him that the columns which the high priest of the Christian people had happened to take from this temple, and on which he had built a church for him, be restored again to the temple of Asclepius. And the Apostate at once ordered this to be done at the bishop's expense. The Hellenes, with great difficulty, with much labour and very great expense, took down one of the columns and brought it with machines as far as the threshold of the church's gate, and for a long time could not move it any further; and they left it and went away. When Julian died, the bishop set it up again very easily and brought it back to its own place.

When Julian was in Antioch, and kept going out to the place called Daphne, in which a statue of Apollo stood, a marvellous work of art, and making sacrifices to it, the Antiochenes mocked him, saying that a sacrificer and not an emperor had come to visit them. And because he let his beard grow long, the same people called him a goat and said he was fit for plaiting ropes. He, mocking them back for their stupidity and softness and luxury, said that he did not offer the Antiochenes his beard for plaiting ropes, so that their hands would not be chafed by its roughness. He also wrote a speech against them, which is entitled the Antiochicus or Misopogon. He sacrificed whole hecatombs to Apollo of Daphne, seeking an oracle from him. When that idol was mute, the temple-wardens, asked the cause of the silence, said the statue was silent because of the dead lying there. For the remains of other martyrs lay there and of the holy martyr Babylas. So Julian ordered them all to be moved from there. When they had been moved, a thunderbolt struck Daphne at night and burned the temple and the statue to ashes. That wretch, thinking the fire had come about through a plot of the Christians, and maddened, closed the churches of the faithful. By him also the great Artemius was punished as a Christian, though the murder of Gallus was brought as a charge against him; and the priests Eugenius and Macarius, punished by him, were found worthy of the martyrs' crowns; and so were those sent to him from Persia on an embassy, Manuel, Sabel, and Ismael, and many others.

## 13.13

Campaigning against the Persians, at first he was successful: he took some cities, killed many, got possession of much booty and many captives, and besieged Ctesiphon. Then, all at once, when his affairs had turned for the worse, he himself and the greater part of the army perished. For the Persians, in despair, planned to thrust themselves into evident destruction so as to do the Romans some terrible harm. Two of them, then, slipped over to the emperor in the guise of deserters, and promised him victory over the Persians if he would follow them. They advised him to leave the river and burn the triremes he had brought with him and the other cargo ships, so that the enemy could not use them, and, with them leading, to take the armies through by other roads, and so without danger and in a short time to seize the interior of the country and easily become master of it. That wretch, persuaded by them out of his wits, though many told him, Hormisdas himself among them, that the thing was a trick, set fire to the ships and burned them all except twelve. There were seven hundred triremes and four hundred cargo ships. When they had already been burned to ashes, since many of the commanders insisted that what those deserters said was an ambush and a trick, he at last consented to have the false deserters examined; and when examined under torture they revealed the secret. Some say that Julian was deceived in this way. Others say that, having given up the siege of Ctesiphon because of its strength and because the army's necessities had run short, he thought of returning; that as they went away the Persians appeared behind them and harassed the rearguard; and that the Gauls who were guarding the rear stood up to the enemy rather bravely and killed many of them, not only common men but also some of the notable ones among them. The Romans were hard pressed by lack of provisions. Julian, at a loss what to do and by which way he must return, chose to make the march through the hill country. The Persians, perceiving this, gathered together and attacked the Romans; and on the left wing the Romans prevailed, but on the right they were losing. Learning this, Julian hurried to help those who were being beaten. He happened to have taken off his breastplate because of its weight and the heat of the sun, for it was summer. Getting into the midst of the enemy, he was struck by a spear in the side. It is said that a strong wind was then blowing and a thick murk spread over the air there; for the masses of the armies raised much dust, so that they did not even know where they were or what they were doing; and that it is uncertain from where the spear-point that struck him was thrown at him, whether by an enemy, or by one of his own men, or by some more divine power; for this too is told. So they say that he caught in the hollow of his hand some of the blood flowing from the wound and, scattering it in the air, said: "Have your fill, Nazarene." So he who had lived so impiously violently belched out his soul, having reigned two years. The army carried his body to Tarsus in Cilicia and buried it in a suburb of the city; and on his tomb this epitaph was written:

"By silver Cydnus, from the streams of the Euphrates, from the Persian land, his work unfinished, having moved his army, Julian got this tomb, both a good king and a mighty spearman."

Later he was carried back to the queen of cities.

He was excited about glory and wished to be praised for chance things, but he was not annoyed at being corrected by his friends for his mistakes. He had a share of every kind of learning, especially the more recondite, and was temperate in his way of life, so that he even avoided those natural things, belching and what is discharged through the mouth. He used to say that the philosopher ought, if it were possible, not even to breathe. They say that when he was in Antioch he saw in a dream a young man with fair hair, who said to him: "You must die in Phrygia." So when he was struck, he asked those present what the place was called; and when he heard that it was called Phrygia, he cried out: "O Sun, you have destroyed Julian." It is said that on the very day on which he died his death was known in Antioch. For one of the staff of the court there, he too a Hellene and of the same religion as the Apostate, while spending the night on guard at the records office, saw a configuration of stars in the sky forming letters that said: "Today in Persia Julian is killed." So the day was noted, and afterwards it was learned that he had been killed on that day. So he died, as has been said, having lived thirty-one years. And the man who learned of his death in advance through the stars took the vision as an occasion for belief.

## 13.14

When Julian had died, Jovian was chosen for sole rule by common vote. He was then a tribune, a pious man, son of Varronianus, who had been a count. He was refusing the throne; and when asked the reason, he cried out that he was a Christian and would not endure to rule an army that was Hellenist. And at once, with one voice, as if at a signal, they all cried back that they were Christians. So he accepted the rule and made a treaty with the Persians, having made by necessity terms unfitting for the Romans. For he withdrew from Nisibis and Singara, famous cities, in their favour, moving out the inhabitants of the cities; and he was openly reviled by them as they lamented. He also gave up to them many lands and rights that had belonged to the Romans from of old. Hostages were given on each side, and so the treaty was confirmed. Marching away from there, the Romans fell into a shortage of necessities, so that they did not even have enough water. When at last he reached Antioch in Coele Syria, Jovian brought back to the churches the Christian priests who had been driven from them under Constantius and under the Apostate, and before the others the great Athanasius to Alexandria. From Antioch he went to Tarsus, adorned Julian's tomb, and went on his way back; and having come to Ancyra in Galatia, and setting out from there and going a stage further, he lodged at Dadastana and died suddenly. As some have written, he ate freshly sprung poisonous mushrooms, for he was simple in his diet. As others say, it being winter, he slept in a room newly plastered with lime, and because of the cold many coals were lit inside, and much vapour rose from the lime as it was heated, and he was suffocated by it in his sleep, not even sensing the suffocation, being heavy-headed from wine; for he is said to have drunk much unmixed wine. And not even his wife managed to see him, though she had come out to meet him with an imperial procession, with his son Varronianus.

When Jovian had died the soldiers, downcast, reached Nicaea, and when they were there they deliberated about an emperor. Some named one man and some another, but most agreed on Sallustius, who was praetorian prefect. He refused, putting forward his old age as his excuse. When they asked for his son, he would not consent, judging him unfit for such a rule because of his youth and simplicity of mind. So, with the others, Sallustius himself also voted for Valentinian as emperor, though he was absent.

Jovian was pious in doctrine and well-meaning. But he was a slave to wine and sex, was tall in stature, and was not unacquainted with letters. Once, walking behind Julian as a tribune, when Julian was going down a slope, he trod on the hem of his purple. He, whether he guessed from this that Jovian would be his successor or knew it from some divination, said: "If only it were even a man." He ruled not even eight full months. His body was carried back to Byzantium and buried in the church of the Holy Apostles, where his wife Charito was afterwards buried with him. Jovian died in the thirty-third year of his age.

## 13.15

In this way, then, as has been said, Valentinian was voted emperor. He was brought, proclaimed, and put on the imperial insignia. Sallustius, because he had shown zeal for his proclamation, asked as a reward to be released from the cares of the prefecture. And the emperor said: "Did you load me with the burden of so many affairs for this, that you yourself should not even touch it?" Valentinian came from Pannonia. He was pious towards God; for this reason Julian condemned him to exile; and then, recalling him from exile, made him tribune of a numerus. He was very vigorous in strength and very just in mind. So he punished many of those then holding office as wrongdoers, saying that the ruler is required to care for justice before all else. He took his brother Valens as partner in rule, and entrusting the eastern part to him, he himself stayed in the west, and having fought many wars against barbarians he set up trophies over them. Before his reign he had a son named Gratian, born to him by his wife Severa; and he proclaimed him emperor. He also married a second wife while the first was still alive. The second was Justina, by whom he begot the younger Valentinian and daughters Justa, Grata, and Galla.

Under him, when Eudoxius, the heterodox high priest of New Rome, died, Demophilus was put in his place, being of the same belief as his predecessor; he held the church for twelve years. This emperor also appointed the great Ambrose bishop of the city of Milan. Learning about his brother Valens that he was championing the Arian heresy and forcing everyone to assent to it, he rebuked him by letter and urged him to depart from the heresy. But he did not depart from his own will, and raged all the more against the orthodox.

A certain Rhodanus, a praepositus, had great power with Valentinian. A woman called Berenice came to the emperor Valentinian against him, charging the praepositus with wronging her. The emperor investigated, and learning that the woman was not lying, he summoned the praepositus and ordered him to remedy the wrong to the woman. He, trusting in his freedom of speech with the emperor, took no account of the wronged woman. So the woman came to the emperor again; and he, learning that the praepositus had taken no notice of the woman, at once stripped him of his rank, and ordered him to be led round the theatre in bonds while a horse race was being held, with heralds going before him crying out the wrong done to the woman and the disobedience to the imperial command; and after being led round and proclaimed, to be burned there on the spot. So he met such an end; and all his property was given over to the woman by imperial letter. This emperor died while staying in the Gauls, having enjoyed a long life; for he lived eighty-four years, of which he reigned eleven. And he left his son Gratian as successor to the rule of the west.

## 13.16

Valens, championing the Arianisers as one of their opinion, persecuted the orthodox and brought many terrible things upon them, persuaded by his own wife Domnica. Once, when he was staying in Nicomedia, eighty men of the priesthood came to him on an embassy from the body of the orthodox, and he ordered them all to be burned with the ship that carried them. And they were all burned up in the middle of the sea with the ship, which lasted as far as Dacibyza. Gregory, great in theology, mentions them too, saying: "burnings of priests at sea". He not only punished the orthodox, but also handed all the churches over to the Arians, driving the orthodox bishops out of them.

It is said, then, that when those of the right belief had been driven out of the catholic church at Nicaea too, they went to the great Basil, and he interceded about this with Valens, but Valens would not be persuaded, and the great Basil said: "We must leave the judgement of this, emperor, to God. Let the church be securely locked, and let those who hold the views of Arius stand outside and pray to God. And if the church opens for them of its own accord, let them have it; but if it does not open, we too must be allowed to pray to God. And if the gates are thrown open of their own accord, it must be thought that the divine precinct has been allotted to us by God; but if the sanctuary does not open for us either, let it in that case be given up to the Arianisers." This pleased the emperor, and he allowed it to be done so. So the church at Nicaea was locked on every side. The Arianisers prayed and babbled for a long time, but the church did not open. At last, late, when the heretics had withdrawn, the orthodox, with the great Basil leading them, began their prayer; and at once the bolts and bars burst, the gates parted from each other, and gave entry to the faithful. This emperor allowed the Hellenes to make sacrifices and favoured the Jews; he opposed only the orthodox.

When the Scythians overran the Thracian and Macedonian country, he went out to stand against them. At that time the great father Isaac, meeting him as he rode, said: "Give back the churches to the orthodox, emperor, and know that you will return victorious; but if you campaign against God, you will not return from there." The most impious emperor was angered by this, and ordered the saint to be kept under guard until he returned. And he said: "If you return, God has not spoken in me." And Valens saw in a dream a man saying to him:

"Go quickly to great Mimas, where a terrible doom snatches you, wretch."

Waking, then, he asked who Mimas might be. And one of those who had devoted themselves to letters (for such men followed the emperors and were their intimates, as I wish they did now too) told him that Mimas was a mountain of Asia lying by the sea, and that Homer mentions it in the Odyssey, saying "past windy Mimas". And he said: "What need have I, then, to go to this mountain and die there?" So campaigning against the Scythians and engaging them near Thrace, he was shamefully defeated, and fleeing he hid in a building, beside which a heap of chaff and sweepings was piled up. When after the emperor's defeat the Scythians plundered that country and also set the houses on fire, that building too was burned down, and Valens perished in it. But Saint Isaac, though shut up, knew in the spirit of Valens' burning, and told those who happened to be there that Valens was at that moment dying by fire. The day was noted, and afterwards it was learned that the saint had not been mistaken. After the barbarians had withdrawn from there, when some were searching for the emperor's body, a tomb was found in the house in which he had been burned, with an epitaph of an ancient man: "Here lies Mimas, Macedonian general."

Against this emperor Procopius, the cousin of Julian, rose up and took Byzantium. But betrayed by his own men, and tied by the legs to two trees bent down by force, when the trees were let go the wretch was torn apart. At that time the walls of the city of Chalcedon were also pulled down, as its citizens had favoured Procopius. As they were being pulled down, a slab was found in their foundations with this written on it:

"But when the Nymphs, delighting in a sacred dance through the city, take their stand along the well-garlanded streets, and a wall becomes a lamentable defence for baths, then countless tribes of far-scattered men, wild, raging, clad in evil strength, crossing the stream of Cimmerian Ister with the spear, will destroy both the Scythian country and the Mysian land. And setting foot on the Thracian land with frenzied hopes, there they would meet the end of life and their doom."

Valens used the material of the walls of Chalcedon to build a channel of water, which common speech calls an aqueduct, and named it after himself, Valens; by it he had water brought into the city, so that it should have plenty of water both for other uses and for baths. And the prefect of the city built a Nymphaeum in the place called the Taurus, displaying through it the benefits of the water channel. These things were followed by the barbarians' invasion too, as written on the slab; they plundered Thrace, and were later destroyed in it.

Under this Valens, it is said, Libanius the sophist and Iamblichus, the teacher of Proclus, practised divination by a cock, seeking to learn who would reign after Valens. It is, as the story goes, of this kind. The twenty-four letters are written in dust, and a grain of wheat or barley is put on each of them. Then a cock is let loose, while certain incantations are sung over them, and it is watched from which letters it takes the grains. And these, put together, are thought to reveal what is sought. When they did this then, they saw the cock take the grain on the theta, and the one on the epsilon, the one on the omicron and the one on the delta. So what was revealed seemed ambiguous: it seemed to mean either Theodorus, or Theodosius, or Theodotus. Learning this, Valens suspected and destroyed many of those called by such names. He also sought those who had made the divination. So Iamblichus, fearing his cruelty, drank a deadly poison, as some have recorded, and took himself out of life. For Valens was hard to appease in his anger; so he used to say that a man who quickly put aside his anger would very quickly put aside justice too. He reigned thirteen years and four months, and perished as his impiety deserved.

## 13.17

Gratian the son of Valentinian and the younger Valentinian his brother became masters of the Roman empire; for Gratian alone had been proclaimed by his father, as has already been related. But when Valentinian died, Gratian was not present at his father's death. So the army at that time proclaimed the younger Valentinian emperor, who was then four years old. Gratian, returning from abroad, reproached the soldiers and even ill-treated some of them, punishing the ringleaders of his brother's proclamation for having proclaimed another without the consent of himself, who was emperor. But he did not refuse his brother himself as co-ruler, but took him as partner in the rule. This emperor emulated his father in piety. So when his uncle Valens once asked him for help against the Scythians, he did not give it, writing to him that one must not fight alongside the enemy of God. And to the shepherds who had been driven out of their own churches he granted by decree their return to them.

When after the defeat of Valens the Scythians had swollen in their pride, and were plundering Thrace and the parts around it and could not be checked, he summoned Theodosius from Hispania (Hispania is the most distinguished city of European Iberia), a man very brave and very pious. He sent him as general with an army against the barbarians; and he engaged them, won, and raised a trophy, a great multitude of Scythians being slaughtered in the war, and the rest turning to flight, some perishing as they were caught, others destroyed by one another in the flight. When nearly all those barbarians had perished, Theodosius left the army there and came himself to bring the news of the victory to Gratian, who was then staying in Pannonia. When he reported the victory to the emperor and announced the good news of the barbarians' destruction, he was disbelieved, because of the speed of the deed and the Scythians' being so very hard to fight. But when the emperor learned the truth and was fully assured of the barbarians' destruction, he admired the man for his speed and his prowess, and praised him. As the share of Valens had now been added to him after his death, he looked at the almost boundless extent of the empire, and realising that he could not by himself alone govern so great an empire, he proclaimed Theodosius emperor of New Rome, at once rewarding him for his prowess, and at the same time judging that no one else would be better than him for partnership in the rule. So he assigned all the east and Thrace to him, and allotted the west to himself. And when he had come to the Gauls he was killed by treachery by Andragathius the general, having reigned six years after his father's death.

## 13.18

When Gratian had died, the younger Valentinian was left as sovereign emperor of the west, not yet having reached manhood. He, corrupted by his mother Justina, who was an Arianiser, assented to the doctrine of the Arians and opposed the orthodox. When Maximus rose against him and attempted a usurpation and got the upper hand in battles, he wrote to the emperor Theodosius what had happened, asking for help. And he wrote back that one should not wonder if the slave gets the better of the master when he has risen against one who rejects his own Master and calls the Creator, who is of one substance and equal honour with the Father, a creature and a slave. But he went to his aid, and he captured and killed Maximus, and Andragathius the general, who had murdered Gratian. Then again Eugenius rose against the younger Valentinian and set about a usurpation. Valentinian, afraid, took himself out of life by hanging. And learning of Eugenius' usurpation, Theodosius took the field against him.

When he came to Thessalonica with the army, he was insulted by the Thessalonians, and the prefect was murdered, the people having rioted for certain reasons. At the time, then, the emperor seemed to bear the people's disturbance with patience; but afterwards he proclaimed a horse race, and when the people had gathered in the theatre he set the armies round them, and they shot the people with arrows and javelins, so that up to fifteen thousand of them died. And having sated his anger in this way, Theodosius set out from there and came to the city of Milan. There he was rebuked by the great Ambrose and not allowed to enter the church. And he did not allow him entry to the divine precinct until he made a law that sentences of death should not be carried out until thirty days had passed after the sentence. He did this because of the emperor's quickness to anger, so that, with his anger calmed over the thirty days, he might review the sentences dispassionately and confirm the lawful ones, and condemn to idleness those voted perhaps in anger. Theodosius engaged the usurper Eugenius in the Gauls, defeated him, captured him, and killed him.

This emperor, while a private man, had a wife who was pious, chaste, and a friend of the poor, Placilla, by whom Honorius and Arcadius were born to him; and he made her Augusta. When she died after his accession, he married Galla, the daughter of Valentinian the Great.

While the emperor was away in the west, the Jews, winning over the prefect of the city, Honoratus, built a costly synagogue in the Chalkoprateia, with his permission; for he championed the cause of the Hellenes. The people of the city, angered at this, reviled the prefect; and he took no account of their abuse. So the people, not bearing it, set fire to the synagogue at night and burned it. The prefect then wrote to the emperor Theodosius about this. And he imposed on those who had dared to burn the synagogue the repayment of what had been spent on it, and allowed it to be built again. The great Ambrose learned of this, and when the emperor was staying in the city of Milan and came to the church on one of the feasts of the Lord, he began to say: "Why, emperor, do you yourself insult the one who made you emperor from a private man, and put into your hands his own flock, which he acquired with his own blood, and crowned your head that was once unkempt, by putting those who reject him above those who acknowledge him, and exacting penalties from Christians on behalf of Jews, and forcing them, in the middle of a city where Christ is preached and the cross worshipped, to build a synagogue for the killers of Christ?" And the emperor, abashed at the rebuke, said: "And shall we allow the people to do what they like, disorderly and shamelessly, in well-governed cities, bishop?" "But neither should this be allowed, emperor," the holy Ambrose retorted, "that the Jews have synagogues in the middle of a pious city and send up blasphemous prayers in the hearing of the God-fearing. Do not you decree this, most orthodox Augustus." Softened by this, Theodosius both let the Byzantines off the penalty and forbade the Jews to have a synagogue within the queen of cities.

When new taxes were imposed on the Antiochenes, the people, moved to indignation by the novelty of the levy, pulled down the images of the emperor Theodosius' former wife standing in the market-place of their city and dragged them through the public streets. Angered at this, he took away the city's rights and made it subject to neighbouring Laodicea, and threatened to treat its citizens badly; and he would have done so, had not Flavian, then high priest of Antioch, come to the emperor and interceded and made him give up his anger. At that time too the great father John Chrysostom, who was a presbyter of the church in Antioch, wrote the speeches entitled On the Statues.

## 13.19

At that time also Gregory, great in theology, who had formerly taught the orthodox in secret in the church of Saint Anastasia because of the shamelessness and boldness of the heretics, when the emperor Theodosius had given back the churches to the orthodox and driven out the Arianisers, openly proclaimed the Son of one substance with the Father and the Holy Spirit as God, worshipped and glorified together with both. For Macedonius, who had been patriarch of Constantinople for a short while, as has already been related, would not allow it to be called God, nor equal in power with the Father and the Son, nor yet of one substance. So the second council was convened at that time, at the emperor's order, a hundred and fifty God-bearing fathers being gathered in the queen of cities. Leading them in the contests were the Theologian, and the great Gregory, bishop of Nyssa, and the holy Amphilochius, head of the church of Iconium. They decreed the Holy Spirit both God and of one substance and of equal power with the Father and the Son, cut off Macedonius and those who held his opinion from the catholic church, added to the holy creed the clause about the Holy Spirit, from "And in the Holy Spirit" to the end, and confirmed what had been decreed at the first council. At that time also some of the bishops, envious of the Theologian because of the throne of Constantinople, said that he had not mounted the throne canonically, since he had previously been proclaimed for another see. So the saint wrote his farewell speech, read it publicly, stepped down from the throne, and returned to his own homeland, Nazianzus. Nectarius was ordained patriarch of Constantinople, a man of senatorial rank who had held civil offices. At that time too the throne of New Rome obtained the second rank, placed after elder Rome but preferred to the others.

The great Amphilochius too at that time asked the emperor to have the Arians driven out of the city, as blaspheming the Son of God, or at least not to be allowed to hold meetings. When he saw that Theodosius was slow about this, he watched for an opportunity, when Arcadius too was sitting with his father, and went in and gave the emperor the fitting greeting and obeisance, but said carelessly to Arcadius: "Greetings to you too, child." When the emperor was angry at the slight to his son, the saint then said: "You, a man, did not bear calmly the dishonour to your son. Do you think that God does not loathe and hate those who blaspheme his only-begotten Son, and is not angry with those who allow them to mix with the orthodox and corrupt many?" The emperor, admiring the saint's method, forbade the heretics' assemblies by decree.

When this emperor had brought down Eugenius, the last usurper, the whole empire of the Romans came round to him, and he proclaimed his two sons emperors. Wishing them to share in education both in letters and in character, he brought from Rome the great Arsenius, who was a deacon of the church there, famous for wisdom and virtue; and he handed the boys over to him, instructing him to treat them not as emperors but as private persons and ordinary boys, and to whip them if he saw them negligent or straying from what was proper, and to deal with them as with his own children. He held Arsenius in great honour and enriched him with money. Arsenius took the boys and seated them on thrones, while he himself stood to give them their lessons. And once the emperor came upon them suddenly, and finding them sitting and Arsenius standing, he was angry; and making the boys get up from the thrones and seating Arsenius, he ordered him to teach them like that. And from then on they stood by the teacher, while the one teaching sat. Once, then, when Arcadius had made a slip, the teacher gave him a whipping. He, resenting it, made a plot against him because of this, resolved to kill the man, and made ready the assassin. Arsenius, learning what was planned, withdrew secretly from the palace, and going to Scetis he entered the solitary life and became equal to the angels. The emperor Theodosius made a great search for him but did not learn where on earth he was.

This emperor fell ill in Milan and departed this life, having governed the empire for seventeen years and five months. He divided it between his two sons, assigning to Arcadius New Rome and the eastern part, and allotting to Honorius elder Rome and the west.

## 13.20

So each of them ruled the part allotted to him; and learning that the great Arsenius was living as a monk in Scetis, they wrote to him very many times asking him to pray for them. Arcadius also wrote to the saint privately, begging him to grant him pardon for the plot against him, and offered to let him take the tribute of all Egypt for that year and use it as he wished. But the great Arsenius would not write back to the emperors; he told those sent to him to say to them: "God will forgive you and will make you do his will; but to me, already dead to the world, money is useless."

This emperor Arcadius built a city in Thrace and named it Arcadiopolis, and he raised the column in the Xerolophos and set up a statue of himself on it. When Nectarius, patriarch of Constantinople, died, he brought John of the golden tongue from Antioch the Great and put him forward as patriarch of New Rome. But being sluggish and soft in mind beyond what was proper, he was ruled by his wife Eudoxia. She was a reckless woman and a slave to love of money, and for this reason most unjust. So, being checked in her impulses by the great father, and sometimes even rebuked, she was roused to anger and was eager to take revenge on the saint. Finding Theophilus of Alexandria as a partner in her wickedness, she persuaded Arcadius to condemn the saint to exile. And he was thrown out of the church and led away into exile. Of the people of the city, some lamented, others rioted. When the sluggish Arcadius learned this, he sent in haste recalling John, and he was brought back and restored to the church again. But he, being unenslaved, did not stop moving his tongue against wrongdoers. The empress Eudoxia, applying to herself the teachings about injustice (for her conscience convicted her of being such), was furious, and inflamed her wrath against the great father. And again, to pass over what happened in between, she persuaded her husband, leading him as though by a halter, to condemn the saint to exile. And he was thrust out of the church tyrannically and led away to dangerous lands and to the most distant frontiers, having experienced many hardships on the road, which one can hear his golden tongue relating in his letters. Having reached Cucusus, and being led away from there to Pityus, when he was at Comana he measured out his life there, aged fifty-two, having held the high priesthood of the queen of cities for five and a half years. Divine justice did not slumber, however, but quickly pursued that thrice-wretched empress. For not yet three months had passed after the death of father Chrysostom when she met a violent death. For while she was carrying, the foetus died, and sharp pains gripped her; and when it rotted inside, the empress's womb also took part in the rot, and so she departed this life wretchedly. When that golden-mouthed father had been thrown out of the church, one Arsacius was put in his place, and after being bishop for two years he died; after him Atticus was ordained.

Eudoxia had died before her husband and emperor, and Arcadius himself followed her not long after, having reigned after the death of his father Theodosius for fourteen years and three months and three days. The column standing at the place called the Pittakia is said to be Eudoxia's. Dying, Arcadius left as successor to the throne his son Theodosius, called the Little to distinguish him from his grandfather, or because he was at a very childish age when his father died; for he was then only seven years old. But let the account of him be put off; let the narrative go through the affairs of Honorius in summary.

## 13.21

He was left by his father as emperor of elder Rome at a very young age (for he was ten years old), and was under the guardianship, by his father's decision, of Stilicho, a man who was first among those in Rome and had been entrusted with the administration of public affairs. He took Stilicho's daughter Maria as his wife. When she died he is said to have married her sister Thermantia, who was not yet of full age. And she too died quickly. Being simple-minded he was hated by the powerful men in Rome, and learning that he was being plotted against he moved to Ravenna, and is said to have sent to Alaric, the ruler of the nation of the Vandals or Goths, asking him to march against Rome, wishing in this way to take revenge on the citizens of Rome for their hatred of him. And Alaric, they say, attacked Rome, but did not sack it; he made a treaty with the Romans, took all the money found in the palace and Honorius' sister Placidia, and went back. He handed the girl over to a certain count Constantius to be guarded; and the man who had received her ran away and brought her to her brother, and he married her to him, having honoured him with senatorial rank, and then even proclaimed him emperor; for Honorius had no offspring of his own. And shortly afterwards Constantius was slain, Placidia having borne him children, Valentinian and Honoria.

Some, then, record these things in this way; but others say that Stilicho was slain, that those in Rome were hostile to Honorius, and that he moved to Ravenna; that Alaric, despising his simple-mindedness, marched on Rome and took it by siege; and that when it was taken someone in tears came to Honorius reporting that Rome had been taken by Alaric. He, shaken in his body by a spasm at the news and striking his thighs with his hands, said: "Rome was here just now. How then has it been taken?" The messenger, understanding, and groaning greatly, said: "I am speaking of the city and not of the bird." For this emperor had an enormous hen, in which he delighted, which he called Rome; and he cried out because of it, thinking it had been taken.

Honorius fell into dropsy and died, having lived forty years and reigned ingloriously thirty of them. A certain John, usurping in Rome, drove out from there his sister Placidia with her two children, Valentinian and Honoria.

## 13.22

The little Theodosius, living in Constantinople, was brought up by his own sister Pulcheria, carefully nurtured and educated, no one rising against them, out of fear of Isdigerdes, who was king of the Persians. For Arcadius, when he was dying, wrote Isdigerdes in his will as guardian of his son, who was still a child, as has been said. When the will was brought to him, he sent Antiochus, the most honoured of the eunuchs at his court, to be guardian and protector of the emperor Theodosius, urging the officials by letter to submit to their emperor; and threatening them that if they did not do so, he himself would come to avenge the boy.

When he had now begun to be counted among the youths, his sister Pulcheria betrothed him to Eudocia of Athens, who was of extraordinary beauty and had a share of every kind of learning. She was the daughter of one Leontius, a philosopher from Athens, and was named Athenais. He, knowing from his science that his daughter was going to have brilliant good fortune, when making his will left his property to his sons (there were two, Valerius and Gesius), and ordered that only a hundred gold pieces be given to his daughter, writing that her fortune was enough for her. So when her brothers had appropriated their father's estate, Athenais demanded her own lawful share; and she asked her brothers not to act according to their father's will, which was unjust. But they even drove her out of her father's house. Her aunt on her mother's side took her in and went up to Constantinople, and they petitioned Pulcheria, telling her the case. She, seeing that the girl was very beautiful and young, asked whether she was still untouched by a man; and learning this, she baptised her, as she was still uninitiated, and renaming her Eudocia she married her to her brother Theodosius, crowned her with a diadem, and called her Augusta.

Having married her, Theodosius got rid of Antiochus, who was the most powerful of the imperial eunuchs and stirred up everything, holding power himself, not merely sharing in it. And so Antiochus was removed from the honour of praepositus; all his property was taken away, and he himself was tonsured as a cleric at the church of the all-praised martyr Euphemia in Chalcedon, and died not long after. The empress Eudocia summoned her brothers, and bearing them no grudge, but rather acknowledging thanks, since she would not have obtained the throne if she had not been driven out by them and for that reason gone to Constantinople, she had Gesius made prefect of Illyricum through the emperor, and honoured Valerius as magister.

The patriarch Atticus, having brought a paralysed Jew to the knowledge of Christ by his exhortations, granted him the divine bath and brought him out of the font healed. He also entered the name of the holy Chrysostom in the church's diptychs, where it had not been entered before; for that most pious father was both called and thought an Origenist. When Atticus died, who had headed the body of the faithful for twenty years, Sisinnius was ordained, and having held the high priesthood for two years he measured out his life, in the twenty-second year of the reign of Theodosius. And Nestorius was put in his place. Having held the high priesthood for three years, he taught people not to call the holy Virgin Mary Theotokos, decreeing that Christ was a mere man and babbling that the Son of God dwelt in him as in one of the prophets. Dividing him into two, he insisted that the Word of God did not take flesh from the pure blood of the Mother of God, but that the Son and Word of God was one, and Christ, made a son by adoption and deified by grace, was another. When the heads of the other patriarchal thrones learned this, Celestine of Rome, Cyril of Alexandria, John of Antioch, and Juvenal of Jerusalem, they referred it to the sovereign Theodosius and Pulcheria, asking for a council to be assembled and for what Nestorius was teaching to be examined. So a council of two hundred fathers was convened at Ephesus, led by the most holy Cyril, who also held the place of Celestine of Rome, as he had been unable to attend because of illness. The doctrines of Nestorius were examined and rejected as heterodox; the holy Virgin was shown to be Theotokos, to be believed and called so, and the one incarnate from her without a man was proclaimed by those holy fathers to be Son of God and God. For a clearer confession of the faith and a plainer refutation of the impiety of Nestorius, the most holy Cyril also composed twelve chapters and handed them to the church as memorials of the right belief. Nestorius was deposed and removed from the company of the right-believing high priests.

Three days after the council, John of Antioch, Theodoret bishop of Cyrrhus, Ibas of Edessa, and others arrived at Ephesus; and because the members of the council had not waited for their arrival too, they were angry with the great Cyril as the leader of the rest, found fault with the deposition of Nestorius, and taking his side voted the deposition of the divine Cyril and of Memnon of Ephesus. Theodoret also attacked Cyril's twelve chapters, and composed others to overturn them, against which the great Cyril contended in turn, proving that Theodoret did not teach rightly. The bishop of Antioch and the rest were condemned by the council and estranged from the orthodox. With matters standing so, and the bishops set against one another, and a schism among them, the emperor Theodosius ordered them all to come to Constantinople; and when they had arrived in the emperor's presence, the inquiry took place, and Nestorius was deposed and exiled to the east, the bishop of Antioch and Theodoret agreeing with the holy council, as having opposed it before out of vexation and rivalry. When Nestorius was perverting many, John of Antioch made a report about this to the emperor, asking that he be moved elsewhere. And the wretch was carried off to Oasis, a barren land of Arabia, blown over by destructive winds.

Maximian, a presbyter of the church, was put in his place in the pastorate of New Rome. And after him, when he had held the high priesthood for two years, Proclus, the disciple of Chrysostom, was appointed patriarch. He had earlier been ordained bishop of Cyzicus by the patriarch Sisinnius, but had not been accepted by the people there, who chose another high priest, and had been without office in the meantime. Enthroned, then, on the throne of Constantine's city, he asked the emperor that the body of Chrysostom be brought back from Pityus, and that the saint not remain in exile even in death. The emperor was persuaded by him, and the saint's body was brought back, received with surpassing honour, and laid within the sanctuary of the famous church of the Holy Apostles.

The emperor Theodosius, wishing to move the walls of the city and make its circuit larger, entrusted the work to Cyrus the prefect; and he, using great zeal and eagerness, raised the land wall from sea to sea in sixty days. So the people of the city, pleased at the work and greatly admiring the prefect for his speed, cried out publicly: "[Constantine] built, Cyrus renewed." For this he was envied and suspected by the emperor, and was tonsured a cleric against his will, and then became bishop of Smyrna.

## 13.23

When the patriarch Proclus died, in the twelfth year after he had headed the orthodox people in Constantinople, the divine Flavian was installed on his throne. At that time also Eutyches, who was an archimandrite, taught that our Lord Jesus Christ did not keep the two natures after the incarnation, but that both were mixed and blended together into one nature. When he remained uncorrected, Flavian cut him off from the body of the church, so that he would not pass on his corruption to what was healthy. So Eutyches went to Chrysaphius the eunuch, who shared his belief and had very great power with the emperor; and he persuaded Theodosius to allow Dioscorus, who held the throne of Alexandria after the departure of Saint Cyril, to come to Ephesus with other bishops and examine the case of Eutyches, with Flavian also present. This Dioscorus, then, being of the same belief as Eutyches, and having taken with him other bishops of the same mind, came to Ephesus and confirmed what Eutyches was introducing. When the most holy Flavian spoke against it, Dioscorus leapt up like some wild ass and jumped with his heel on that pious man's chest, and beating him on the temple with his fist did not let up until he had pushed him out of the assembly. And the saint, two days later, departed this life from the blow to his chest, having adorned the high-priestly throne of the younger Rome for two years. Dioscorus forced the bishops present there to subscribe to the definition he had set out. For the power of the eunuch mentioned had caused armed soldiers to be present there and to frighten those who objected. Domnus, the bishop of Antioch, subscribed too, being swept along, but then denounced Dioscorus and attacked the definition as full of impiety.

When the emperor Theodosius learned of this and came to know about Flavian, he held Chrysaphius to blame and was angry with him. Dioscorus, afraid, advised him to persuade the emperor to appoint as patriarch of Constantinople Anatolius, his own apocrisiarius, so that he would receive Eutyches into communion and the affair of the blessed Flavian would remain uninvestigated. So the eunuch persuaded the emperor, who was easily led, and Anatolius was ordained patriarch. This Chrysaphius, taking advantage of the ruler's compliance, also persuaded him, with the empress Eudocia as his helper, to remove Pulcheria from the administration of affairs. And she withdrew from the palace and lived by herself and kept quiet at the Hebdomon. But the emperor, coming to himself late and with difficulty, and reckoning up the bloody murder of the blessed Flavian and the affair of Pulcheria, and finding that profane eunuch to be the cause of everything, was moved to just anger against him, and condemned him to exile and confiscated his property, and summoning his sister he asked her pardon, and put the administration of affairs in her hands again.

Pulcheria was very intelligent, and of her brother's many shortcomings she corrected some and covered up others. It is said, then, that this emperor signed documents without paying attention; and she advised him not to confirm everything put before him, but to look over what was written first. He insisted that he was not being cheated by anyone; for he maintained that he knew what was being written. So, to prove her brother's sluggishness of mind, Pulcheria thought of something like this. She drew up a document of sale in his name, as though he had sold the Augusta Eudocia to her, and putting the deed of purchase before him as something else, she persuaded him to confirm it with his signature; then, when Eudocia was summoned by him, she would not let her go. When the emperor asked the reason, Pulcheria showed him the bill of sale, and so proved to him that he signed many things he neither knew nor perhaps wished to happen. At the time, then, as that pretended sale had no force, the empress was restored to the emperor.

But afterwards, when a certain cause arose, the emperor fell out of love with the Augusta. The cause: an apple of extraordinary size was brought to the emperor. He, marvelling at it, sent it to the empress, and she gave the apple to Paulinus. The man was learned, and for that reason close to the empress. He, admiring the size of the apple and knowing nothing about it, presented it to the emperor. He recognised what was presented, and hiding it he asked his wife where the apple sent to her was. She said she had eaten it, afraid that her husband would suspect what he already suspected. He asked again more insistently; and she again insisted with an oath that she had eaten the apple. The emperor, full of anger, brought it out. And she was caught in the act not telling the truth. And for him the suspicion was confirmed. And so Paulinus was killed on a false suspicion, and Eudocia was hated. She asked to go to Jerusalem, and going there with great wealth she built churches and gave much to the poor and to the monasteries there. Returning from there, she went there again after her husband's death, and died there. What she was like in letters is shown by the so-called Homerocentra. For when one Patricius had attempted the work but left it unfinished and, so to speak, unarranged, she brought it to completion and arranged it, as the inscription in heroic verse which she gave the work shows those who read it.

The emperor Theodosius died at the age of fifty, having reigned almost all the years he lived, a very few with his father and the others on his own. As to the cause of his death, some say it was illness, others have recorded that while he was riding in a hunt his horse fell with him, and that, having been struck in vital parts, he died shortly afterwards. He was not unacquainted with literary education, but had also had a share of the other mathematical books and had a taste of astronomy itself; he was trained to the highest degree in riding and archery, and was skilful at painting and modelling. Being mild in character he was soft in the handling of affairs; so the eunuchs who had power with him did many things they should not have, Antiochus and after him Amantius, and lastly Chrysaphius.

In the times of this emperor, when the patriarch Proclus was holding a procession of prayer with the clergy and a very large part of the city's population, suddenly a child was lifted up into the air. As the people were amazed and cried "Lord, have mercy" for a very long time, the child was brought down again, saying it had been taught that "who was crucified for us" must not be added to the Trisagion.

## 13.24

So Theodosius died. Pulcheria, before the emperor's death was yet known to many, sent for Marcian, a man already old, but good in character and self-controlled, and told him of the emperor's death, and said: "I have chosen you before all for emperor, if you will give me assurance that you will keep my virginity untouched, which I have dedicated to God." When he agreed, she summoned the patriarch and the senate, and proclaimed him and crowned him with a diadem.

Marcian had not been one of the distinguished men before, nor of those of brilliant family, but simply a soldier. Once, going off on campaign with his own regiment, he fell ill in the province of the Lycians and was left behind by his comrades. He was given hospitality there by two brothers, one named Julius and the other Tatianus, and when he had recovered from his illness he went out hunting with his hosts. In the course of the hunt they became tired out and lay down on the ground to rest, and fell asleep around midday. Tatianus shook off sleep before the others, and saw an eagle with its wings spread out shading Marcian and keeping off from him the heat of the sun's rays. Marvelling, he quietly woke his brother and showed him the strange sight; and he too was astounded. Both men, then, thought that what the eagle had done to Marcian signified the throne, and when he woke from sleep they foretold the throne to him, and asked him to remember them when he obtained the rule; and giving him two hundred coins they let him go. Again, campaigning with Aspar, who had undertaken a war against the Vandals, he was taken prisoner with very many others, and was kept under guard in a courtyard with his fellow captives. The leader of the Vandals, leaning out from above, was looking over the captives, and he too saw Marcian asleep, and an eagle above him with its wings raised making shade for him. He too, then, took the sight as an omen pointing to the throne, and sending for Marcian he demanded of him that if he became emperor he would not make war on the Vandals; and when he agreed he released him from prison. So in this way God signified the throne to him long before. And then what had been foretold came to pass, and at once he summoned the brothers who had been his hosts and had announced the good news of the rule to him, and he appointed Tatianus prefect of the city and entrusted Julius with the government of Illyricum. He was good to everyone.

In these times Pulcheria built the church of the Mother of God at Blachernae, and the fourth council took place, Leo the pope of Rome and Anatolius the patriarch of New Rome having asked the emperor Marcian that the affair of Dioscorus of Alexandria and Eutyches, and all that they had dared against the most holy Flavian, should not remain uninvestigated. So it was decided that a council should take place, and six hundred and thirty God-bearing fathers gathered in the church of the holy martyr Euphemia in Chalcedon. Their leaders were Leo the pope of Rome, Anatolius of Constantinople, and Juvenal of Jerusalem. The multitude of fathers was assembled against Dioscorus and Eutyches, who told the fable that our Lord Jesus Christ wore flesh of another substance than ours, and that he was one nature, so that the sufferings were thereby attached to the Godhead. The divine fathers deposed and anathematised them, since they would not renounce their heterodoxy. They received Theodoret and Ibas, once they had anathematised Nestorius and the chapters they themselves had published out of rivalry; and they confirmed the three earlier councils and strengthened the holy creed, bringing under anathema those who introduce a pair of sons, and those who teach that the Godhead can suffer, and those who dare to think and say that there is a mixture and confusion of the two natures, or who invent that there were two natures in Christ before the union but one after the union, and those who tell the fable that the form taken by the Lord from us was heavenly or of some other substance. And they decreed that our Lord Jesus Christ is perfect in Godhead and perfect in manhood, truly God and truly man, of a rational soul and body, of one substance with the Father in his Godhead and of one substance with us in his manhood, and like us in all things except sin; one and the same, made known in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation, the properties of each nature being preserved and coming together into one person and one hypostasis.

## 13.25

When these things had been decreed by all those God-inspired fathers, with the pious emperor Marcian also present, Dioscorus was sent into exile at Gangra by imperial order; for Eutyches had died miserably before. In place of Dioscorus, Proterius, a most holy and most orthodox man, was ordained bishop of Alexandria. When the heretics of the party of Eutyches and Dioscorus went about the city mocking what had been decreed by the council, and saying that the council had prevailed through the imperial power and not through the truth, and still trying to establish their own heterodoxy, the patriarch Anatolius called an assembly of those of his own opinion and those of the opposing belief, and said to his opponents: "Since you still hold to your own opinion and are not persuaded that you do not believe rightly, but accuse us of accepting the doctrine of Nestorius because we proclaim two natures in the Saviour, each keeping its properties unconfused, we must, if you agree, leave the decision about this to God. Let your view of the faith be written by you in a volume, and ours will be written by us; and let both volumes be placed in the coffin of the all-praised martyr Euphemia, and let the judgement between those who teach rightly and those who teach perversely be given to her." This pleased the heretics too; and the volumes were written, and the stone closing the coffin was lifted, and the papers were laid on the martyr's breast, and the stone was put back on the coffin again and sealed by both parties. After that there were supplications to God and prayers all night, that he would reveal which of the opinions seemed to him pleasing and which to be rejected. And on the third day they came together, the emperor not being absent either; and when the coffin was opened (O your wonders, Lord!) the volume of the heretics was found lying at the martyr's feet, and the other held in her right hand, which she is said to have stretched out to the emperor and the patriarch, handing them the volume. Those who championed the right doctrine broke into applause, while those of the other opinion went away downcast and covered in a darkness of shame; and some of them even changed over to orthodoxy.

At that time the empress Pulcheria also died, piously and gloriously and with good hopes. For she distributed all her wealth to the poor, and by her mercy to them she gained the mercy of God.

Valentinian, the nephew of Honorius, of whom we made mention somewhere above as born of his sister Placidia, while reigning in elder Rome, took as his wife Eudoxia, the daughter of the younger Theodosius. But living dissolutely and licentiously, he left his own wife alone, though she was very beautiful, and debauched himself with other men's wives; he also resorted to divinations and sorceries. Living in this unholy way, he died pitiably. For Maximus, one of the patricians in Rome, a grandson of the Maximus who had usurped long before and whom Theodosius the Great defeated in war, got into the palace and slaughtered Valentinian in it, and took Eudoxia by force, and seized the throne. She, since her father Theodosius had died and Pulcheria had measured out her life, and she hoped for vengeance from no other quarter, sent to Gizericus, who was called rex of the Vandals, and begged him to free her from the tyranny of Maximus; and he attacked Rome with a fleet and a large force. Maximus fled from it, and was killed by those with him. Gizericus took Rome without effort, and having gathered up all its wealth, and the offerings of the divine churches made of gold and adorned with stones, and some of the vessels of the temple there which Titus had carried off from Jerusalem, these too made of gold, and taking Eudoxia with her two daughters, he returned to Africa. He married one of her daughters, Eudocia, to the elder of his own sons, Honorichus; and learning that Placidia was betrothed to a husband, the patrician Olybrius, he kept her with her mother Eudoxia. The empress Eudoxia, having spent two years there, returned to Byzantium with her daughter Placidia while Marcian was reigning. For Eudocia was left with her husband; and having lived with him for sixteen years and borne a son, Ilderichus, because her husband was of the Arians' belief she was distressed at living with him. And seizing an opportunity, she too moved to Constantinople, her mother having already died; then she went off to Jerusalem and died there.

Marcian died having reigned six years and some months, as some say, from illness, as others, poisoned at the nod of the patrician Aspar, having become an old man and lived long. Being mild in mind and good to his subjects, he used to say that an emperor must not take up arms as long as it was possible to be at peace. The patrician Aspar, who then had great power, was not chosen to be emperor by the people of the city, because he belonged to the heresy of Arius. So he himself made Leo emperor, as some have recorded, on his own initiative, as others, having received the authority from the people. Leo, it is said, was the manager of his estates, and Aspar demanded of him that he crown one of his sons Caesar. And when he had promised this, Aspar brought him up to the height of the throne. Some say he was a tribune and commander of the units at Selymbria. And some say he drew his descent from the Thracians, others from the Dacians in Illyricum.
