# Orosius, Historiae adversus Paganos: our English translation

Translated from the Latin text on The Latin Library, one section per chapter, with its book and chapter numbers. Made with Claude Opus 5.5 (docs/adr/0003). So far Books 1 to 4 (the Creation to the fall of Carthage), and Book 7, chapters 17 to 25 (Septimius Severus to Diocletian), made on 14 November 2026.

## 1.praef

I have obeyed your instructions, most blessed father Augustine; and would that I had done so as effectively as I did it gladly. Yet I am little troubled either way about the outcome, whether I have acted rightly or otherwise. For you have already laboured over that judgement, whether I could do what you were instructing; I am content with the testimony of obedience alone, if indeed I have adorned it with will and effort. For in the great house of a great father of a family, though there are many animals of different kinds useful in helping the household, the care of the dogs is not the least. To them alone is it given by nature to be eager of their own will for what they are trained for, and, through an inborn rule of obedience, to be held back only by the expectation of a disciplined fear, until they are sent off by a nod or a sign with leave to do it. For they have their own appetites, as much nearer to rational creatures as they excel the brutes: that is, to discern, to love and to serve. For, discerning between masters and strangers, they do not hate those they chase, but are zealous for those they love; and loving their master and his house, they keep watch not as if from a body fit by nature, but out of the conscience of an anxious love. Hence also, in a mystic sacrament in the Gospels, the Canaanite woman was not ashamed to say, and the Lord did not disdain to hear, that the little dogs eat the crumbs under their masters' table. The blessed Tobias too, following the angel as his guide, did not scorn to have a dog as his companion. So, bound to your love for all by a special love, I have willingly obeyed your will. For since my submission owes the deed to the command of your fatherhood, and all of it is yours, since it returns from you to you, I have rendered my work more abundantly in this one thing that is mine: that I did it gladly.

You had instructed me to reply to the empty-talking perversity of those who, strangers to the City of God, are called pagans from the crossroads and villages (pagi) of country places, or gentiles because they are wise in earthly things. Since they do not seek the future, and either forget or do not know the past, they nevertheless defame the present times as if they were beset by evils beyond the usual, for this reason alone: that Christ is believed and God is worshipped, while idols are worshipped less. You had instructed me, then, to set out in an orderly and brief book whatever I could find, in all the records of histories and annals that can be had at present, from the ages gone by, that was grievous with wars, or corrupted with diseases, or sad with famine, or terrible with earthquakes, or unusual with floods of water, or fearful with eruptions of fire, or cruel with strokes of lightning and the blows of hail, or wretched too with parricides and crimes. This above all because it was not fitting that your reverence, intent on completing the eleventh book against these same pagans (the ten rising rays of which, as soon as they were lifted up from the watchtower of the Church's brightness, shone over the whole world), should be occupied with a slight little work; and your holy son Julian of Carthage, a servant of God, was demanding that his request in this matter be satisfied, with the same confidence with which he had made it. I set to work, and first of all I was overwhelmed with confusion myself, for as I often reflected on it the disasters of the present times had seemed to me to have boiled over beyond measure. For I have found that past days were not only as heavy as these, but more terribly wretched by as much as they were further from the remedy of the true religion. So that by this inquiry it has rightly become clear that death, greedy for blood, reigned while the religion that would forbid bloodshed was unknown; that as this religion dawned, death was stunned; that death is shut in now that the religion prevails; and that death will be nothing at all when this religion alone shall reign. I except, of course, and set aside those last days at the end of the world, at the appearing of Antichrist or even at the conclusion of the Judgement, when the Lord Christ foretold through the Holy Scriptures, and by His own testimony too, that there would be distress such as there had never been before. Then, by the same measure that is now and always is, but in a plainer and heavier trial, the intolerable tribulations of those times will bring proof to the saints and destruction to the wicked.

## 1.1

Almost all men devoted to writing, among the Greeks as well as the Latins, who have spread abroad in words the deeds of kings and peoples to keep their memory long, have made the beginning of their writing with Ninus, son of Belus, king of the Assyrians. Though in their blind opinion they would have the origin of the world and the creation of men believed to be without beginning, they nevertheless define kingdoms and wars as having begun with him; as if indeed the human race had lived until then in the manner of cattle, and only then had awoken, shaken and roused as if to a new foresight. I, however, have determined to trace the beginning of men's misery from the beginning of man's sin, touching on only a few things, and those briefly. From Adam, the first man, to Ninus, the 'great' king as they call him, when Abraham was born, there are 3,184 years, which have been either passed over or not known by all the historians. From Ninus or Abraham to Caesar Augustus, that is to the birth of Christ, which was in the forty-second year of Caesar's rule, when peace was made with the Parthians, the gates of Janus were shut and wars ceased in the whole world, are reckoned 2,015 years, in which the leisure and business of all wore themselves out between those who did deeds and those who wrote them. Therefore the matter itself demands that I touch, as briefly as possible, a few things from those books which, speaking of the origin of the world, made the past credible by foretelling the future and by the proof that followed afterwards. Not that I would seem to press their authority on anyone, but because it is worthwhile to recall the common opinion that we share with all. First, because if the world and man are directed by divine providence, which is just as it is good; and since man, who because of the changeableness of his nature and the freedom of his choice is both weak and stubborn, ought to be governed with kindness when he is in need of help, and must be corrected with justice when he is unrestrained in his freedom: then whoever sees the human race through himself and in himself perceives that this world has rightly been exercised, from the beginning of man, by good and evil things in turn. Next, since we are taught that sin and the punishment of sin began from the first man himself. Further, since even these writers, beginning from the middle of time, though they nowhere mention what came earlier, have described nothing but wars and disasters (and what else are wars to be called but evils falling on one side or the other? And evils of this kind, as they were then and as they are now, in so far as they are, are without doubt either open sins or the hidden punishments of sins): what hinders us from laying open the head of the matter whose body they have set out, and from showing, even by the slightest account, that those earlier ages, which we show to have been far more numerous, endured similar miseries?

So, being about to tell of the conflicts of the human race from the founding of the world to the founding of the city, and from there to the principate of Caesar and the birth of Christ, from which time the empire of the world has remained under the power of the city, or even to our own days, as far as I am able to call them to knowledge, and being about to show, as from a watchtower, the world burning with evils in its various parts, set alight by the torch of greed, I think it necessary first to set out the very circle of the lands that the human race inhabits, as it was divided into three by our ancestors and then marked out by regions and provinces. So that, when the disasters of wars and diseases are shown in their places, the studious may gain knowledge not only of events and times but of places too.

## 1.2

Our ancestors laid down that the circle of the whole earth, surrounded by the border of the Ocean, was three-cornered, and called its three parts Asia, Europe and Africa; though some have thought there were two, that is Asia, and then Africa, which should be taken as part of Europe.

Asia, girdled by the Ocean on three sides, stretches across the whole region of the east. Towards the west, on its right, it touches Europe, which begins under the pole of the north; on its left it gives way to Africa; and below Egypt and Syria it has our sea, which we generally call the Great Sea.

Europe begins, as I have said, in the region of the north, at the river Tanais, where the Riphaean mountains, facing away from the Sarmatian Ocean, pour forth the river Tanais. It passes the altars and boundaries of Alexander the Great, set in the territory of the Rhobasci, and swells the Maeotic marshes, whose immense overflow enters the Euxine Sea widely near the city of Theodosia. From there long straits are sent out near Constantinople, until this sea that we call Our Sea receives them. In Spain the western Ocean is the boundary of Europe, above all where the Pillars of Hercules are seen at the islands of Gades and the tide of the Ocean is let into the mouth of the Tyrrhenian Sea.

Africa begins at the borders of Egypt and the city of Alexandria, where the city of Paraetonium lies, on this Great Sea that washes between all the regions and the lands in the middle. From there, through the places the inhabitants call Catabathmos, not far from the camp of Alexander the Great and above the lake Chalearzus, and then past the borders of the upper Auasitae, it runs crosswise through the Ethiopian deserts and touches the southern Ocean. The boundaries of Africa to the west are the same as those of Europe, that is the mouth of the strait of Gades. Its farthest limit is Mount Atlas and the islands they call the Fortunate.

And since I have briefly given the general divisions of the threefold world, I shall take care to mark out, as I promised, the regions of the parts themselves.

Asia has, at the middle of its eastern front, the mouths of the river Ganges in the Eastern Ocean. On the left it has the promontory of Caligardamana, below which, to the south-east, lies the island of Taprobane, from which the Ocean begins to be called Indian. On the right it has the promontory of Samara on Mount Imavus, where the Caucasus ends; below it, to the north-east, lie the mouths of the river Ottorogorra, from which the Ocean is called Seric.

Within these bounds is India, which has the river Indus on the west, which is received by the Red Sea, and the Caucasus mountain on the north; the rest, as I have said, is bounded by the Eastern and the Indian Ocean. It has forty-four peoples, not counting the island of Taprobane, which has ten cities, and not counting a great many other inhabitable islands.

From the river Indus, which is on the east, to the river Tigris, which is on the west, are these regions: Arachosia, Parthia, Assyria, Persis and Media, in mountainous and rugged country. These have the Caucasus mountain on the north, and on the south the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, and in their midst the chief rivers Hydaspes and Arbis. In them there are thirty-two peoples. But it is generally called Parthia, though the Holy Scriptures often call the whole of it Media.

From the river Tigris to the river Euphrates is Mesopotamia, beginning in the north between Mount Taurus and the Caucasus. To the south it is followed by Babylonia, then Chaldaea, and last Arabia Eudaemon, which stretches eastwards in a narrow tract of land between the Persian and the Arabian Gulf. In these there are twenty-eight peoples.

From the river Euphrates, which is on the east, to Our Sea, which is on the west, and from the north, that is from the city of Dagusa, which lies on the border of Cappadocia and Armenia not far from the place where the Euphrates rises, to Egypt and the farthest end of the Arabian Gulf (which stretches westward from the Red Sea, that is from the Ocean, towards the south in a long, narrow channel full of rocks and islands), the land is generally named Syria. It has the very large provinces of Commagene, Phoenicia and Palestine, not counting the Saracens and Nabataeans, whose peoples are twelve.

At the head of Syria is Cappadocia, which has Armenia on the east, Asia on the west, the Themiscyrian plains and the Cimmerian Sea on the north-east, and on the south Mount Taurus, below which lie Cilicia and Isauria as far as the Cilician Gulf, which faces the island of Cyprus.

The region of Asia, or, to speak properly, Asia Minor, except for the eastern part where it runs on towards Cappadocia and Syria, is surrounded on every side by sea: on the north by the Euxine Sea, on the west by the Propontis and the Hellespont, on the south by Our Sea. There is Mount Olympus.

Lower Egypt has Syria Palaestina on the east, Libya on the west, Our Sea on the north, and on the south the mountain called Climax, and Upper Egypt, and the river Nile. The Nile seems to come up from the shore of the Red Sea where it begins, at the place called the market of Mossylon; then, flowing for a long way westward and making in its middle an island named Meroe, it at last bends to the north and, swollen by its seasonal floods, waters the plains of Egypt. Some writers say that it has its source not far from Atlas and is at once swallowed up by the sands; then, after a short space, it overflows into a vast lake, and from there glides eastward, as far as the Ocean, through the Ethiopian deserts, and turning again to the left comes down to Egypt. It is indeed true that there is a great river of this kind, with such a source and such a course, and that it truly breeds all the monsters of the Nile. The barbarians near its source call it Dara; the other inhabitants call it Nuhul. But in the region of the peoples who are called Libyo-Egyptians, not far from the river that we said bursts out from the shore of the Red Sea, it is received into an immense lake and swallowed up; unless perhaps it pours out by a hidden passage into the bed of the river that comes down from the east.

Upper Egypt stretches a long way to the east. It has the Arabian Gulf on the north and the Ocean on the south. For on the west it begins from Lower Egypt, and on the east it is bounded by the Red Sea. There are twenty-four peoples there.

And since we have described the southern part of all Asia, it remains to set out the part that is left, from the east to the north.

The Caucasus mountain first rises between the Colchians, who are above the Cimmerian Sea, and the Albanians, who are by the Caspian Sea. Its ridge seems to be one as far as the farthest east, but it has many names; and many would have this same ridge believed to be that of Mount Taurus, because the Parcohatras, a mountain of Armenia lying between Taurus and Caucasus, is in truth thought to join Taurus with Caucasus. But the river Euphrates shows that this is not so: pouring out from the root of Mount Parcohatras and making for the south, it keeps the Caucasus on its left and shuts off Taurus on its right. So between the Colchians and the Albanians, where it also has its gates, it is called the Caucasus mountain; from the Caspian Gates to the Armenian Gates, or to the source of the river Tigris, between Armenia and Hiberia, the mountains are called Acroceraunian; from the source of the Tigris to the city of Carrhae, between the Massagetae and the Parthians, it is Mount Ariobarzanes; from the city of Carrhae to the town of Cathippi, between the Hyrcanians and the Bactrians, it is Mount Memarmali, where amomum grows, and the next ridge from it is called Mount Parthau; from the town of Cathippi to the village of Safris, between the Dahae, the Sacaraucae and the Parthyenes, it is Mount Oscobares, where the river Ganges rises and laser grows; from the source of the river Ganges to the springs of the river Ottorogorra, which are in the north, where the mountain-dwelling Paropanisadae live, it is Mount Taurus; from the springs of the Ottorogorra to the city of Ottorogorra, between the Hunnic Scythians and the Gandaridae, it is Mount Caucasus. The last, between the Eoae and the Passyadrae, is Mount Imavus, where the river Chrysorhoas and the promontory of Samara are received by the Eastern Ocean. So from Mount Imavus, that is from the foot of the Caucasus and the right-hand part of the east where the Seric Ocean stretches, to the northern promontory and the northern river, and from there along the Scythian Sea, which is on the north, as far as the Caspian Sea, which is on the west, and as far as the outstretched ridge of the Caucasus, which is on the south, there are forty-two peoples of Hyrcanians and Scythians, wandering widely because of the barren expanse of the land.

The Caspian Sea rises from the Ocean in the region of the north wind, and both its shores near the Ocean are held to be deserted and uncultivated places. From there it stretches southwards through long straits, until, broadening over great spaces, it is bounded by the roots of the Caucasus mountain. So from the Caspian Sea, which is on the east, along the coast of the northern Ocean to the river Tanais and the Maeotic marshes, which are on the west, along the shore of the Cimmerian Sea, which is to the south-west, as far as the head and the gates of the Caucasus, which are on the south, there are thirty-four peoples. But generally the nearer region is called Albania, and the farther, below the Caspian Sea and mountain, the land of the Amazons.

The bounds of Asia have been set out as briefly as possible. Now I shall travel with my pen over Europe, as far as is granted to the knowledge of man.

It begins at the Riphaean mountains, the river Tanais and the Maeotic marshes, which are on the east, and runs along the shore of the northern Ocean to Gallia Belgica and the river Rhine, which comes down from the west; then as far as the Danube, which they also call the Hister, which is on the south and, directed towards the east, is received by the Pontus. On the east is Alania, in the middle Dacia, where Gothia is too, then Germany, where the Suebi hold the greatest part. Of all these there are fifty-four peoples.

Now I shall set out whatever the Danube shuts off from the barbarian lands as far as Our Sea.

Moesia has the mouths of the river Danube on the east, Thrace on the south-east, Macedonia on the south, Dalmatia on the south-west, Histria on the west, Pannonia on the north-west, and the Danube on the north.

Thrace has on the east the gulf of the Propontis and the city of Constantinople, which was formerly called Byzantium; on the north part of Dalmatia and a gulf of the Euxine Sea; on the west and south-west Macedonia; on the south the Aegean Sea.

Macedonia has the Aegean Sea on the east, Thrace on the north-east, Euboea and the Macedonian Gulf on the south-east, Achaea on the south, and on the west-south-west the Acroceraunian mountains at the narrows of the Adriatic Gulf, which mountains face Apulia and Brundisium; on the west Dalmatia, on the north-west Dardania, on the north Moesia.

Achaea is surrounded by sea almost on every side. For it has the Myrtoan Sea on the east, the Cretan Sea on the south-east, the Ionian Sea on the south, the islands of Cephallenia and Cassiope on the south-west and west, the Corinthian Gulf on the north, and on the north-east a narrow ridge of land by which it is joined to Macedonia, or rather to Attica. This place is called the Isthmus, where Corinth is, which has not far away in Attica, to the north, the city of Athens.

Dalmatia has Macedonia on the east, Dardania on the north-east, Moesia on the north, Histria, the Liburnian Gulf and the Liburnian islands on the west, and the Adriatic Gulf on the south.

Pannonia, Noricum and Raetia have Moesia on the east, Histria on the south, the Pennine Alps on the south-west, Gallia Belgica on the west, on the north-west the source of the Danube and the frontier that separates Germany from Gaul between the Danube and Gaul, and on the north the Danube and Germany.

Italy lies stretched from north-west to south-east, having the Tyrrhenian Sea on the south-west and the Adriatic Gulf on the north-east. The part of it that is shared with and joined to the continent is blocked by the barrier of the Alps. These, rising from the Gallic Sea above the Ligurian Gulf, shut off first the territory of the Narbonenses, then Gaul and Raetia, until they are fixed in the Liburnian Gulf.

Gallia Belgica has on the east the frontier of the river Rhine and Germany, on the south-east the Pennine Alps, on the south the province of Narbonensis, on the west the province of Lugdunensis, on the north-west the Britannic Ocean, and on the north the island of Britain.

Gallia Lugdunensis, drawn out long and bent narrowly, half encircles the province of Aquitania. It has Belgica on the east, and on the south part of the province of Narbonensis, where the city of Arelate lies and the river Rhône is received by the Gallic Sea.

The Narbonese province, part of the Gauls, has the Cottian Alps on the east, Spain on the west, Aquitania on the north-west, Lugdunensis on the north, Gallia Belgica on the north-east, and on the south the Gallic Sea, which is between Sardinia and the Balearic islands; and in front of it, where the river Rhône goes out into the sea, it has the Stoechades islands.

The province of Aquitania is bent into a circle by the slanting course of the river Liger, which is for the most part its boundary. It has on the north-west the Ocean that is called the Aquitanian Gulf, on the west the Spains, on the north and east Lugdunensis, and on the south-east and south it touches the province of Narbonensis.

Spain as a whole is three-cornered in its position, and by the flowing round of the Ocean and the Tyrrhenian Sea it is made almost an island. Its first corner, facing the east, hemmed in on the right by the province of Aquitania and on the left by the Balearic Sea, fits into the territory of the Narbonenses. The second corner points north-west, where Brigantia, a city of Gallaecia, raises a very high lighthouse, a work worth recording among few, as a watchtower towards Britain. Its third corner is where the islands of Gades, facing south-west, look out over a gulf of the Ocean between them towards Mount Atlas.

Hither Spain, beginning from the east, is drawn by the Pyrenean forests on the northern side as far as the Cantabrians and Asturians, and from there through the Vaccaei and the Oretani, whom it has on the west, it is bounded by Carthago, set on the shore of Our Sea.

Farther Spain has the Vaccaei, Celtiberians and Oretani on the east, the Ocean on the north, the Ocean on the west, and on the south the Gaditan strait of the Ocean, through which Our Sea, which is called Tyrrhenian, is let in.

And since the Ocean has islands, which they call Britain and Hibernia, lying on the far side of the Gauls, facing Spain, they will be briefly set out.

Britain, an island of the Ocean, stretches a long way to the north; on the south it has the Gauls. Its nearest shore is opened to those who cross by a city called the port of Rutupiae; from there it looks out, not far from the Morini, towards the Menapii and Batavi lying to the south. This island is eight hundred miles in length and two hundred in breadth.

Behind it, where it opens on the boundless Ocean, it has the Orcades islands, twenty of which are deserted and thirteen inhabited.

Then the island of Thule, which, separated from the others by a boundless distance, lying in the middle of the Ocean towards the north-west, is known to scarcely a few.

The island of Hibernia, lying between Britain and Spain, stretches a long way from south-west to north-east. Its nearer parts, facing the Cantabrian Ocean, look far off, across a wide interval, towards Brigantia, the city of Gallaecia, which faces it from the south-west to the north-west, above all from the promontory where the mouth of the river Scena is and where the Velabri and Luceni live. This island is nearer to Britain, narrower in extent of land, but more useful for the mildness of its climate and soil; it is inhabited by the peoples of the Scots.

Next to it is also the island of Mevania, itself of no small size, and of fertile soil. It too is inhabited by the peoples of the Scots.

These are the bounds of all Europe.

Africa, as I have said, when our ancestors described it as to be taken as the third part of the world, they followed not the measures of its extent but the reasons of the division. For this Great Sea, which rises from the Ocean in the west and inclines more to the south, has made the bounds of Africa narrower, hemmed in between it and the Ocean. Hence some too, understanding that it was equal in length but much narrower, thought it unfitting to call it a third part, and preferred rather to count Africa with Europe, that is to call it a portion of the second. Moreover, since much more land in Africa is uncultivated and unknown through the heat of the sun than in Europe through the rigour of the cold (for almost all living and growing things come more patiently and bearably to the extreme of cold than to the extreme of heat), that is the reason why Africa seems smaller in every way in extent and peoples: because by its nature it has less space, and through the harshness of its climate more desert. Its description by provinces and peoples is this:

Libya Cyrenaica and the Pentapolis are the first in the part of Africa after Egypt. It begins at the city of Paraetonium and the Catabathmos mountains, and from there stretches along the sea as far as the altars of the Philaeni. Behind it, as far as the southern Ocean, it has the peoples of the Libyo-Ethiopians and the Garamantes. It has Egypt on the east, the Libyan Sea on the north, on the west the Greater Syrtes and the Troglodytes (opposite whom is the island of Calypso), and on the south the Ethiopian Ocean.

The Tripolitan province, which is also called Subventana or the region of the Arzuges, where the city of Leptis Magna is (though the Arzuges are generally so called along the long frontier of Africa), has on the east the altars of the Philaeni between the Greater Syrtes and the Troglodytes; on the north the Sicilian, or rather the Adriatic, Sea and the Lesser Syrtes; on the west Byzacium as far as the Salt Lake; and on the south the barbarian Gaetuli, Nathabres and Garamantes, reaching as far as the Ethiopian Ocean.

Byzacium, Zeugis and Numidia. We find that Zeugis was formerly the general name not of one judicial district but of the whole province. Byzacium, then, where the city of Hadrumetum is, Zeugis, where Great Carthage is, and Numidia, where the cities of Hippo Regius and Rusiccade are, have on the east the Lesser Syrtes and the Salt Lake; on the north Our Sea, which looks towards the islands of Sicily and Sardinia; on the west Mauretania Sitifensis; and on the south the Uzara mountains and, beyond them, the peoples of the Ethiopians, roaming as far as the Ethiopian Ocean.

Mauretania Sitifensis and Caesariensis has Numidia on the east, Our Sea on the north, the river Malva on the west, and on the south Mount Astrixis, which divides the living land from the sands that lie as far as the Ocean, over which the Gangines Ethiopians roam.

Mauretania Tingitana is the last of Africa. It has the river Malva on the east; on the north Our Sea as far as the strait of Gades, which is narrowed between the two facing promontories of Abenna and Calpe; on the west Mount Atlas and the Atlantic Ocean; on the south-west Mount Hesperius; and on the south the peoples of the Autololes, whom they now call the Galaules, reaching as far as the Hesperian Ocean.

This is the boundary of all Africa. Now I shall measure the places, names and extents of the islands that are in Our Sea.

The island of Cyprus is surrounded on the east by the Syrian Sea, which they call the Gulf of Issus; on the west by the Pamphylian Sea; on the north by the Cilician strait; on the south by the sea of Syria and Phoenicia. Its extent is 175 miles in length and 125 miles in breadth.

The island of Crete is bounded on the east by the Carpathian Sea, on the west and north by the Cretan Sea, and on the south by the Libyan Sea, which they also call the Adriatic. It is 172 miles in length and 50 in breadth.

The Cyclades islands (of which the first on the east is Rhodes, on the north Tenedos, on the south Carpathos, on the west Cythera) are bounded on the east by the shores of Asia, on the west by the Icarian Sea, on the north by the Aegean Sea, on the south by the Carpathian Sea. All the Cyclades are fifty-four in number. They extend 500 miles from north to south and 200 miles from east to west.

The island of Sicily has three promontories. One, called Pelorus, looks to the north-east, and the city of Messana is next to it; the second, called Pachynus, below which is the city of Syracuse, looks to the south-east; the third, called Lilybaeum, where the city of the same name also lies, points to the west. It is 159 miles from Pelorus to Pachynus, and 187 from Pachynus to Lilybaeum. It is surrounded on the east by the Adriatic Sea, on the south by the African Sea, which is opposite the Subventani and the Lesser Syrtes; on the west and north it has the Tyrrhenian Sea; and from the north-east round to the east the Adriatic strait, which divides the Tauromenitani of Sicily from the Bruttii of Italy.

The islands of Sardinia and Corsica are divided by a small strait, that is, of twenty miles. Of these Sardinia has on the south, facing Numidia, the Caralitani, and facing the island of Corsica, that is towards the north, the Ulbienses. Its extent is 230 miles in length and 80 in breadth. It has on the east and north-east the Tyrrhenian Sea, which looks towards the port of the city of Rome; on the west the Sardinian Sea; on the south-west the Balearic islands, lying far off; on the south the Numidian Gulf; on the north, as I have said, Corsica.

The island of Corsica is angular with many promontories. It has on the east the Tyrrhenian Sea and the port of the City, on the south Sardinia, on the west the Balearic islands, on the north-west and north the Ligurian Gulf. It is 160 miles in length and 26 in breadth.

The Balearic islands are two, the greater and the lesser, with two towns each. The greater faces Tarraco, a city of Spain, and the lesser faces Barcino, towards the north. Below the greater lies the island of Ebusus. Then they look towards Sardinia on the east, the Gallic Sea on the north-east, the Mauretanian Sea on the south and south-west, and the Iberian Sea on the west.

These are the islands set through the whole Great Sea from the Hellespont to the Ocean that are held to be more famous for their cultivation and in memory.

I have reviewed briefly, as well as I could, the provinces and islands of the whole world. Now I shall set out, as far as I am able, the local miseries of each people, as they have arisen without ceasing from the beginning, and how and by whom they were stirred up.

## 1.3

When, after the making and adorning of this world, man, whom God had made upright and spotless, and so the human race, corrupted by its lusts, had grown filthy with sins, a just punishment at once followed an unjust licence. The sentence of God the creator and judge, laid down for sinning man, and for the earth because of man, and to last as long as men inhabit the earth, we all, unwilling though we may be, either prove by denying it or endure by confessing it; and their own weakness brands it as a witness on stubborn minds that Scripture has not faithfully persuaded. Next, the most truthful writers teach most plainly that when the sea was poured back over all the earth and the Flood let in, so that, with the whole world covered, there was one expanse of sky and sea, the whole human race was destroyed, a few being kept in the ark, by the merit of their faith, to replace its origin. That it happened has been attested even by those who, though they did not know past times nor the very author of time, nevertheless learned it by inference, from the evidence and the conjecture of the stones that we often see on remote mountains rough with shells and oysters, and often hollowed out by water. And although more things of this kind, both worth telling and of certain credit, could still be brought forward by us, let these two things, as if the chief ones, suffice to be said: of the transgression of the first man and the condemnation of his offspring and his life, and then of the destruction of the whole human race. Only so far, that if the gentile historians have touched on any of our matters in some order, these may be brought forward more fully together with the rest, in the very order in which they occur.

## 1.4

In the 1,300th year before the founding of the city Ninus, king of the Assyrians, the 'first' as they would have it, out of lust for extending his dominion, carried arms abroad and spent a bloody life of fifty years in wars throughout all Asia. Rising from the south and from the Red Sea, under the farthest north he subdued the Euxine Sea by laying it waste, and taught the Scythian barbarians, then still unwarlike and harmless, to rouse their sleeping savagery, to know their own strength, and to drink no longer the milk of cattle but the blood of men, and at last to conquer while being conquered. Last of all he crushed in battle and killed Zoroaster, king of the Bactrians and, they say, the inventor of the art of magic. Afterwards he himself, while he was attacking a city that had revolted from him, was struck by an arrow and died. When he was dead his wife Semiramis succeeded him, having a man's spirit and the dress of her son, and for forty-two years she exercised her peoples, already greedy from the practice of blood, in the slaughter of nations. Not content with the boundaries that she had received, acquired by her husband in fifty years when he was the only warrior then, the woman added to her empire Ethiopia, pressed by war and smeared with blood. She also made war on the Indians, whom no one but she and Alexander the Great ever entered. To pursue and slaughter peoples living in peace was crueller and heavier at that time than it is now for this reason: that among them then there were neither any fires of war abroad nor such great exercise of greed at home. This woman, burning with lust and thirsting for blood, amid unceasing debauchery and murders, used to kill all those whom she had summoned like a queen and kept like a harlot when she had enjoyed their embraces; and at last, having shamefully conceived a son, impiously exposed him and incestuously known him, she covered her private disgrace with a public crime. For she ordered that between parents and children, with no respect paid to nature, it should be free for each to seek marriage as they pleased.

## 1.5

In the 1,160th year before the founding of the city the region bordering Arabia, which was then called the Pentapolis, was utterly burned by fire from heaven, as Cornelius Tacitus too records among others. He speaks thus: "Not far from there are plains which they say were once fertile and inhabited by great cities, and were burned by the stroke of lightning; but that traces remain, and that the earth itself, solid in appearance, has lost its power to bear fruit." And though in this place, as if ignorant, he said nothing of the cities burned for the sins of men, a little later, as if forgetting his intention, he adds and says: "Just as I would grant that once famous cities blazed with fire from heaven, so I think that the earth is infected and corrupted by the exhalation of the lake." By saying this, though unwillingly, he openly confessed that he both knew and granted the burning of the cities, which without doubt were consumed by the guilt of their sins, and he plainly showed that it was not knowledge of the truth that he lacked but the will to express it. This will now be set out by me more fully.

On the border of Arabia and Palestine, where the mountains sent down on either side are received by the plains below them, there were five cities: Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim and Zoar. Of these Zoar was small, the others large and great, for they had both a fertile soil beneath them and the river Jordan, spread over the plains and divided in convenient places, spent itself in increasing their richness. For this whole region, which used its goods ill, the abundance of things was the cause of its evils. For out of abundance grew luxury, and out of luxury foul lusts, so that males, working shame with males, rushed headlong without regard even to places, conditions or ages. So God in His anger rained fire and brimstone upon this land, and condemned the whole region, burned up with its peoples and cities, to eternal destruction, to be a witness of His judgement in time to come; so that even now the shape of the region indeed appears, but the region is found to be of ashes, and the sea poured over it now covers the middle valley that the Jordan had watered. And so much divine indignation was kindled over things that are thought small, that because those people, using their goods ill, had made the fruits of His mercies into the food of their lusts, the very earth too that had held these cities, first burned by fire and then overwhelmed by water, perished from the common sight into eternal damnation.

## 1.6

So now, if they please, let those who, as far as in them lies, spit at Christ, whom we have shown to be the judge of the ages, distinguish between the causes of Sodom and of Rome and compare the punishments. These I need not go over again, chiefly for this reason, that they are known to all. And yet how gladly I would accept their opinions, if they would confess faithfully what they feel. Though, since few murmur about the Christian times, and that in corners, I should not think it something to take so very hard, since the feeling and the speech of the whole Roman people has been made known with one voice and an equal judgement. It testified beyond all doubt that its habit of pleasures had hesitated only a little, through a small and slight disturbance, for it cried out freely: "If it got back the circus, nothing had happened to it"; that is, the swords of the Goths had done nothing at Rome if the Romans were allowed to watch the circus games. Unless perhaps, as is the way with very many at this time above all, who after long rest think even a small worry that arises an intolerable labour, they put these most merciful warnings, by which we are all at some time touched, above the punishments of others that they have heard and read. Them at least I warn about this very end of the Sodomites and Gomorrhaeans, so that they may learn and understand how God has punished sinners, how He can punish, and how He will punish.

## 1.7

In the 1,070th year before the founding of the city the Telchines and the Caryatii waged a stubborn battle against Phoroneus, king of the Argives, and the Parrhasians, with doubtful hope and without the fruit of victory. And the same Telchines a little later, defeated in war, fleeing from their country and knowing nothing of the world, believing that they were withdrawing themselves entirely from contact with all human habitation, took the island of Rhodes, which was formerly called Ophiussa, as if it were a safe possession.

In the 1,040th year before the founding of the city there was in Achaea a savage flood, with very great devastation of almost the whole province. Because it was poured out in the times of Ogyges, who was then the founder and king of Eleusis, it gave its name to the place and the time.

## 1.8

In the 1,008th year before the founding of the city there was in Egypt first an unusual and wearisome plenty, and then a continuous and intolerable famine, which Joseph, a just and wise man, relieved by divine foresight. So the historian Pompeius and his abridger Justin teach, who among other things says: "Joseph was the youngest of his brothers. His brothers, fearing his outstanding talent, seized him and sold him to foreign merchants. Carried off by them to Egypt, when he had there learned the magic arts by his clever wit, he was soon very dear to the king himself. For he was most skilled in prodigies and was the first to found the understanding of dreams, and nothing of divine or human law seemed unknown to him; so much so that, foreseeing a barrenness of the fields many years ahead, he had gathered the harvests; and such were the proofs of his skill that his answers seemed to come not from a man but from a god. Joseph's son was Moses, whom, besides the inheritance of his father's knowledge, the beauty of his form also commended. But when the Egyptians were suffering from scab and leprosy, warned by an oracle, they drove him out of the borders of Egypt together with the sick, so that the plague should not creep to more." So Justin. But since this same Moses, whom these writers attest was wise and learned, has written of these things more fully and truly, as done by himself and his people, the ignorance of these writers must first be supplied by his credit and authority, which even they approve; and then the deceitful malice of the Egyptian priests must be refuted. They either, by cunning, which is more evident, tried to withdraw from memory the plain anger and mercy of the true God, by a confused account given piecemeal, so that they should not show, to the shame of their idols, that He was rightly to be worshipped by whose counsel, they would have taught, these evils were announced and by whose help they were avoided; or perhaps, to take it more indulgently, they forgot. For by the foresight of our Joseph, who was a servant of the true God and was piously and earnestly concerned for the creation of his Lord, they had abundance of grain, as priests; but, because they were false priests, they did not grieve with the others who were hungry. For truly, the one who is pleased forgets; the one who is grieved remembers. Yet though the histories and annals are silent, the land of Egypt itself declares itself a witness to the events of this time. Brought then under the king's power and restored to its cultivators, it pays to this day, from all its produce, an unceasing tax of a fifth part. So this great famine was under a king of the Egyptians of Diospolis named Amosis, at the time when Baleus ruled the Assyrians and Apis the Argives. Before the seven years of famine there were seven other years of plenty before them. Their abundance, which would perish as carelessly as it had been born richly, our Joseph by his skill gathered and stored, and he preserved all Egypt. He acquired all the money for Pharaoh and the glory for God, rendering with most just stewardship tribute to whom tribute, and honour to whom honour. He gathered the cattle, the lands and the property of all; and those who had sold themselves with their lands for the rate of the dole they received, he released on the agreed terms of a fifth part. Who would believe that this Joseph, whom God appointed for the Egyptians as the author of their preserved safety, so quickly fell out of their memory that a little later they bound his sons and his whole kindred to slavery, afflicted them with labours and struck them down with slaughter? Therefore it is no wonder if now too some are found who, when they have turned aside the sword hanging over their necks by holding out the Christian name, either disown or defame the very name of Christ, by which alone they are saved, and claim that they are burdened by the times of those by whose merits they are set free.

## 1.9

In the 810th year before the founding of the city Amphictyon reigned at Athens, third from Cecrops. In his times a deluge of waters swept away the greater part of the peoples of Thessaly, a few being saved by refuges in the mountains, above all on Mount Parnassus. In its neighbourhood Deucalion then held the throne; he received those who fled to him on rafts and cherished and fed them along the twin ridges of Parnassus; and so they say that the human race was restored by him. Then too Plato is witness that very many plagues and dreadful diseases raged in Ethiopia, almost to the point of desolation. And lest it be thought that the times of God's anger and of the fury of war were separate, at that season Father Liber subdued India, soaked it with blood, filled it with slaughter and polluted it with lusts: a people, certainly, never subject to any man, content only with its native quiet.

## 1.10

In the 805th year before the founding of the city unspeakable evils and intolerable plagues fell upon the Egyptians, as Pompeius and Cornelius attest. Since both of them set out to tell this of the Jews, they have troubled me somewhat by their disagreement. For Pompeius, or Justin, speaks in this way: "When the Egyptians were suffering from scab and leprosy, warned by an oracle, they drove Moses out of the borders of Egypt with the sick, so that the plague should not creep to more. So, made leader of the exiles, he carried off by theft the sacred objects of the Egyptians; and the Egyptians, trying to recover them by arms, were forced by storms to return home." But Cornelius speaks of the same matter thus: "Most writers agree that when a wasting disease arose throughout Egypt, which disfigured the body, King Bocchoris, approaching the oracle of Ammon and asking for a remedy, was ordered to purge his kingdom and to carry off that race of men into other lands as hateful to the gods. When the crowd, sought out and gathered in this way, had been left in waste places, and the rest were benumbed with tears, Moses, one of the exiles, warned them to look for no help from gods or men, but to trust themselves to a heavenly leader, by whose help they had first driven off their present miseries." So Cornelius says that the Jews were driven out into the deserts under compulsion from the Egyptians themselves, and afterwards adds carelessly that they had driven off their miseries in Egypt by the help of their leader Moses. By this it is shown that some things that were vigorously done by Moses have been concealed. Likewise Justin asserts that Moses, driven out together with the people, stole the sacred objects of the Egyptians, and that the Egyptians, trying to recover them by arms, were forced back by storms and returned home. And he too has revealed something more, though not the whole, of what the other concealed. Therefore, since both have borne witness to that great leader Moses, let the events be set out from him, as they were done and spoken through him.

When the Egyptians were tormenting the people of God, that is the race of Joseph, by whose help they had been saved, crushed with slavery and labour, and were moreover compelling them by a cruel order to kill their own offspring, God commanded through Moses, His messenger, that His people be let go free to serve Him. When He was scorned He drove the stubborn with the harshest punishments, until, loaded and crushed by ten plagues, they at last forced those whom they had not wished to let go even to hurry. After the waters were turned into blood, bringing to those burning with thirst remedies heavier than the punishments; after the horrid filth of frogs creeping over all things clean and unclean; after the fiery gnats, which could be escaped nowhere as the whole air quivered; after the dog-flies, running about with horrid motions even through the inner parts of the limbs and bitterly inflicting torments as grievous as they were foul; after the sudden ruin and general slaughter of all the cattle and beasts of burden; after the boils bursting out and the running sores and, as they preferred to call it, the 'scab and leprosy' breaking out over all their bodies; after hail mixed with fire, trampling down men, herds and trees everywhere; after the clouds of locusts, which, when everything had been consumed, pursued even the very roots of the seeds; after darkness dreadful with phantoms, palpable in its thickness, deathly in its length; at last after the death, alike throughout all Egypt, of the first-born, and an equal storm of bereavement over all, those who had not yielded to God when He commanded yielded to Him when He punished. But soon, with the worst repentance, they dared to pursue those they had let go, and paid the last penalty of their wicked obstinacy. For their king drove the whole army of Egypt, furnished with chariots and horsemen, against the wanderers; we can guess its number chiefly from this one proof, that six hundred thousand men feared it and fled. But God, the protector of the oppressed and the avenger of the stubborn, suddenly divided the Red Sea, and, broadening the edges on each side, held up the sides of the stiffened waves, raised like a mountain, so that, drawn on by the hope of an unobstructed path, the pious entered a way of salvation they had despaired of, and the impious a pit of death they had not expected. So, while the Hebrews walked safely over the dry ground, the masses of standing water poured back behind them, and the whole multitude of Egypt was overwhelmed and killed with its king; and the whole province, already tormented before by the plagues, was emptied by this last slaughter. There still exist the most certain monuments of these deeds. For the tracks of the chariots and the ruts of the wheels are seen not only on the shore but also in the deep, as far as the sight can reach; and if they are disturbed for a time, either by chance or by curiosity, they are at once restored by divine power to their former appearance by the winds and waves: so that whoever is not taught the fear of God by zeal for the religion made known may be terrified by the example of the vengeance He has taken.

In these times too a heat so continuous and heavy blazed up that the sun, carried along paths out of its way, is said not to have warmed the whole world with heat but to have scorched it with fire; and the Ethiopian could not bear the fierce heat pressed on him, more than usual, nor the Scythian, unused to it. From this some, while they do not grant God His unspeakable power, searching for empty little reasons, have woven the ridiculous fable of Phaethon.

## 1.11

Likewise in the 775th year before the founding of the city fifty parricides were committed in one night among the sons of the brothers Danaus and Aegyptus. Then Danaus himself, the contriver of such great crimes, driven from the kingdom he had acquired by so many outrages, withdrew to Argos. There, after the Argives had been shamefully persuaded into the crime, he drove from the throne Sthenelas, who had received him as an exile in need, and reigned himself. Then too there was in Egypt the cruel hospitality, and crueller religion, of Busiris, a most bloodthirsty tyrant, who used to offer the blood of innocent guests to the gods who shared in his crimes: a thing without doubt execrable to men, though I should like to see whether it seemed execrable to the gods themselves too. Then too parricide was joined to the incest of Tereus, Procne and Philomela, and a feast of unspeakable food more execrable than either was added, when, because of the stolen chastity and cut-out tongue of her sister, the mother killed her little son and the father ate him. In the same times Perseus crossed from Greece into Asia, and there tamed barbarian peoples in a heavy and long war, and at last, as victor, gave his name to the subject people: for from Perseus they were called Persians.

## 1.12

But I am now forced to confess that, for the advantage of keeping the end in sight, I am passing over very many things out of such a throng of the world's evils and shortening everything. For I could never pass through so dense a wood unless I sometimes flew over it with frequent leaps as well. For since the kingdom of the Assyrians lasted 1,160 years, through almost fifty kings, to Sardanapalus, and until that time almost never rested from making or receiving wars, what end will be found if we try to recount them by numbering them, not to say by describing them? Above all since the matters of the Greeks must not be passed over, and those of the Romans must above all be reviewed. Nor is there any need for me now to enumerate the shameful deeds of Tantalus and Pelops and the more shameful fables. Of these, Tantalus, king of the Phrygians, when he had most shamefully carried off Ganymede, the son of Tros, king of the Dardanians, kept him with a greater foulness of the struggle that followed, as the poet Phanocles confirms, who records that a very great war was stirred up over this; or else because he would have this same Tantalus, as an attendant of the gods, be thought to have prepared the stolen boy for the lust of Jupiter by a household pandering, the man who did not hesitate to set his own son Pelops too before Jupiter's feast. It is wearisome too to tell of the contests of Pelops himself against Dardanus and the Trojans, however great, which, because they are commonly celebrated in fables, are listened to rather carelessly. I pass over those things too which are told, in Palaephatus's writing, of Perseus, Cadmus, the Thebans and the Spartans, through the inextricable recurrences of alternating evils. I keep silent about the outrages of the Lemnian women; I pass by the lamentable flight of Pandion, king of the Athenians; I say nothing of the hatreds, debaucheries and parricides of Atreus and Thyestes, hateful even to heaven. I leave out Oedipus, the killer of his father, husband of his mother, brother of his sons, stepfather to himself. I prefer to keep silent that Eteocles and Polynices laboured in mutual combat so that neither of them should fail to be a parricide. I do not wish to recall Medea, "wounded by cruel love" and rejoicing in the slaughter of her little children, and whatever was done in those times. It can be guessed how men endured what even the stars are said to have fled from.

## 1.13

In the 560th year before the founding of the city there was a most savage contest between the Cretans and the Athenians, in which, after the peoples on both sides had been unhappily struck down, the Cretans exercised a bloodier victory. They cruelly assigned the sons of the noble Athenians to be devoured by the Minotaur, whether I should more fitly call it a savage man or a human beast I do not know, and fattened that shapeless monster on the plucked-out lights of Greece. In the same days the Lapiths and the Thessalians fought in all too famous conflicts. But Palaephatus, in the first book of his Incredible Things, reveals that the Thessalians themselves were believed by the Lapiths to be, and were called, Centaurs, because their horsemen, running about in war, seemed like one body of horses and men.

## 1.14

In the 480th year before the founding of the city Vesozes, king of Egypt, eager either to mix in war or to join in a kingdom the south and the north, regions separated by almost the whole sky and sea, was the first to declare war on the Scythians, having first sent envoys to tell the enemy the terms of obedience. To these the Scythians replied to the envoys that a very wealthy king had foolishly taken up war against the poor, a war that he himself should rather have feared, the other way round, because of the uncertain outcome of war, with no rewards and plain losses. Further, they said they would not wait to be come to, but would go of their own accord to meet their booty. Nor was there any delay, for deeds followed the words. First they forced Vesozes himself, terrified, to flee back into his kingdom, and then they attacked the army he had abandoned and seized all the equipment of war; and they would have laid waste all Egypt too had they not been driven back, hindered by the marshes. Then, returning at once, they subdued Asia with endless slaughter and made it tributary. There they stayed fifteen years without peace, until at last they were called back by the insistence of their wives, who declared that unless they returned they would seek offspring from their neighbours.

## 1.15

Meanwhile among the Scythians two young men of the royal house, Plynos and Scolopetius, driven from home by a faction of the nobles, drew a great body of youth with them and settled on the coast of Pontic Cappadocia near the river Thermodon, with the Themiscyrian plains subject to them. There, after long plundering all that was nearest, they were massacred by an ambush through a conspiracy of their neighbours. Their wives, stirred by exile and widowhood, took up arms; and so that all might have an equal spirit from a like condition, they killed the men who had survived, and, inflamed against the enemy with their own blood, they achieved vengeance for their slain husbands by the destruction of their neighbours. Then, having won peace by arms, they took foreign men to their beds. The males that were born they soon killed; the females they brought up with care, burning off the right breasts of the infants so that the shooting of arrows should not be hindered; hence they were called Amazons. They had two queens, Marpesia and Lampeto, who divided their host into two parts and took by lot in turn the care of war and the guarding of home. So when they had subdued Europe for the most part, and had taken a good many cities of Asia, and had themselves founded Ephesus and other cities, they called home the chief part of their army, loaded with very rich booty; the rest, left to protect the rule of Asia, were massacred with Queen Marpesia by an onset of the enemy. Her place was taken by her daughter Sinope, who crowned a singular glory of valour with perpetual virginity. So great an admiration and dread had seized the peoples, roused by this fame, that Hercules too, when he had been ordered by his master to bring the queen's arms, as if destined to an inescapable danger, gathered all the chosen and noble youth of Greece and prepared nine long ships; and yet, not content with this swarm of strength, he preferred to attack unexpectedly and to surround them when they did not expect it. Two sisters were then at the head of the kingdom, Antiope and Orithyia. Hercules, carried by sea, overwhelmed them while they were off their guard and unarmed and idle through the carelessness of peace. Among the very many killed and captured, two sisters of Antiope were held, Melanippe by Hercules and Hippolyte by Theseus. But Theseus took Hippolyte in marriage, while Hercules returned Melanippe to her sister and took the queen's arms as the price of her ransom. After Orithyia, Penthesilea gained the throne, of whose valour among men in the Trojan War we have received the most famous proofs.

## 1.16

Oh, the pity of it, the shame of human error! Women who were fugitives from their country entered, roamed through and laid waste Europe and Asia, that is the greatest and strongest parts of the world, and held them for almost a hundred years, overthrowing very many cities and founding others: and yet it was not counted as a misery of men in the pressure of the times. But now the famous Getae, who are now the Goths too, whom Alexander declared to be avoided, whom Pyrrhus dreaded, whom Caesar too turned aside from, having left and emptied their own homes, entering the Roman provinces all together with all their strength, and long shown off together for terror, hope by entreaty for the alliance of a Roman treaty, which they could have claimed by arms. They ask for a seat of small habitation, not by their own choice but by our judgement, though the whole earth lay subject and open to them and it was free to them to take as much as they pleased. They offer themselves for the protection of the Roman realm, they whom alone unconquered kingdoms feared. And yet blind heathendom, since it does not see these things done by Roman valour, does not believe that they were obtained by the faith of the Romans, nor does it acquiesce, though it understands it, to confess that by the kindness of the Christian religion, which joins a kindred faith through all peoples, those men have been made subject to it without battle whose women destroyed the greater part of the earth with immense slaughter.

## 1.17

Then, in the 430th year before the founding of the city, there is told the rape of Helen, the conspiracy of the Greeks and the gathering of a thousand ships, then the ten-year siege and at last the famous destruction of Troy. In that war, waged most bloodily for ten years, what nations and how great peoples the same whirlwind caught up and struck down, Homer, a poet famous above all, has made plain in a most splendid poem; nor is it our business now to go over it again in order, for it seems both long for this work and known to all. Yet let those who have learned of the length of that siege, the cruelty of the overthrow, the slaughter and the captivity, see whether they are rightly offended at the present state of the times, whatever it is: when enemies whom, by the hidden mercy of God, they might have to pursue in war over all lands with armies drawn up, they follow over all seas for the sake of peace, holding out hostages; and so that they should not be thought to do this, perhaps, out of love of quiet, these offer themselves and their own dangers for the peace of the Romans against other peoples.

## 1.18

Then, after a few years had passed, what arms the arrival in Italy of Aeneas, a fugitive from Troy, set in motion, what wars it stirred up for three years, how many peoples it entangled and struck down with hatred and destruction, has been branded on our memory too by the teaching of the elementary school. Moreover, in the middle of these times lie the exiles and shipwrecks of the Greeks, the disasters of the Peloponnesians broken at the death of Codrus, the unknown Thracians rising in new wars, and a general upheaval then through all Asia and Greece.

## 1.19

In the 64th year before the founding of the city Sardanapalus reigned last among the Assyrians, a man more corrupt than a woman. Seen among herds of harlots in a woman's dress, handling purple on the distaff, by his prefect Arbatus, who was then over the Medes, he was held in execration; soon he was also challenged to war when the peoples of the Medes were roused, and when he was defeated he threw himself on a burning pyre. From then the kingdom of the Assyrians passed to the Medes. Then, with many battles springing up on every side, which it seems in no way fitting to discuss in order, through various fortunes it passed to the Scythians and Chaldaeans and returned again by a like road to the Medes. In this brevity it must be weighed how great were the ruins and disasters of peoples, how many wars flowed, where so many and such kingdoms were so often changed. After this Phraortes ruled the Medes, who spent the twenty-two years of his reign in very frequent wars with the Assyrians and the Persians. After him reigned Diocles, a man most experienced in arms and always busy with wars, who at his death gave the empire, widely increased, to Astyages. Astyages, having no male offspring, had a grandson, Cyrus, born among the Persians. But Cyrus, as soon as he grew up, gathered a band of Persians and declared war on his grandfather. Astyages, forgetting the crime he had once committed against Harpalus, when he killed his only son, a little child, and set him before the father to feast on, and, so that no happy ignorance should take anything from that most unhappy bereavement, reproached the father with the infamous feast by showing him the head and hands: forgetful, then, of this deed, he entrusted the chief command of the war to Harpalus himself, who at once handed over the army he had received to Cyrus by treachery. When he learned this Astyages, taking forces with him, set out himself against the Persians and renewed the contest more fiercely, with a threat to his men that if anyone tried to withdraw from the battle he would be met with the sword. Since the Medes fought hard under this necessity, the line of the Persians was again driven back; and as it was gradually giving way, their mothers and wives ran to meet them and begged them to return to the battle. When they hesitated, the women lifted their clothes and showed the shameful parts of the body, asking whether they wished to flee back into the wombs of their mothers or wives. Blushing at this, they returned to the battle, and making a charge they forced those they had been fleeing to flee. Then Astyages was captured there; Cyrus took nothing from him but his kingdom, and put him over the very great people of the Hyrcanians. He himself did not wish to return to the Medes. This was the end of the empire of the Medes. But the cities that had been tributary to the Medes revolted from Cyrus, and this was the cause and origin of many wars for Cyrus.

## 1.20

At that time Phalaris of Sicily, having seized a tyranny, was ravaging the people of Agrigentum. Cruel in mind, crueller in his devices, doing everything wickedly against the innocent, the unjust man once found someone whom he punished justly. For a certain Perillus, a worker in bronze, seeking the tyrant's friendship and thinking it a gift fit for his cruelty, made a bronze bull, in whose side he skilfully set a door to serve as a way in for thrusting the condemned inside: so that when someone shut in there was roasted with fires set beneath, the hollow bronze, by its capacity, would increase the sound of the voice forced from him and, with a deathly beat, would give out a murmur suited to the image, and in this wicked spectacle it would seem the lowing of a beast and not the groaning of a man. But Phalaris, embracing the deed and cursing its maker, provided matter both for vengeance and for cruelty: for he punished the craftsman himself with his own invention.

There had also been, a little earlier, a king among the Latins, Aremulus, who for eighteen years grew in outrages and impieties, and at last, cut off by a thunderbolt by divine judgement, paid a ripe punishment at an unripe age.

Let the Latins and the Sicilians now choose, if they please, whether they would rather have lived in the days of Aremulus and Phalaris, who wrung the lives of the innocent out of them with punishments, or in these Christian times, when the Roman emperors, ordered above all by religion itself, after crushing tyrannies for the good of the state do not exact vengeance even for the wrongs done to themselves by the tyrants.

## 1.21

In the 30th year before the founding of the city a very great war was waged between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians with all their strength and spirit, in which they were driven by mutual slaughter to this point: that they withdrew from one another as if defeated and abandoned the war. Then too a sudden invasion of Asia by the people of the Amazons and the Cimmerians caused very great devastation and slaughter, for a long time and far and wide.

In the 20th year before the founding of the city the Lacedaemonians, warring against the Messenians with untiring fury for twenty years because their maidens had been slighted at a solemn sacrifice of the Messenians, entangled the whole strength of Greece in their own ruin. They had devoted themselves with great curses and bound themselves with oaths never to return home unless Messene were taken by storm; and after ten years, worn out by the long siege and yet having gained no fruit of victory, they were being called back, moved besides by the complaints of their wives protesting at their long widowhood and the danger of barrenness. Holding a council, they feared that, with the hope of offspring cut off, this perseverance was breeding destruction for themselves more than for the Messenians. So they sent back to Sparta those chosen men in the army who had come as reinforcements after the oath was sworn, and allowed them to lie with all the women indiscriminately: a licence infamous enough, and yet not useful. They themselves, holding to their purpose, took the Messenians by a trick, and pressed the conquered into slavery. But the Messenians, after long enduring a bloody domination among blows and chains, shook off the yoke, took up arms and renewed the war. The Lacedaemonians chose Tyrtaeus, an Athenian poet, as their leader in battle. Routed in three encounters, they made good their lost army by calling a band of slaves to freedom. But when even so they thought that they should give up the contest for fear of danger, they were fired again by a poem composed by Tyrtaeus, their poet and general, and recited before the assembly, and they soon rushed into battle; and they met with such force of spirit that rarely has a bloodier battle ever blazed up. In the end, however, the victory was the Lacedaemonians'. A third time the Messenians renewed the contest. Nor did the Lacedaemonians delay. Both sides brought many forces as auxiliaries. The Athenians, for their part, prepared to attack the Lacedaemonians from the other side while they were busy elsewhere. Nor were the Lacedaemonians idle: for, being occupied with the Messenians themselves, they sent in the Peloponnesians to meet the Athenians in battle. The Athenians, having sent a small fleet to Egypt, were unequal in strength and were easily beaten in a naval encounter. Then, when their fleet had been recovered and they had been strengthened too by a force of soldiers, they challenged the victors to battle. The Lacedaemonians too left the Messenians and turned their arms against the Athenians. For a long time there were varied and heavy fights and a doubtful state of victory, and at last both sides withdrew with the outcome undecided. It must above all be known, however, that Sparta is the same city as Lacedaemon, and that from this the Lacedaemonians are called Spartans. So the Lacedaemonians, called back from there to the Messenian war, so as not to leave the time between idle for the Athenians, made a pact with the Thebans that they would restore to them the leadership of the Boeotians, which they had lost in the times of the Persian War, if they would take up the war with the Athenians. So great was the fury of the Spartans that, though entangled in two wars, they did not refuse to take up a third, provided that they could find enemies for their foes. The Athenians, disturbed by so great a storm of wars, chose two generals: Pericles, a man of proven valour, and Sophocles, the writer of tragedies. They divided the army, laid waste the territory of the Spartans far and wide, and added many cities of Asia to the empire of the Athenians. From then on, for fifty years, they fought on land and sea with victory always uncertain, until the Spartans, with their wealth diminished and their good faith ruined, became a disgrace even to their allies.

But that these things weighed on Greece through so many volumes of ages is thought of little account. Now, however, it is not borne that pleasures should sometimes be interrupted and lusts hindered for a little. Yet there is this difference between the men of that time and those of this one: that they bore these unbearable things with an even mind, because they had been born or brought up in them and had known nothing better; while these, accustomed all their lives to an unbroken clear sky of calm and delights, are disturbed by every cloud of worry, however slight, that gathers. And would that they would pray to Him who drives away even this slight disquiet, by whose gift they have had this continuity of peace unknown in other times. And since I remember that I promised, when I marked out the order of my telling as if by certain joints, to speak from the founding of the world to the founding of the city, let this be the end of this volume, which we have unfolded from the founding of the world, so that the following book may begin from the founding of the city. It will contain the evils of those times, more closely woven, since men were by then better practised and better schooled in wickedness.

## 2.1

I think there is no longer any man from whom it can be hidden that God made man in this world. Hence too, when man sins, the world is reproved, and to restrain our intemperance this earth on which we live is chastised by the failing of the other animals and the barrenness of its fruits. So if we are God's creatures, we are rightly God's charge too. For who loves more than He who made? And who rules in better order than He who both made and loves? And who can order and rule what is made more wisely and more strongly than He who both foresaw what was to be made and accomplished what He foresaw? Therefore those who have not read perceive, and those who have read recognise, that all power and every ordering is from God. And if powers are from God, how much more are kingdoms, from which the other powers proceed; and if kingdoms are various, how much more fittingly is there some greatest kingdom, to which the whole power of the other kingdoms is subject: such as was in the beginning the Babylonian, and then the Macedonian, afterwards too the African, and at the end the Roman, which remains to this day. And by the same unspeakable ordering, at the four cardinal points of the world, the leaderships of four kingdoms stand out in distinct degrees: the Babylonian kingdom in the east, the Carthaginian in the south, the Macedonian in the north and the Roman in the west. Between the first and the last of these, that is between the Babylonian and the Roman, as if between an old father and a little son, the African and the Macedonian came in, brief and in the middle, as if guardian and trustee, admitted by the power of the time and not by the right of inheritance. Whether this is so, I shall take care to set out most plainly.

## 2.2

The first king among the Assyrians who was able to stand out above the rest was Ninus. When Ninus was killed his wife Semiramis, queen of all Asia, restored the city of Babylon and made it the capital of the kingdom for the Assyrians. The kingdom of the Assyrians stood a long time with its power unshaken; but when Arbatus, whom others call Arbaces, prefect of the Medes and a Mede by nation, had killed his king Sardanapalus at Babylon, he transferred the name and the sum of the kingdom to the Medes. So the kingdom of Ninus and of Babylon was diverted to the Medes in the very year in which Procas began to reign among the Latins, the father of Amulius and Numitor and the grandfather of Rhea Silvia, who was the mother of Romulus. And so that I may show thoroughly that all these things were arranged by unspeakable mysteries and by the deepest judgements of God, and did not happen either by human strength or by uncertain chance: all ancient histories begin from Ninus, and all Roman histories arise from Procas. Then, from the first year of the rule of Ninus until Babylon began to be restored by Semiramis, sixty-four years passed; and from the first year of Procas, when he began to reign, to the founding of the city by Romulus, there were likewise sixty-four years. So in the reign of Procas the seed of the future Rome was sown, though no shoot yet appeared. In the same year of the reign of this Procas the kingdom of Babylon failed, though Babylon itself still stood. When Arbatus left for the Medes, the Chaldaeans kept part of the kingdom in their own hands and claimed Babylon for themselves against the Medes. So the power over Babylonia was with the Medes, the ownership with the Chaldaeans; but the Chaldaeans, because of the ancient dignity of the royal city, preferred to call themselves its people rather than call it theirs. So it came about that Nebuchadnezzar and the other kings after him down to Cyrus, though they are read of as powerful with the strength of the Chaldaeans and famous with the name of Babylonia, are nevertheless not counted in the number and at the turning point of the illustrious kings. So Babylon was dishonoured under the prefect Arbatus in the same year in which Rome, to speak properly, was sown under King Procas. Babylon was at last overthrown by King Cyrus at the very time when Rome was first freed from the domination of the Tarquin kings. For in one and the same correspondence of times the one fell and the other rose: the one then first suffered the domination of foreigners, the other then first scorned even the arrogance of its own rulers; the one then, as if dying, left its inheritance, and the other, coming of age, then recognised itself as heir; then the empire of the east set and that of the west rose.

And not to delay longer with words, I commit myself to the teeth of the raving, but to be set free by the protection of the truth.

## 2.3

Ninus reigned fifty-two years. He was succeeded, as I have said, by his wife Semiramis, who herself reigned forty-two years and in the middle of her rule founded Babylon as the capital of the kingdom. So Babylon, about 1,164 years after it was founded, was despoiled of its wealth and its kingdom, and deprived of its king himself, by the Medes and by Arbatus, their king but its own prefect; yet it remained unharmed itself for some while afterwards. Likewise Rome too, after the same number of years, that is about 1,164, was broken into by the Goths and Alaric their king, though its own count, and despoiled of its wealth, not of its rule; it still remains and reigns unharmed. Yet the order of the whole correspondence between the two cities has been kept by hidden decrees to such a degree that there its prefect Arbatus seized the kingdom, and here its prefect Attalus tried to reign: though in this city alone the profane attempt was made void by the merit of a Christian emperor.

So I have thought these things should be recalled above all for this reason: that, with so much of the unspeakable judgements of God laid open in part, those who murmur so foolishly about the Christian times may understand that one God has ordered the times, both for the Babylonians in the beginning and for the Romans at the end; that it is of His mercy that we live, and of our own intemperance that we live wretchedly. See, the rise of Babylon and of Rome is alike, the power alike, the greatness alike, the times alike, the goods alike, the evils alike; and yet the end is not alike, nor the failing. For the one lost its kingdom, the other keeps it; the one was bereaved by the killing of its king, the other is secure with its emperor unharmed. And why so? Because there the foulness of lust was punished in the king; here the most self-restrained justice of the Christian religion was kept in the king. There, without reverence for religion, the licence of fury satisfied the greed for pleasure; here there were Christians who spared, Christians whom they spared, and Christians for whose memory and in whose memory there was sparing. Therefore let them stop tearing at religion and provoking the patience of God, through which they have it that even this may go unpunished, if at some time they stop. Let them indeed recall with me the times of their ancestors, most restless with wars, execrable with crimes, foul with dissensions, most continuous in miseries: which they may rightly shudder at because they were, and must needs pray that they may not be; and pray indeed to the one God alone, who then by a hidden justice allowed them to happen, and now by an open mercy grants that they do not. These will now be set out by me more fully from the very beginning of the City, with the histories unrolled in order.

## 2.4

In the 414th year after the overthrow of Troy, and in the sixth Olympiad (which is held, with a contest and games, at Elis, a city of Greece, every fifth year, four years being completed between), the city of Rome was founded in Italy by the twins Romulus and Remus as its founders. Romulus at once stained its kingship with parricide, and with a like success of cruelty he took the Sabine women, seized against all custom, bound them in wicked marriages and endowed them with the blood of their husbands and parents. So Romulus, having first killed his grandfather Numitor and then his brother Remus, seized the rule and established the city; he dedicated the kingdom with the blood of his grandfather, the walls with that of his brother, the temple with that of his father-in-law; he gathered a band of criminals by a promise of impunity. His first field for war was the forum of the city, a sign that wars foreign and civil, mixed together, would never be lacking. The women of the Sabines, whom he had enticed by a treaty and games, he seized as dishonourably as he defended them wickedly. Their leader Titus Tatius, an old man pressing honourable claims of piety, was long driven off by arms, and as soon as Romulus had taken him into partnership in the kingdom, he killed him. A battle was fought with the Veientes, then still of small name but already of great strength. The town of the Caeninenses was taken and destroyed. Once arms had been taken up there was never rest, since a foul want and a shameful hunger at home would be feared if they ever settled into peace. From here I shall touch as briefly as possible on the unceasing contests, always heavy in proportion to their strength: that Tullus Hostilius, the founder of military affairs, in his confidence in a well-trained youth made war on the Albans, and after long uncertain hope on both sides, and certain disaster, at last ended the worst outcomes and doubtful results by the short-cut of the combat of the triplets; that when the peace was broken again Mettus Fufetius, wavering in the Fidenate war and even planning treachery, paid the penalty of his double mind with a divided body, chariots tearing him in opposite directions; that the Latins often met Ancus Marcius as their enemy in battle and were sometimes overcome; that Tarquinius Priscus cut down in countless encounters all his neighbours and the twelve peoples of Etruria, powerful at that time; that the Veientes, pressed by Servius Tullius, were defeated but not tamed; that the reign of Tarquinius Superbus was taken up by the crime of the murder of his father-in-law, kept by cruelty to the citizens and lost by the outrage of the violation of Lucretia; and, among the vices at home, the virtues shining abroad, that is the strong towns in Latium taken by him, Ardea, Oricolum, Suessa and Pometia, and whatever he achieved against Gabii, whether by his own fraud, by his son's punishment or by Roman strength. But how great were the evils that the Romans endured through 243 years of that continuous domination of kings is shown not only by the expulsion of one king, but by their abjuring of the royal name and power. For if the pride of only one man had been at fault, he alone should have been driven out and the royal dignity kept for better men. So when the kings had been driven from the city, the Romans, thinking that they should take counsel for themselves rather than leave anyone to lord it over their liberty, created consuls: under whom, as if in the grown age of a growing state, it was exercised in bolder ventures.

## 2.5

In the 244th year after the founding of the city Brutus, the first consul among the Romans, strove not only to equal the first founder and king of Rome in parricide but even to surpass him. For he dragged before the assembly his two young sons and as many brothers of his wife, the young Vitellii, charged with a plan to recall the kings to the City, beat them with rods and struck them with the axe. Then he himself, in the war with the Veientes and the Tarquinienses, met Arruns, the son of Superbus, in battle, and fell dying together with him. Porsenna, king of the Etruscans, a most weighty supporter of the royal name, forcing Tarquin on them, terrified, shut in and besieged the anxious city for three continuous years. And unless Mucius, by his steadfast endurance in burning his hand, or the maiden Cloelia, by her admirable boldness in crossing the river, had moved the enemy, the Romans would surely have been compelled to suffer either captivity, overcome by the pressing enemy, or slavery, subjected to the king they had taken back. After this the Sabines, scraping together forces from everywhere, marched on Rome with great preparation for war. Struck with fear at this, the Romans created a dictator, whose authority and power should go before the consul's; and this was of the greatest benefit in that war then. There followed the secession of the plebs from the fathers when, while the dictator M. Valerius was conducting a levy of soldiers, the people, goaded by various wrongs, occupied the Sacred Mount in arms. What could be more dreadful than this ruin, when the body, cut off from the head, was planning the destruction of that through which it drew breath? And the Roman name would have been finished by an inward ruin, had not a timely reconciliation crept in before the secession knew itself. Besides those open disasters of wars, a hidden ruin pressed on and threatened, with a wretched sequence: for in the consulship of T. Geganius and P. Minucius the two very greatest abominations of all evils, famine and pestilence, seized the weary city. Battles ceased for a little, but deaths did not cease. The Etruscan Veientes, heavy enemies, rising in war with the forces of their neighbours joined to them, were met by the consuls M. Fabius and Cn. Manlius. There, after the oath by which the Romans had devoted themselves not to return to camp except after victory, the contest was so savage and the appearance of conquered and conquerors so alike that, when most of the army had been lost and the consul Manlius and the consular Fabius had been killed in the fight, the consul M. Fabius refused to accept the triumph offered to him by the senate, because mourning rather was owed for such great losses to the state. What bereavement that family of the Fabii, most glorious in number and strength, brought on the state by its fall, when it drew the war with the Veientes as its lot, the river that destroyed them and the gate that sent them out bear witness, with names infamous to this day. For when three hundred and six Fabii, truly the brightest lights of the Roman state, had asked that a war against the Veientes be decreed to them as their own, they strengthened the hope of an expedition rashly undertaken by its first successes; then, drawn into an ambush and surrounded by the enemy, all were massacred there, only one being kept to announce the disaster, so that their country should hear more wretchedly that they were lost than it had lost them. Besides this, such things were not done at Rome only, but every province seethed with its own fires; and what the foremost poet described of one city, I would say of the whole world: "Everywhere cruel grief, everywhere fear, and very many an image of death."

## 2.6

At the same time, then, Cyrus, king of the Persians (whom I mentioned above for the sake of setting out the history, and who was then roaming with arms through Asia, Scythia and the whole east, while Tarquinius Superbus, as king or as enemy, was pressing the city either with slavery or with war): Cyrus, I say, having subdued all those against whom he had gone, made for the Assyrians and Babylon, then a people and a city wealthier than all. But the river Gyndes, second in size after the Euphrates, cut off his attack. For one of the royal horses, outstanding in whiteness and form, persuaded by confidence to cross, was snatched away, hurled down and drowned by the whirlpools that rose where they struck a shallow in the rushing bed. The king in his anger resolved to take vengeance on the river, declaring that the river that had now swallowed his splendid horse should be left fordable by women barely wetting their knees. Nor was he slower in carrying it out: with all his forces, for a whole year, he cut the river Gyndes through and drew it off into great ditches, breaking it up into four hundred and sixty channels. Using the diggers well trained by this work, he also diverted the Euphrates, by far the strongest river, which flows through the middle of Babylonia. And so, with the fords made passable and parts of the bed lying open, he made a dry path, and took the city that it was almost incredible to mortals either could have been built by human work or could be destroyed by human valour. For many have recorded that Babylon was founded by the giant Nimrod and restored by Ninus or Semiramis. It was visible on every side on a level plain, a most pleasant site by nature, laid out in a square like a camp, with equal walls. The strength and size of its walls are scarcely credible to tell: fifty cubits in breadth and four times as much in height. Its circuit, moreover, ran round four hundred and eighty stades. The wall was made of baked brick bonded with bitumen poured between, and a ditch, open wide on the outside, flowed round it in place of a river. In the front of the walls were a hundred gates of bronze. And the breadth itself, at the top of the battlements, with the dwellings of the defenders set equally on each side, took swift four-horse chariots in the space between. The houses within, of fourfold dwellings, were wonderful in their threatening height. And yet that great Babylon, the first founded after the restoration of the human race, was now conquered, taken and overthrown with almost the least delay. Croesus, king of the Lydians, famous for his wealth, had come there then to help the Babylonians; defeated, he fled anxiously back to his kingdom. Cyrus, after he had invaded Babylon as an enemy, overthrown it as a victor and ordered it as a king, carried the war into Lydia, where he overcame without any trouble an army already terrified by the earlier battle. He even captured Croesus himself, and when he had captured him he granted him both his life and his patrimony.

There is no need at this point to enlarge on the unstable states of changeable things. For the capture of Babylon confirms that whatever is made by labour and hand slips and is consumed by age. As its empire was the first and the most powerful, so it also was the first to give way, so that, as if by a certain right of the succeeding age, the inheritance owed to its successors might be handed on, and they too would keep the same rule of handing it on. So at the very first attempts of the approaching Cyrus great Babylon fell, and vast Lydia; the widest arms of the east, with their head, fell in the campaign of one battle. And our people complain with unconsidering anxiety if that mass of the once most powerful Roman state now trembles, shaken more by the weakness of its own old age than by the strength of others.

## 2.7

So the same Cyrus, with the success of the time just past, made war on the Scythians. Queen Tamyris, who was then at the head of that people, though she could have prevented him from crossing the river Araxes, allowed him to cross: first because of her confidence in herself, and then because of the advantage of having the enemy shut in by the barrier of the river. So Cyrus, entering Scythia, measured out a camp far from the river he had crossed, and then by a trick abandoned it, furnished with wine and feasts, as if he had fled in terror. When she learned this the queen sent a third of her forces and her young son to pursue Cyrus. The barbarians, as if invited to a feast, were first conquered by drunkenness, and soon, when Cyrus returned, all were cut down together with the young man. Tamyris, having lost her army and her son, prepared to wash away the grief of a mother and of a queen with the blood of the enemy rather than with her own tears. She feigned distrust, in despair at the disaster inflicted, and by gradually giving way she drew the proud enemy into an ambush. There, with an ambush laid among the mountains, she destroyed two hundred thousand Persians together with the king himself, with this added to the wonder of the thing above all, that not even a messenger of so great a disaster survived. The queen ordered the head of Cyrus to be cut off and thrown into a skin filled with human blood, taunting him not like a woman: "Glut yourself," she said, "with the blood you thirsted for, of which you went on insatiable for thirty years."

## 2.8

In the 245th year from the founding of the city Darius obtained the kingdom by lot, some interval after Cyrus had been killed among the Scythians. For Cambyses, the son of Cyrus, reigned between them. He conquered Egypt, abominated the whole religion of Egypt, and put down its ceremonies and temples. After him the Magi too dared to creep into the kingdom under the name of the king whom they had killed; but they were soon caught and crushed. So Darius, one of those who had put down the boldness of the Magi with the sword, was made king by the consent of all. After he had recovered by war the Assyrians and Babylon, which were revolting from the kingdom of the Persians, he made war on Antyrus, king of the Scythians, chiefly for this reason: that he had not obtained the marriage he sought with his daughter. A great necessity, no doubt, that seven hundred thousand men should be exposed to the danger of death for the lust of one man. For he entered Scythia with an incredible preparation, with seven hundred thousand armed men; and since the enemy gave him no chance of a proper battle, and moreover tore apart the ends of his forces with sudden raids, fearing that his return might be denied him if the bridge over the river Hister were broken, he fled back in alarm, having lost eighty thousand fighting men; though he did not count this number of the lost among his losses, and he did not feel the loss of a force that scarcely anyone would have dared to aspire to have. From there he attacked and subdued Asia and Macedonia. He also overcame the Ionians in a naval encounter. Then he made an attack on the Athenians, and directed his arms against them, because they had helped the Ionians against him. When the Athenians learned that Darius was approaching, though they had asked the Lacedaemonians for help, yet when they had learned that the Persians were held back by the idleness of a four-day religious observance, seizing the hope from the occasion, with only ten thousand citizens drawn up and a thousand Plataeans as auxiliaries, they burst out on the plains of Marathon against six hundred thousand of the enemy. Miltiades was in command in that war. Relying on speed more than on valour, by a quick enough advance he closed with the enemy hand to hand before he could be driven back by an unhindered discharge of arrows. So great was the difference of fighting in that war that on one side there seemed to be men ready to kill, on the other cattle prepared to die. Two hundred thousand Persians fell on the plains of Marathon at that time. Darius felt this loss: for, defeated and put to flight, he took to his ships and fled back to Persia. But while he was renewing the war and planning vengeance on the victors, he died in the very preparations, in the 74th Olympiad, that is in the 275th year after the founding of the city, at which time at Rome the virgin Popilia was buried alive for the crime of unchastity.

## 2.9

Xerxes, succeeding his father Darius in the kingdom, spent five years preparing the war against Greece that his father had undertaken. Demaratus the Lacedaemonian, who happened then to be in exile with Xerxes, betrayed it to his people by means of tablets first written on and then covered with wax. Xerxes is said to have had seven hundred thousand armed men from his kingdom and three hundred thousand from his auxiliaries, and also twelve hundred beaked ships and three thousand transports; so that it has rightly been recorded that for so unheard-of an army and so immense a fleet the rivers were scarcely enough for drinking, the lands scarcely enough for marching, the seas scarcely enough for sailing. This column, so incredible in our times, whose number it is now harder to establish than it was then to conquer, Leonidas, king of the Spartans, withstood with four thousand men in the narrows of Thermopylae. Xerxes, in contempt of the small number set against him, ordered battle to be joined and hands to be locked. Those whose kinsmen and comrades had fallen on the plains of Marathon were the beginning both of the contest and of the disaster. Then, as a greater and slower crowd came on in turn, since it was now neither free to run forward nor unhindered to fight nor quick to flee, and was held up only by deaths, there was for three days on end not a battle of two peoples but the slaughter of one. On the fourth day, when Leonidas saw that the enemy was pouring round on every side, he urged his allied auxiliaries to withdraw from the battle, escape to the top of the mountain and keep themselves for better times; but he said that he and his Spartans must meet another lot: he owed more to his country than to his life. When he had sent the allies away he told the Spartans that they must hope for very much glory and nothing for life; that they must not wait either for the enemy or for daylight, but by the opportunity of night they must burst into the camp, mingle their arms with the enemy's and throw their columns into confusion; nowhere would the victors perish more honourably than in the camp of the enemy. So, persuaded to prefer to die, they armed for vengeance for their coming death, as though they both demanded and avenged their own destruction. Wonderful to tell, six hundred men burst into the camp of six hundred thousand. An uproar arose throughout the camp; the Persians themselves helped the Spartans by slaughtering one another; the Spartans, searching for the king and not finding him, cut down and laid low everything, roamed through the whole camp, and among the dense heaps of bodies could scarcely keep up with the few men left: victors beyond doubt, had they not chosen to die. The battle was drawn out from the beginning of the night into the greater part of the day. At last, wearied with conquering, wherever each of them, as his limbs failed, seemed to himself to be glutted with vengeance for his death, there, among the obstacles of corpses and on a field quivering with thick, half-congealed blood, he fell, exhausted, and died.

## 2.10

Xerxes, twice defeated on land, prepared a naval battle. But Themistocles, the leader of the Athenians, when he understood that the Ionians (in giving help to whom in the earlier war he had turned the attack of the Persians on himself) were bringing a fleet equipped to help Xerxes, determined to win them to his side and draw them away from the enemy. And because the opportunity of speaking with them was denied, he ordered signs to be set up and fixed on rocks in the places where the Ionians seemed likely to come with their ships. In them he rebuked with fitting reproach those who had once been allies and sharers in danger, but now were unjustly idle, and persuaded them with a religious exhortation to the rights of their ancient treaties, and warned them above all that, when battle was joined, they should back water like men giving way and take themselves out of the fighting. So the king, keeping part of the ships with him, stayed on the shore as a spectator of the battle. But Artemidora, queen of Halicarnassus, who had come to help Xerxes, threw herself into the fighting most fiercely among the foremost leaders, so that, the other way round, a woman's caution was seen in the man and a man's boldness in the woman. When the fight was doubtful the Ionians, following the instruction of Themistocles, began little by little to withdraw from the contest; and their defection persuaded the Persians, who were already looking round for flight, to flee openly. In this panic many ships were sunk and captured; but more, fearing the savagery of the king as much as the cruelty of the enemy, slipped away to their homes. Mardonius approached the king, anxious with so many evils, urging that the king should return to his kingdom before bad news stirred up revolution at home; he himself, if the remaining forces were handed over to him, would both exact vengeance from the enemy and drive off the disgrace at home, or, if the misfortunes of war continued, he would indeed give way to the enemy, but without discredit to the king. The plan was approved and the army handed over to Mardonius. The king set out with a few for Abydos, where he had joined the bridge as if he were the conqueror of the sea. But when he found the bridge broken up by the winter storms, he crossed in alarm in a fisherman's boat. It was indeed something for the human race to look at and grieve over, measuring the changes of things by this variation above all: that he was content to hide in a tiny boat, he under whom the sea itself had once lain hidden and had carried the yoke of its captivity when the bridge was joined; that he needed the meanest service of one little slave, he to whose power, while mountains were cut through, valleys filled and rivers drained, the very nature of things had given way. The infantry too, which had been entrusted to the generals, wasted away so with toil, hunger and fear, and, as disease grew cruel, so great a plague and such foulness of the dying arose, that the roads were filled with corpses, and dread birds and shameless beasts, roused by the lure of food, followed the dying army.

## 2.11

But Mardonius, to whom Xerxes had entrusted the rest of the war, was first puffed up by a brief success and soon thrown down to the depths. He stormed Olynthus, a town of Greece. Having tried to lead the Athenians into a hope of peace by various solicitations, when he saw that their liberty could not be stormed, he burned part of the city and led all his equipment of war down into Boeotia. A hundred thousand Greeks followed him there too, and joining battle without delay they destroyed his forces and forced Mardonius to flee with a few, stripped as if from a shipwreck. They took his camp, stuffed with the king's wealth, at no small cost indeed to their ancient industry: for after the division of this booty, Persian gold was the first corruption of the valour of Greece. Utter destruction, once begun, pressed on the wretched. For it happened that on the same day on which the forces of Mardonius were destroyed in Boeotia, part of the Persian army was fighting a naval battle in Asia under Mount Mycale. There a new rumour suddenly filled the ears of both fleets and peoples: that the forces of Mardonius had been wiped out and that the Greeks had been the victors. A wonderful ordering of divine judgement, that a battle fought in Boeotia at sunrise should be reported in Asia at midday on the same day, with such great spaces of sea and land lying between! This rumour supported above all the outcome that it made the Persians, seized on hearing of the disaster of their allies first with grief and then with despair, neither ready for battle nor fit for flight. And so the enemy, made more steadfast by his success, attacked them while they were dismayed and routed. Xerxes, having become contemptible to his people by waging the war in Greece unsuccessfully, was trapped in the palace by his prefect Artabanus and killed.

O times most worthy of longing and remembrance! O those days of unbroken calm, which are set before us to look back to as if out of darkness! In which, within the shortest interval, three wars under the three successive kings snatched nineteen hundred thousand men from the vitals of one kingdom; to say nothing of most unhappy Greece at that time, which surpassed in dying the whole number about which we now grow dull. That most famous Leonidas of the Lacedaemonians, in this war against Xerxes, which was the last both for himself and for the enemy, when he had spoken to his six hundred those most famous words of encouragement, "Take your breakfast as men who will dine among the dead", nevertheless advised the auxiliaries, whom he ordered to leave the war, mercifully, to keep themselves for better times. See, while he promised that the future would be better, these assert that the past was better: and when each loathes his own present, what else can be concluded but that the times are either always good but unthanked, or never better at all?

## 2.12

But at Rome (to return to the time from which I digressed; for I am not forced by the interval of miseries to pass to others, but, just as the evils boiling up everywhere were once bound together by their very deeds, so they are told mixed together; for our purpose is to compare the times of the world with one another, not to insult the labours of any part of it): at Rome, then, in the 290th year after the founding of the city, when war was halted for a little, a heavy pestilence, which there always either cut short the rare truces that had been made or forced them to be made, blazed up violently through the whole city; so that it was rightly with a prodigy going before that the sky seemed to be on fire, when the head of the nations burned with so great a fire of disease. For in that year the pestilence carried off both consuls, Aebutius and Servilius, wasted the military forces for the most part, and destroyed many nobles and above all the plebs with a foul wasting; though already four years before a plague had arisen and ravaged the same people.

Then in the next year exiled citizens and runaway slaves, led by Herbonius, a Sabine, seized and set fire to the Capitol. There the younger men resisted most bravely indeed under the consul and commander Valerius; but the crisis of the battle was so savage and heavy that the consul Valerius himself was killed there, and moreover defiled with his own death an unworthy victory over slaves.

There followed a year in which a consul was besieged with his defeated army. For the Aequi and Volsci overcame the consul Minucius when he met them in battle, and surrounded him as he fled on Algidus with hunger and the sword; and it would have ended unhappily had not Quinctius Cincinnatus, that outstanding dictator, crushed the enemy and broken the tight siege. Found in the country and summoned from the plough to the fasces, he took up the office, drew up an army and was soon made victor; he put the yoke of oxen on the Aequi, and, holding victory as if it were the plough handle, he drove the subjugated enemies before him, the first to do so.

## 2.13

In the year next to the three hundredth from the founding of the city, while the envoys sent to the Athenians to bring over the laws of Solon were awaited, famine and pestilence held Roman arms in check.

In the three hundredth year itself, that is in the ninety-fifth Olympiad, the power of the consuls, handed over to the decemvirs for the sake of drawing up laws on the Attic model, brought great ruin on the state. For first of the decemvirs, the rest giving way, Appius Claudius alone prolonged his command for himself, and at once a conspiracy of the others followed, so that, scorning the custom by which the insignia of command were with one man and the power was shared, all did everything at their own pleasure. So, among the other things that all of them most insolently presumed to do, suddenly each one came out with twelve fasces and the other insignia of command: and in this new beginning of a wicked ordering, with the religious respect for the consuls put away, a band of tyrants shone forth. Having added two tables of laws to the ten earlier ones, and doing very many things with most insolent arrogance, on the day on which it was the custom to lay down the magistracy they came out with the same insignia. The lust of Appius Claudius too increased their unpopularity greatly. In order to rape the maiden Verginia he first brought a suit claiming her as a slave; and so her father Verginius, driven by grief for her freedom and shame at the disgrace, in the sight of the people struck down his daughter, dragged off into slavery, a pious parricide. The people, moved by the cruelty of this necessity and warned of the danger to their liberty, occupied the Aventine Hill in arms. Nor did they stop defending their liberty with arms until the conspiracy of the plotters had abdicated their very offices too.

In the 103rd and 105th Olympiad, for almost the whole year, there were such frequent and such heavy earthquakes in Italy that Rome was wearied by continual news of countless shakings and collapses of villas and towns. Then there was so continuous and scorching a drought that it denied the hope of the earth bearing its fruits both for that year and for the next. In the same times, when the enemy Fidenates, terrible with a band of very great auxiliaries crowding round them, were threatening the citadels of the Romans, Aemilius, dictator for the third time, drove off and healed the great mass of evil, only just taking Fidena itself. Such was the strain of evils and of spirits among them that either the wars flooding in from outside blotted out the disasters at home, or, after the losses of wars, various plagues, blazing up from sky and earth, corrupted the truces they had relaxed with unceasing assault.

## 2.14

Sicily was from the beginning the homeland of the Cyclopes, and after them always the nurse of tyrants, often too the captive of slaves: of whom the first fed on the flesh of men, the middle ones on tortures, the last on deaths; except that in foreign wars it was held either as booty or as prize. To finish as briefly as possible, it knows no rest from evils except now; or rather, so that the differences of the times may be shown more plainly, as before it alone among all always suffered disorders internal or external, so now alone of all it never does. For even (to say nothing of the length either of that disaster by which it was pressed, or, on the contrary, of this peace which it enjoys) Etna itself, which then seethed with frequent eruptions, to the destruction of cities and fields, now only smokes with a harmless appearance, as proof of the past. So (to pass over for the present the tyrants, of whom whoever was the avenger was soon made the successor) in the meantime, that is in the 335th year from the founding of the city, when the people of Rhegium near Sicily were troubled by discord and the city was divided by dissension into two parts, one part called in veterans from Himera, a city of Sicily, to help them. Those veterans, having first driven out of the city those against whom they had been called in, and then soon slaughtered also those whom they had come to help, occupied the city with the wives and children of their allies, daring a crime that no tyrant could match. For it would have been better for the people of Rhegium to suffer anything rather than of their own accord invite men to whom they themselves, as exiles, would leave their country, wives, children and household gods as booty.

The people of Catina too, when they were suffering from the hostile and heavy Syracusans, asked the Athenians for help. But the Athenians, more for their own interest than for that of their allies, sent a fleet equipped to Sicily, since they were both planning to extend their empire and feared that the Syracusan fleet, newly equipped, would help the Lacedaemonians. And because the Athenians who had been sent had made a prosperous beginning by slaughtering the enemy, they brought back larger forces and a stronger army into Sicily under the generals Laches and Chariades. But the people of Catina, moved by weariness of the war, made a treaty with the Syracusans and spurned the help of the Athenians. Afterwards, however, when the Syracusans, intent on domination, overstepped the terms of peace, they again sent envoys to Athens, unkempt in hair and beard and dressed in mourning, to beg for pity and help by both speech and dress. So a great fleet was equipped under the generals Nicias and Lamachus, and Sicily was sought again with such great forces that even those who had obtained the vote feared it. The Athenians at once fought two infantry battles with favourable success, and shut in the enemy, worn down in the city and surrounded by the fleet set against them, by land and sea. But the Syracusans, with their affairs broken and wearied, asked the Lacedaemonians for help. Gylippus was soon sent by them, alone indeed, but one who was put forward as the equal of all reinforcements. On arriving, when he heard that the state of the war was already sinking, he gathered auxiliaries partly in Greece and partly in Sicily and seized places favourable for the war. Then, defeated in two battles but not frightened, in the third encounter he killed Lamachus, put the enemy to flight and freed his allies from the siege. From here the Athenians, defeated in a land battle, made trial of the sea and prepared to engage in a naval contest. When Gylippus learned this he sent for an equipped fleet from the Lacedaemonians; the Athenians likewise sent Demosthenes and Eurymedon, with fresh forces, in place of the lost general; and the Peloponnesians too, by the consent and decree of many cities, sent great help to the Syracusans. So, under the appearance of a war for allies, they carried on their domestic quarrels, and, as if the contest had by agreement been transferred from Greece to Sicily, they fought on both sides with their utmost strength. So the Athenians were beaten in the first encounter, and lost their camp too with all their money, public and private, and the whole equipment of a long campaign. With their resources broken and reduced to straits, Demosthenes urged that, while their affairs were not yet wholly lost, however battered they seemed, they should return home and leave Sicily; but Nicias, made more desperate by shame at things badly managed from the beginning, insisted on staying. They renewed the naval contest and soon, through lack of skill, were drawn into the narrows of the sea of Syracuse and caught in the enemy's ambush: the general Eurylochus was killed first, and eleven ships were burned. Demosthenes and Nicias abandoned the fleet, as if they would flee more safely by a land march. But Gylippus first seized their hundred and thirty abandoned ships, and then, setting out to pursue the fugitives themselves, captured and killed a great many. Demosthenes escaped the disgrace of slavery by a voluntary death; but Nicias crowned an unworthy and shameful life with the disgrace of captivity.

## 2.15

So the Athenians, having struggled for two years in Sicily, not without loss to the Lacedaemonians, were beset by other evils at home. For Alcibiades, who had earlier been declared general against Syracuse, and was soon held for trial on some charge, went into voluntary exile to Lacedaemon and pushed the Spartans, while the Athenians were in disorder, to press on again with a new war and not to give them room to breathe before they were crushed. All Greece agreed to this undertaking as if, for the public good, counsel were being taken with combined forces to put out a common fire. Darius too, king of the Persians, remembering his father's and grandfather's hatred for this city, made a treaty with the Lacedaemonians through Tissaphernes, the prefect of Lydia, and promised them the costs of the war and forces. Wonderful to tell, so great was the strength of the Athenians at that time that, when they were attacked, that is a single city, by the forces of Greece, Asia and the whole east, they seem to have been worn out by often fighting and never giving way rather than conquered. For at first Alcibiades forced all their allies to revolt from them to the Lacedaemonians; but, attacked by the Lacedaemonians too with an ambush out of envy, he fled and withdrew to Tissaphernes. Having at once become more intimate with him by a ready wit and the charm of fitting eloquence, he persuaded him not to help the Lacedaemonians with such lavish wealth; he ought rather to become the umpire and spectator of this contest, and to keep the strength of Lydia intact against the victor. For this reason Tissaphernes ordered part of the fleet, with a considerable force, to be taken to Lacedaemon, so that the Lacedaemonians should neither fight safely at another's risk with abundant support, nor, wholly abandoned, give up the contest they had undertaken.

## 2.16

Among the Athenians, when they had long been troubled by discord at home, with danger threatening the supreme command was transferred to the senate by the will of the people: for discords are nourished by leisure, but when necessity presses, private causes and hatreds are put aside and counsel is taken for the common good. But since this very thing was ruinous because of the pride and tyrannical lusts inborn in that people, at last the exile Alcibiades was recalled by the army and made commander of the fleet. When they learned this the leading men first tried to betray the city to the Spartans; then, when they had planned this in vain, they went into exile of their own accord. So Alcibiades, having freed his country, directed the fleet against the enemy. When battle was joined the Athenians took the victory. Further, the greater part of the Spartan army was slaughtered, almost all their generals too were killed, and eighty ships were captured, not counting those that perished, burned and sunk in the fighting. The war was carried again onto land, and it went equally unhappily for the Spartans. Broken by these things the Lacedaemonians asked for peace, but could not obtain it. Moreover, the Syracusan garrisons were recalled to Sicily when the threat of the Carthaginian war was heard. For this reason Alcibiades, with his victorious fleet, roamed through all Asia, plundered and laid low everything with wars, fires and slaughter, and captured and recovered a great many of the cities that had lately revolted from the alliance.

## 2.17

So Alcibiades, having won a great name, entered Athens as victor, to the admiration and joy of all. After a short interval he increased his forces, enlarged the army and the fleet in number and made for Asia again. The Lacedaemonians, for their part, put Lysander in command of the fleet and the war. Cyrus too, the brother of Darius, put over Ionia and Lydia in place of Tissaphernes, strengthened them with great wealth and help. So Lysander crushed by a sudden attack the army of Alcibiades, intent on booty and so scattered and roaming everywhere, conquered it without any struggle and cut it down as it fled. This was a great disaster for the Athenians, and they received a wound much more terrible than the one they had lately inflicted. When they learned of it the Athenians supposed that Alcibiades had taken care to avenge the old grief of his exile by this crime of treachery, and so they put Conon in his place and entrusted to him the remaining force and the supreme command of the war. Wishing at least to make up in number the exhausted forces, he levied old men and boys and enrolled an army. But a force of this kind brought no delay to the war, since war is usually decided by strength, not by number. So at once the unwarlike force was either captured or slaughtered, and so great a carnage of the slain was made in that battle that not only the empire but even the name of the Athenians seemed to have been destroyed. They, in despair, decided to give the city to foreigners (so that those who had lately been masters through all Asia might now at least protect their walls and liberty from this rabble); and although even in their own judgement they were not sufficient to protect even these things with the walls in front of them, they nevertheless prepared to try a naval battle again. Fury without counsel thinks grief is valour, and boldness promises as much as anger plans. So, with all either captured or killed, nothing was left even of the remnants. Only Conon, the general, survived the war and his people, and fearing the cruelty of the citizens he withdrew to King Cyrus. Euagoras, the general of the Lacedaemonians, took away all their cities and left the Athenians nothing but an empty city; nor this for long, for afterwards he surrounded the city itself with a siege. Within, famine, desolation and disease worked on the besieged, and when, after every abomination of miseries, which it is a horror even to tell, no hope but death presented itself, they asked for peace. There was a great debate about this between the Spartans and their allies. When very many declared that the most restless city should be levelled to the ground and the most hostile people abolished together with its very name, the Spartans said that they would not allow one of the two eyes of Greece to be torn out; and moreover they promised peace if the fortifications leading from the Piraeus harbour to the city were turned round and they handed over their remaining ships of their own accord, and then received thirty chosen governors. When they were bound to this condition and submitted to it, the Lacedaemonians appointed Lysander to draw up the laws of obedience in the city. This year was notable for the storming of Athens, the death of Darius, king of the Persians, and the exile of Dionysius, tyrant of Sicily.

So the thirty governors appointed for the Athenians rose up as thirty tyrants. First they surrounded themselves with three thousand attendants, and soon they also set seven hundred soldiers of the victorious army about their persons. They made the killing of Alcibiades the omen of a slaughter of all, everywhere, to come: as he fled he was shut in a bedroom on the road and burned alive. When he had been killed, secure as if the avenger had been removed, they drained the wretched remnants of the city with slaughter and plunder. They also massacred Theramenes, one of their own number, whom they perceived to be displeased by these things, as an example and a terror to the rest. So all fled from the city in every direction; but since, by a prohibition of the Lacedaemonians, hospitality was denied to the exiles throughout all Greece, they all betook themselves to Argos and Thebes. There they were so cherished by the kindness of hospitality that they not only soothed their grief for the lost country but even began to think of the hope of recovering it. Among the exiles was Thrasybulus, an energetic man, famous among his people for the nobility of his family, who was the leader in daring for his country. So the exiles, gathering together, took Phyle, a fort on the borders of Attica, and, helped by the resources of many cities, gained strength. Lysias too, the Syracusan orator, sent them five hundred soldiers with their pay, as if to help the city that was the common homeland of eloquence. That battle was savage; but since the one side was fighting for the liberty of its country, the other for domination over another's, the fight itself gave judgement on their spirits and causes. For the defeated tyrants fled back into the city, and removed from the guarding of the city all the Athenians whom they had earlier chosen as their attendants, now suspected of treachery. They even dared to try to corrupt Thrasybulus himself; and when this was hoped for in vain, they sent for help from Lacedaemon and rushed into war again. There the two by far most savage of all the tyrants were massacred. The rest, defeated and put to flight, Thrasybulus, when he understood that they were for the most part Athenians, pursued with shouts, held back with speech and bound with entreaties, setting before their eyes whom they wished to flee and to whom they wished to flee back: he had taken up war against thirty masters, not against wretched citizens; rather, all who remembered that they were Athenians should follow the avengers of the liberty of the Athenians. This exhortation had such power with them that they soon returned to the city and forced the tyrants to leave the citadel and move to Eleusis. After they had received into fellowship of the city their fellow citizens, until then exiles, they roused the tyrants to war by rivalry, for the liberty of others seemed to them like their own slavery. Then, when battle had been declared and they first came together as if for a parley, the tyrants were trapped in an ambush and massacred like victims of peace. So, called back into one, after the insatiable tears of great joys, they laid these first foundations of their recovered liberty, with the solemn testimony of an oath that the discords and animosities of the past should be brought into perpetual oblivion and undying silence. This kind of pact, as if shaping a new institution of life and a new happiness for their state, they called amnesty, that is the abolition of evils.

A most wise provision of the Athenians, especially after so many proofs of miseries: if only human affairs could prevail, with the consent of men lasting, as they are ordered. But this same agreement was so corrupted almost in the very words of the agreement that, barely two years having passed, Socrates, the most famous of philosophers, was driven by evils to wring his life from himself by poison among them; and then, barely forty years having passed, to say nothing of other things, the same Athenians, wholly deprived of their liberty, were slaves under Philip, king of the Macedonians. Yet the Athenians, wisest of all, and taught well enough by their own evils that the smallest things grow by concord and the greatest fall by discord, and that all things, good or even evil, that are done abroad are rooted in and sent out from inward beginnings, wiped away hatreds at home and suppressed wars abroad, and left their descendants an example from their ruin and a counsel from their restoration: if only, because of the weakest changeableness of the human mind, what is counselled in adversity were kept in prosperity.

## 2.18

At almost the same time a civil war, or rather more than civil, scarcely ended without parricide, was being waged among the Persians. For when King Darius died and his sons Artaxerxes and Cyrus disputed the kingdom, at last they fought with great preparations, with ruin to provinces and peoples on both sides. In this conflict, when chance had set the two brothers, charging from opposite sides, against each other, first Artaxerxes, wounded by his brother, escaped death by the speed of his horse. But Cyrus was soon overwhelmed by the royal cohort and put an end to the contest. So Artaxerxes, having gained both the booty of his brother's expedition and his army, confirmed his royal power by fratricide. So all Asia and Europe were thrown into confusion with deaths and outrages, partly each within itself, partly with one another.

See, on the smallest page and in the fewest words, I have not so much unfolded the acts of so many provinces, peoples and cities as I have wound up balls of miseries. For who could unfold in speech the disaster of that time, its deaths, or equal its griefs with tears? Yet these very things, because they have faded through the long interval of ages, have become for us exercises for the wit and the delights of stories. Though if anyone applies his mind more closely, and with the whole feeling of his mind almost mingles himself in the very causes and wars, and then again, set as if in the citadel of a spectacle, measures both times in their qualities, I would readily say that he will judge that those times could not have been so unhappily disordered and confused except by an angry and averted God, nor these times so settled except by a God gracious and merciful.

Then in these times Sicily was shaken by a very heavy earthquake, and was laid waste besides, with very great damage to fields and villas, by the fires and hot ashes of Mount Etna boiling over.

Then too the city of Atalante, adjoining Locris and joined to its land, was cut off by a sudden onset of the sea and left desolate as an island. A plague too attacked the wretched remnants of the Athenians and ravaged them for a long time.

## 2.19

In the 355th year from the founding of the city the siege of Veii, for ten continuous years, wore down the besiegers more than the besieged. For the Romans, often broken by sudden sallies of the enemy, were moreover forced to take their lot of war in winter quarters, to winter under hides, and at last to endure hunger and cold in sight of the enemy; at the end they took the city, without any testimony worthy of Roman valour, by tunnels and a secret creeping in. This victory, more useful than noble, was followed first by the exile of the dictator Camillus, who won it over the Veientes, and then by the irruption of the Gauls and the burning of the city. Let anyone dare, if he can, to compare any disturbances of this time with that disaster: though the story of a past evil does not weigh as much as the injury of a present one. So the Senonian Gauls, under the leadership of Brennus, with a numerous and very strong army, while they were besieging the city of Clusium, which is now called Tuscia, saw the envoys of the Romans, who had come then to make peace between them, fighting against them in the battle line. Moved with indignation at this, they gave up the siege of the town of Clusium and made for Rome with all their strength. As they rushed on in this way the consul Fabius met them with an army; but he did not withstand them; rather, that hostile charge cut down, laid low and passed over it like a dry crop. The river Allia bears witness to this disaster of Fabius, as the Cremera does to that of the Fabii. For no one could easily recount a like ruin of the Roman soldiery, even had Rome not been burned besides. The Gauls entered the open city; they massacred the senators sitting stiff like statues in their seats, and, burning them in the fire of their houses, buried them under the fall of their own roofs. All the rest of the youth, which is known to have been barely a thousand men at that time, hiding in the citadel of the Capitoline Hill, they shut in by a siege, and there they wore down, subdued and sold the unhappy remnants by hunger, plague, despair and dread: for they agreed on a thousand pounds of gold as the price of their departure; not that Rome was of small name among the Gauls, but that they had already worn it down so much before that it could not then be worth more. When the Gauls went out there remained, in that former circuit of the city, a foul heap of shapeless ruins; and everywhere, as people wandered through the obstructions and did not know one another among their own homes, the echo of a voice that struck against them, answering, held their frightened hearing in suspense. Horror shook their minds, the very silences terrified them, since rarity in wide spaces is the stuff of fear. Hence they thought of, agreed upon and attempted changing their dwelling place, living in another town, and even being registered under another name.

Behold the times by comparison with which the present are weighed; behold the times for which remembrance sighs; behold the times that strike repentance for the religion chosen, or rather neglected! Truly these two captivities are equal and are compared with each other: the one raging for six months and this one passing in three days; the Gauls, when the people had been wiped out and the city destroyed, pursuing the very name of Rome to its last ashes, and the Goths, giving up their intention of plundering, driving bewildered crowds to the refuges of safety, that is to the holy places; there scarcely any senator found who escaped even by being absent, here scarcely anyone sought who perhaps perished in hiding. I might rightly compare that the number of the saved there was the number of the lost here. Plainly, as the facts show, it must be confessed too: in this present disaster God raged more and men less, since, by Himself doing what they had not completed, He showed why He had sent them. For since it was beyond human strength to set fire to bronze beams and to undermine the masses of great structures, the forum, with its empty images, which by a wretched superstition feign either god or man, was struck down by the stroke of lightning; and of all these abominations, what the fire let in by the enemy did not reach, the fire sent from heaven overthrew.

And since the matter for speaking is abundant and can by no means be concluded in this book, let this be the end of the present volume, so that we may pursue the rest in those that follow.

## 3.praef

I have already declared in the previous book, and I now repeat of necessity, according to your instruction, about the past conflicts of the world: that neither all things, nor all things in full, can be set out that were done, and as they were done. For great and countless things have been written most fully and by very many writers, and the writers, though not with the same causes, had the same events set before them, since they unroll wars, and we the miseries of wars. Moreover, out of this very abundance of which I complain there arises a difficulty for me, and a more knotted anxiety shuts me in. For if, out of zeal for brevity, I leave some things out, it will be thought either that they were lacking to me now or that they did not exist at that time; but if, striving to indicate everything without expressing it, I gird it up in a compendious brevity, I shall make it obscure, and for most readers things will be so said that they will not seem said at all: above all since, on the contrary, we take care to commend the force of events, not their image; and brevity and obscurity, or rather, as brevity always is, obscure brevity, though it offers an image of knowing, takes away the vigour of understanding. But since I know that both must be avoided, I shall do both, so that each may in some way be tempered by the other, if things seem neither much left out nor much compressed.

## 3.1

In the 364th year from the founding of the city (a year that Rome felt as most grievous because of a captivity unknown to it, and that Greece held as magnificent because of an unusual peace), at the very time when the Gauls held Rome captured and burned and sold it back, Artaxerxes, king of the Persians, ordered through envoys that all Greece should withdraw from arms and rest in peace, declaring that whoever opposed the peace would be attacked with war. The Greeks could surely have scorned him when he gave such an order as steadfastly as they had often bravely defeated him, had they not drunk in so gladly the opportunity offered from any quarter, which they had greedily desired. For they showed how reluctantly and wretchedly they had carried on until then the things that they laid down so easily, even on unworthy terms. For what is so unworthy of free and brave men as to lay down arms and be slaves to peace at the command of one far off, often defeated, still an enemy and moreover threatening? Unless, at the mere sound of the peace announced, the sick intent of war had wasted away in the hearts of all, and after long vigils of toil an unexpected rest had relaxed them, yawning and stupefied, before the will could arrange the rest itself by pacts. Whence so great a weariness had overwhelmed the hearts and bodies of all the peoples throughout Greece, which so easily persuaded their savage spirits to rest in an unknown leisure, I shall show as briefly as I can.

The Lacedaemonians, being men, and Greek men, desiring more the more they had, after they had become masters of the Athenians, swallowed up all Asia in hope of dominion. So, stirring up war against the whole east, they chose Hircylides as general for this campaign. When he saw that he had to fight against two very powerful prefects of Artaxerxes, king of the Persians, Pharnabazus and Tissaphernes, he took a plan suited to the moment, to escape the weight of a double encounter: he declared war on and attacked one, and held the other off with a pact of peace. Pharnabazus accused Tissaphernes before Artaxerxes, then their common king, as a traitor, especially as one who had made an agreement about the terms of a treaty with the enemy in time of war; and he urged the king to put in his place, as general for the naval war, Conon the Athenian, who happened then to be living in exile in Cyprus. So Conon, having received five hundred talents of silver, was summoned through Pharnabazus and put in command of the fleet. When they learned this the Lacedaemonians themselves asked, through envoys, for help for a naval war from Hercynion, king of Egypt, from whom they received a hundred equipped triremes and six hundred thousand measures of grain; and from their allies too they gathered great reinforcements from every side. For this campaign they appointed by the consent of all Agesilaus as general, a man lame of foot, but one whom they preferred, in a most difficult state of affairs, to have as a limping king rather than to have a limping kingdom. Rarely have generals so equal in every kind of energy met in one war; worn out by the bitterest battles with each other and smeared with much blood, they withdrew from one another as if unconquered. So Conon, having again received pay from the Great King himself, returned to the fleet, invaded the enemy's lands, stormed towers, forts and the other defences, and, like a storm let loose, laid everything low wherever it fell. The Lacedaemonians, beset by evils at home, stopped gaping after foreign ones, and threw away the hope of dominion with the danger of slavery threatening; they recalled Agesilaus, whom they had sent to Asia with an army, to help their country. Meanwhile Pisander, the general left at Sparta by King Agesilaus, had fitted out a very great and very strong fleet, moved by rivalry with the valour of Agesilaus, so that, while Agesilaus was conducting a land campaign, he too might range along the sea coast in naval raids. Conon, having taken on the task, gave it a double care, owing diligence to his allies and loyalty to his country, so as to show to the one his nature and to give the others his energy: in this more inclined to his fellow citizens, that he spent the hazard of others' blood for their quiet and freedom, and fought against most insolent enemies at the king's risk and for his country's reward. So they joined a naval battle, the Persians under Conon, the Spartans under Pisander; soldiers, rowers and the leaders themselves were swept with one equal ardour into mutual slaughter. The greatness and savagery of this war is shown by the state of the Lacedaemonians, which from then on always declined: for from that time the hope of the Spartans seemed to ebb and to be carried back, slipping, until, by rising with difficulty and falling back wretchedly, it was finished and lacked both power and name.

For the Athenians this same battle was the beginning of recovering their power, as for the Lacedaemonians of losing theirs. So first the Thebans, supported by the help of the Athenians, attacked the Lacedaemonians, wounded and anxious from their earlier defeat, much encouraged by confidence because of the valour and energy of their general Epaminondas, with whom they thought they could easily obtain the empire of all Greece. So a land battle was fought, the Thebans winning with the least trouble. For in this conflict too Lysander was defeated and killed; Pausanias too, the other general of the Lacedaemonians, was accused of treachery and driven into exile. The Thebans, having gained the victory, gathered the force of their whole army and made for Sparta, thinking they would enter without any trouble a city empty of defence, almost all of whose forces, with the king himself, they had already destroyed, and which they saw abandoned by all its allies. The Lacedaemonians, driven by the danger to the city, held a levy of untrained soldiers, such as it was, and went out to meet the enemy. But, once defeated, they had neither the courage nor the spirit to stand against the victors. So when there was a slaughter of almost one side only, suddenly King Agesilaus, summoned from Asia, came unexpectedly upon the war. He attacked the Thebans, already more cheerful and slacker from the success of a double victory, and overcame them without difficulty, above all since he still had almost his whole strength with him. Agesilaus himself, however, was seriously wounded. But the Athenians, when they learned that the Lacedaemonians had been raised up by an unexpected victory, fearful of their former slavery, from which they had then barely begun to breathe again, gathered an army and joined it to the Boeotians as help. It was entrusted to the general Iphicrates, who, though quite young, scarcely twenty years old, strengthened the weakness of his age with maturity of mind. Conon too, an Athenian indeed but general of the Persian army, hearing of the return of Agesilaus, came back to lay waste the lands of the Lacedaemonians. So the Spartans, shut in and terrified by the din of the enemies sounding round them on every side, wasted away in almost the last despair. But Conon, after he had glutted himself with the vast plundering of the enemy's soil, went on to Athens, himself sad amid the very great joy of the citizens, since he saw the city, once most adorned with people and refinement, now worn down by the wretched squalor of ruins and desolation. So he wrought a great monument of piety and compassion in its restoration. For he filled it, emptied by the Lacedaemonians, with the spoils of the Lacedaemonians, and rebuilt it, burned by the Persians, with the Persians building. Meanwhile Artaxerxes, king of the Persians, as was said at the beginning, commanded all the peoples of Greece through envoys to withdraw from arms and rest in peace: not because he took mercifully thought for the weary, but so that, while he was busy with wars in Egypt, no attack should be attempted on his kingdom.

## 3.2

So, when all the Greeks were relaxed in the most longed-for rest and grew sluggish in leisure at home, the Lacedaemonians, restless rather than energetic, and intolerable more for fury than for valour, after laying wars aside tried the thefts of war. For, spying out the absence of the Arcadians, they broke into a fort of theirs by a sudden attack. The Arcadians, roused by the wrong, joined to themselves the help of the Thebans and sought back by war what had been lost by theft. In that battle Archidamus, the general of the Lacedaemonians, wounded, when he saw his men already being cut down as conquered, asked through a herald for the bodies of the slain for burial: this is held among the Greeks to be the sign of victory conceded. The Thebans, content with this admission, gave the signal to spare them and put an end to the contest. Then, after a few days of truce had passed, when the Lacedaemonians had turned to other wars, the Thebans, with Epaminondas as their general, took confidence about invading Lacedaemon as if it were secure and abandoned. They came silently to Lacedaemon in the dead of night; but they did not find it as unwary or undefended as they thought. For the old men, armed, with the rest of the crowd of unwarlike age, knowing beforehand of the enemy's coming, threw themselves into the very narrows of the gates, and barely a hundred men worn out by age burst out against fifteen thousand soldiers. While they were bearing so great a weight of war, the young men came up and decided without delay to meet the Thebans in open contest. When battle was joined and the Lacedaemonians were being beaten, suddenly Epaminondas, the general of the Thebans, fighting too incautiously, was wounded. So, while fear arose from grief in the one side and stupor from joy in the other, both withdrew as if by silent consent. Epaminondas, gravely wounded, when he had learned of the victory of his men and had kissed his shield, took away the hand with which he had closed the wound and opened the way out for his blood and the way in for death. His death was followed by the ruin of the Thebans in such a way that they seemed not to have lost their general but to have perished with him themselves.

I have woven an inextricable hurdle of disordered history, and, following in words close on the track, I have entangled the uncertain circles of wars carried on here and there with a frantic fury: for, as I see, I have written the more disorderly the more I have kept to the order. The wicked greed of the Lacedaemonians for domination: among how many peoples, what cities, which provinces it stirred up movements of hatred of what kind, how many causes of contests, who could set it out by number, order or reason? Since they themselves too are said to have been afflicted no more by wars than by the confusion of wars. For as this continuous war was drawn out through several ages, the Athenians, Lacedaemonians, Arcadians, Boeotians, Thebans, and at last Greece, Asia, Persia and Egypt with Libya and the greatest islands carried on naval and land conflicts together in indiscriminate raids. I could not report the thousands of men slain, even if I numbered the wars.

But now let him reproach these times and praise those who does not know that all these peoples of those cities and provinces now grow old only at the games and theatres, as then they wasted away above all in camps and battles. That city of the Lacedaemonians, most flourishing and then aspiring to the empire of the whole east, could scarcely have a hundred old men; so, beset by unceasing evils, it wretchedly paid out ages that never ripened. And men complain now, whose cities, full of boys and old men, are enriched by the safe travels of the young and win, by peaceful exercises, the wages of pleasure at home: unless perhaps (as all present things are wont to be worthless to human changeableness) life itself is wearisome to those who itch for novelties of things in deed and in hearing.

## 3.3

In the 376th year from the founding of the city all Achaea was shaken by a most savage earthquake, and two cities, Ebora and Helice, were then swallowed up when the ground broke open in chasms.

But I could now, on the contrary, tell of like things in our own days at Constantinople, now the chief of the nations in the same way: foretold and begun but not completed, when, after a terrible warning and the foreknowing conscience of its evil, the earth trembled, moved from its foundations below, and a flame poured from heaven hung above, until God, moved by the prayers of the emperor Arcadius and of the Christian people, turned aside the present destruction, proving that He alone is both the preserver of the humble and the punisher of the wicked. But I would yield this to modesty, that these things be recalled rather than set out, so that he who knows may remember and he who does not may inquire.

Meanwhile the Romans, who for seventy years had been worn down, subdued and ground by continual wars with the city of the Volsci, and besides with the Falisci, the Aequi and the people of Sutrium, at last in the days written above took these same cities, under Camillus as general, and put an end to the reviving contest. At the same time they also defeated the Praenestines, who had come, warring and killing, as far as the gate of Rome, at the river Allia, with T. Quinctius fighting.

## 3.4

In the 384th year from the founding of the city, in the consulship of L. Genucius and Q. Servilius, a great pestilence seized all Rome. It was not, as usually happens, that a disordered balance of the seasons, more or less than usual (that is, an untimely drought in winter, or a sudden heat in spring, or an unseasonable moisture in summer, or the undigested enticement of a rich autumn), and on top of this a corrupting air breathed out from the Calabrian forests, brought sudden passing attacks of acute illnesses. But a heavy and long one, different in no sex, unlike in no age, wore out everyone continually for two years with a general wasting, so that even those whom it did not drive to death it left emptied out and afflicted by a foul emaciation. I suppose the detractors of the Christian time would complain at this point if I happened to pass over in silence with what ceremonies the Romans then appeased the gods and calmed the diseases. When the pestilence grew more cruel day by day, the pontiffs as advisers urged that stage plays be performed, since the gods demanded them. So, to drive away a temporary plague of bodies, a perpetual disease of souls was summoned. This is indeed now a rich place for my grief and reproach; but since your reverence has already exercised its zeal for wisdom and truth upon it, it is not right for me to venture upon it. Let it be enough for me to have given a reminder, and to have sent the reader, from whatever purpose, to the fullness of that reading.

## 3.5

This wretched plague and its more wretched expiation were followed in the next year by a sad enough prodigy. For suddenly the earth sprang apart in the middle of the city, and the depths, gaping with a vast break, were suddenly laid open. The shameless cave remained for a long time, with its chasm open, as a spectacle and a terror to all, and, as the gods' interpreters said, demanded the wicked burial of a living man. M. Curtius, a knight in arms, satisfied the wicked jaws by throwing himself in, and cast upon the cruel earth an unexpected glut: for it was too little for it to receive through the tombs the dead from so great a pestilence, unless, split open, it swallowed the living too.

## 3.6

In the 388th year from the founding of the city a terrible flood of Gauls again settled by the river Anio, at the fourth milestone from the city. Without doubt it would easily have seized the city, thrown into confusion by the weight of its multitude and the eagerness of its valour, had it not grown sluggish through leisure and slowness. There Manlius Torquatus began a most savage fight in single combat, and T. Quinctius, the dictator, finished it in a most bloody encounter. Very many Gauls, put to flight in this battle, renewed their forces and rushed again into war, and were overcome by the dictator C. Sulpicius. A little later too there followed a battle with the Etruscans under C. Marcius: in which one may guess how many men were slain when eight thousand Etruscans were captured.

A third time in the same days the Gauls spread themselves for plunder through the coastal places and the plains lying below the Alban mountains. Against them, after a new levy of soldiers had been held and ten legions enrolled, sixty thousand Romans went out, the help of the Latins being refused them. M. Valerius finished this battle with the help of a raven, from which he was afterwards called Corvinus. For when the Gaul who issued the challenge had been killed, the enemy, terrified and fleeing in all directions, were heavily slaughtered.

## 3.7

I think too that the first treaty struck with the Carthaginians, which was in the same times, must be counted among the evils; especially since such heavy evils arose from it that they seem to have begun from it. For in the 402nd year from the founding of the city envoys were sent from Carthage to Rome and made a treaty. That a hail of evils and an unbroken darkness of continuous miseries would follow this entry of the Carthaginians into Italy is testified by the trustworthiness of the histories, the infamy of the places, and the abomination of the days on which these things were done.

Then too night seemed to stretch over the greater part of the day, and a hail of stones coming down from the clouds beat the earth with real stones.

In these days too Alexander the Great was born, truly that whirlpool of miseries and most savage hurricane of the whole east.

Then too Ochus, who is also Artaxerxes, after finishing a very great and long war in Egypt, drove a great many of the Jews into migration and ordered them to live in Hyrcania by the Caspian Sea. It is believed that they remain there to this day with the most ample increase of their race, and that from there they will one day break out. In the storm of this war, too, passing through, he destroyed Sidon, the wealthiest city of the province of Phoenicia, and Egypt, though he had been defeated before, he then subdued, broke with the sword and subjected to the empire of the Persians.

## 3.8

From here at once wars were taken up by the Romans against the Samnites, a people strong in wealth and arms, on behalf of the Campanians and Sidicini. The Samnite war, waged with doubtful fortune, was taken over by Pyrrhus, the very greatest enemy of the Roman name; the war of Pyrrhus was soon followed by the Punic. And though the gates of Janus, always open, show that there was never a pause from the disasters of wars after the death of Numa, yet from this time the heat of evils blazed up as if at midday, pressing over the whole sky. Further, once the Punic war had begun, whether wars, slaughter, ruins and every kind of unspeakable death ever ceased except under the rule of Caesar Augustus, let whoever thinks the Christian times are to be defamed inquire, find and declare. Apart from that, however, during the Punic wars, for the passing of one year, like a bird flying by, the Romans, because the gates of Janus were shut, amid the fevers and diseases of the state, were enticed by this very short sign of peace, as if by the slightest draught of cold water, so that, growing hot again for the worse, they were afflicted much more heavily and violently.

But if it is established beyond all doubt that first under Caesar Augustus, after the Parthian peace, the whole world, with arms laid down and discords abolished, settled in a general peace and a new quiet, obeyed the laws of the Romans, preferred Roman law to its own arms and, scorning its own leaders, chose Roman judges; and lastly that all nations, all provinces, countless cities, endless peoples, all lands had one will, to serve peace with a free and honourable zeal and to take counsel for the common good (which before not even one city or one people of citizens or, what is more, one house of brothers could have had continuously); and if also, since these things came about under the rule of Caesar, it is made manifest by the clearest proof that the birth of our Lord Jesus Christ shone into this world in the very reign of Caesar: then, unwilling though they may be, those whom envy drove to blasphemy will be compelled to recognise and confess that this peace of the whole world and its most tranquil calm came about not by the greatness of Caesar but by the power of the Son of God, who appeared in the days of Caesar; and that the world itself, by a general recognition, obeyed not the emperor of one city but the creator of the whole world, who, as the rising sun floods the day with light, so at His coming mercifully clothed the world with a peace spread wide. This will be set out more fully when, with the Lord Himself bringing it about, we have come to it.

## 3.9

So in the 409th year from the founding of the city the Romans made war on the rebellious Latins, in the consulship of Manlius Torquatus and Decius Mus. In this war one consul was killed and the other became a parricide. For Manlius Torquatus killed his own son, a young man and a victor, the slayer of Maecius of Tusculum, a noble horseman and then an enemy notable for challenging and insulting. The other consul, when in a renewed conflict he saw the wing he commanded being cut down and broken, rushed of his own accord among the thickest of the enemy and fell. Manlius, though a victor, did not earn, as a parricide in his triumph, the meeting of the noble Roman youths that is customarily given.

In the year following this the Vestal virgin Minucia was condemned for having committed unchastity, and was buried alive in the field which is now called Accursed.

## 3.10

But after a short time had passed I shudder to tell what was done. For in the consulship of Claudius Marcellus and Valerius Flaccus the Roman matrons blazed up with an incredible madness and love of crimes. It was indeed a foul and pestilential year, and the slaughter now inflicted on every side was being carried out in crowds, and still everyone simply believed that the air was corrupted; until, when a certain slave woman came forward as informer and proved it, first many matrons were forced to drink the poisons they had brewed, and then, as soon as they drank, were consumed. So great was the multitude of matrons who shared in these crimes that three hundred and seventy of them are reported to have been condemned at once.

## 3.11

In the 422nd year from the founding of the city Alexander, king of the Epirotes, the uncle of the great Alexander, brought his forces over into Italy. While he was preparing war against the Romans and was busy with wars, striving to strengthen the forces of his army around the cities neighbouring Rome and either to win auxiliaries for himself or to take them away from his enemies, he was defeated and killed in a very great war in Lucania by the Samnites, who were supporting the people of the Lucanians.

But since I have gone some way in reviewing the disasters of the Romans, reminded too by the mention of this Alexander, I shall go back a very few years and gather, as far as I can, great things in small compass about Philip, king of the Macedonians, who had as his wife Olympias, the sister of this Alexander of Epirus, and by her begot Alexander the Great.

## 3.12

In the 400th year from the founding of the city Philip, the son of Amyntas and father of Alexander, obtained the kingdom of the Macedonians and held it twenty-five years, in which he built up all these heaps of bitterness and every mass of evils. He was first given by his brother Alexander as a hostage to the Thebans, and for three years was educated with Epaminondas, a most energetic general and a supreme philosopher. When Alexander himself had been killed by the crime of their mother Eurydice (though she had already committed adultery, had killed another son first and, widowing her daughter, had pledged marriage to her son-in-law by the death of her husband), he was compelled by the people to take up the kingdom, which he was guarding for the little son of his murdered brother. Wearied abroad by the onset of enemies rising up on every side, and at home by fear of plots often detected, he first waged war with the Athenians. When they were defeated he carried his arms to the Illyrians, and having slaughtered many thousands of the enemy he took the very noble city of Larissa. From there he invaded Thessaly, not so much from love of victory as from ambition to have the Thessalian cavalry, so as to mix their strength into his army. So, having forestalled the Thessalians unexpectedly and brought them under his power, by joining the bravest squadrons and forces of horse and foot he made an army most unconquerable. So, the Athenians defeated and the Thessalians subjected, he married Olympias, the sister of Arruba, king of the Molossians. Arruba, thinking that by this, that he was making an alliance with the Macedonians through a marriage tie with the king, he would extend his own rule, was deceived by this very thing and lost it, and grew old as a private man in exile. Then Philip, while he was attacking the city of Methone, lost an eye from the stroke of an arrow. But he soon stormed and took the city itself.

From there he forestalled almost all Greece by his plans and subdued it by his strength. For while the cities of Greece each desired to rule, all of them lost their rule; and while they rushed without limit into mutual destruction, they felt only at last, when crushed and enslaved, that what each lost was perishing for all. While Philip watched their mad dealings as from a watchtower and, always suggesting help to the weaker side, cleverly fostered the contests, the kindling of wars, as a skilful craftsman of deceit, he subjected to himself conquered and conquerors alike. The immoderate domination of the Thebans gave him the opportunity of obtaining the dominion of all Greece. They forced the defeated Lacedaemonians and Phocians, worn out besides with slaughter and plunder, to fly to arms, when moreover in the common council of Greece they had loaded them with so great a fine of money that they could in no way pay it. So the Phocians, under Philomelus as general and supported by the help of the Lacedaemonians and Athenians, joined battle, put the enemy to flight and took the camp of the Thebans. In the following battle, amid immense slaughter of both peoples, Philomelus was killed; in his place the Phocians made Onomarchus general.

Then the Thebans and Thessalians, giving up a levy of their citizens, of their own accord asked for Philip, king of Macedonia, as their general, whom they had before laboured to drive off as an enemy. When battle was joined and the Phocians were cut down almost to extermination, the victory went to Philip. But the Athenians, hearing the outcome of the war, seized the narrows of Thermopylae to stop Philip crossing into Greece, by the same reasoning as earlier against the approaching Persians.

So when Philip saw that he was shut out from entering Greece by the blocking of Thermopylae, he turned the war prepared against the enemy against his allies. For the cities of which he had a little before been the general, open to congratulate and receive him, he invaded as an enemy and cruelly plundered; and, utterly abolishing all sense of alliance, he sold the wives and children of all of them under the crown, and also overturned and despoiled all the temples; and yet in twenty-five years he was never defeated, as if the gods were angry. After this he crossed into Cappadocia and waged war there with like treachery; he killed the neighbouring kings, taken by trickery, and subjected all Cappadocia to the empire of Macedonia. From there, after the slaughter, burning and plundering done in allied cities, he turned to the murder of his brothers. Fearing those born to his father by his stepmother as co-heirs to the kingdom, he set about killing them. When he had killed one of them, two fled to Olynthus. Philip soon attacked it as an enemy, filled that most ancient and most flourishing city with slaughter and blood and emptied it of its wealth and its people, and gave up his brothers, dragged out, to torture and death. Then, elated by the destruction of his allies and the murder of his brothers, thinking that everything he had thought of was allowed him, he seized the gold mines in Thessaly and the silver mines in Thrace, and, so as not to leave any law, human or divine, unviolated, he seized the sea and, with his fleet spread out, set about practising piracy too. Moreover, when two brothers, kings of Thrace, disputing about the boundaries of their kingdom, had by consent chosen him as judge, Philip, after the manner of his nature, came to the judgement as if to war with an army drawn up, and deprived the unsuspecting young men of life and kingdom.

The Athenians, who earlier had kept Philip from entering by fortifying Thermopylae, now of their own accord sought peace with him and so reminded that most deceitful enemy that the guard of the entrance was neglected. The other cities of Greece too, so as to be free to give themselves more intently to civil wars, under the appearance of peace and treaty subjected themselves of their own will to a foreign domination; especially when the Thessalians and Boeotians demanded of Philip that he show himself the general he had professed to be against the Phocians and carry on the war he had undertaken, while the Phocians, with the Athenians and Lacedaemonians on their side, laboured with bribes and prayers to have the war either put off or taken away. Philip secretly promised different things to both: to the Phocians he confirmed by oath that he would give peace and pardon, while to the Thessalians he pledged that he would soon be there with an army; but he forbade both to prepare for war. So Philip, with his forces drawn up, entered the narrows of Thermopylae without fear and, having seized them, fortified them with garrisons placed there. Then for the first time not the Phocians alone, but all Greece, felt itself captured. For Philip, breaking faith and trampling on his oath, first gave the Phocians up to unspeakable tearing apart, and then, ravaging the cities and territories of all, he brought about by his bloody presence that he was feared even when absent. When he returned to his kingdom, after the manner of shepherds, who drive their flocks now through summer, now through winter pastures, he moved peoples and cities at his pleasure, as places seemed to him to be filled or abandoned. Everywhere a pitiable sight and a most savage kind of misery presented itself: to suffer destruction without an attack, captivity without war, exile without crime, domination without a victor. Among the stings of injuries an overflowing fear pressed on the wretched, and grief grew by the very concealment, sunk the deeper the less they were allowed to show it, fearing that even their tears would be taken for stubbornness. Some peoples, torn from their homes, he set against the territories of his enemies; others he settled on the farthest borders of his kingdom; some, out of jealousy of their strength, so that they should not be able to do what they were believed able to do, he divided up to make good the exhausted cities. So that once most glorious body of flourishing Greece fell into many torn fragments, its liberty extinguished first.

## 3.13

When he had practised these things on a good many cities of Greece, and yet was pressing all of them with fear, guessing the wealth of all from the booty of a few, and thinking that a coastal city was necessary, as a useful source of profit, for completing an equal devastation of all, he judged Byzantium, a famous city, most fitting to be his base by land and sea, and at once surrounded it with a siege when it resisted. This Byzantium, once founded by Pausanias, king of the Spartans, and afterwards enlarged by Constantine, the Christian emperor, and called Constantinople, is now the seat of a most glorious empire and the head of the whole east. Philip, after a long and fruitless siege, set about piracy, so as to make up by plundering the money he had exhausted in besieging. So he took a hundred and seventy ships laden with goods and sold them off, and so relieved his gasping want with a small refreshment. Then, to carry on plunder and attend to the siege, he divided his army. He himself set out with the bravest and took many cities of the Chersonese and, routing their peoples, carried off their wealth. He also crossed into Scythia with his son Alexander, intent on plunder. Atheas then reigned over the Scythians. When he was pressed by a war with the Histrians, he asked Philip for help through the people of Apollonia; but when the king of the Histrians died soon after, freed both from the fear of war and from the need of help, he broke off the treaty agreed with Philip. Philip gave up the siege of Byzantium and attacked the Scythian war with all his strength. When battle was joined, though the Scythians were superior in both number and valour, they were defeated by Philip's trickery. In that fight twenty thousand boys and women of the Scythian people were captured and a great quantity of cattle driven off; nothing of gold or silver was found, and this was the first thing to give proof of the poverty of the Scythians. Twenty thousand noble mares were sent into Macedonia to supply the breed.

But as Philip was returning the Triballi met him in war, in which Philip was wounded in the thigh so that the horse under him was killed through his body. When everyone thought he had been killed, they turned to flight and lost the booty. Then for some little while, while he recovered from the wound, he rested in peace; but as soon as he recovered he made war on the Athenians. Placed in such great danger, they took to themselves the Lacedaemonians, once their enemies and now their allies, and wearied the cities of all Greece with embassies, so that they might attack the common enemy with common forces. So a good many cities joined themselves to the Athenians, but fear of war drew some to Philip. When battle was joined, though the Athenians were far superior in the number of their soldiers, they were defeated by the valour of the Macedonians, hardened by continual wars. That this battle was far more savage than all the earlier wars, the outcome of the matter itself showed: for this day ended throughout all Greece the glory of the dominion it had won and the state of its most ancient liberty.

## 3.14

Afterwards Philip used a most bloody victory over the Thebans and Lacedaemonians. For of the leading men of the cities he struck some with the axe, drove others into exile and deprived all of their goods. Those long since driven out by their fellow citizens he restored to their country; and he put three hundred of these exiles in charge as judges and governors, so that, treating their old grief with a new power, they would not allow the unhappily oppressed peoples to breathe again in hope of liberty. Moreover, having held a great levy of soldiers from all Greece in support of the royal arrangements, he equipped two hundred thousand foot and fifteen thousand horse, besides the army of the Macedonians and a boundless barbarism of peoples, intending to send them into Asia for an expedition against Persia. He chose three generals to send ahead against the Persians, namely Parmenion, Amyntas and Attalus. And while the forces written above were being gathered from Greece, he decided to celebrate the marriage of Alexander (who was the brother of his wife Olympias, and was afterwards laid low by the Sabines in Lucania, and whom he had made king of Epirus as the price of the debauchery he had practised on him) by joining his daughter Cleopatra to him. The day before he was killed, Philip, asked what end should most be wished for a man, is said to have answered that the best was the one that could befall a brave man, reigning in peace after the glories of his deeds, suddenly and quickly by an unexpected sword, without struggle of the body or disgrace of the mind: which soon befell him. Nor could he be prevented by the angry gods, whom he had always held of small account and whose altars, temples and images he had overthrown, from obtaining the death that seemed to him most to be chosen. For on the day of the wedding, as he was making for the magnificently prepared games between the two Alexanders, his son and his son-in-law, he was trapped in a narrow place without his guards by Pausanias, a noble young Macedonian, and killed.

## 3.15

Let them now assert and extol these things with many voices, as the praises of brave men and happy deeds, by which the bitterest calamities of others turn into sweet stories: provided that they themselves never deplore with a sadder account the wrongs by which they are at some time troubled. But if they wish others hearing their own complaints to be moved as much as they themselves felt in suffering, let them first compare, not the past with the present, but deeds with deeds, and judge both from hearing, as if arbiters of others' affairs. For twenty-five years the fraud, ferocity and domination of one king drove on the burning of cities, the destructions of wars, the subjugation of provinces, the slaughter of men, the plunder of wealth, the driving off of cattle, the selling of the dead and the captivity of the living. These deeds of Philip, set in our memory, would be enough as an example of miseries even if Alexander had not succeeded him in the kingdom. His wars, or rather the evils of the world that followed in order under his wars, I put off for a little, so that in this place, for the agreement of the times, I may add the Roman affairs.

In the 426th year from the founding of the city the notable infamy of the Romans made the Caudine Forks quite celebrated and famous. For when, in the earlier war, twenty thousand Samnites had fallen when Fabius, master of the horse, joined battle, the Samnites encamped at the Caudine Forks with more careful attention and more furnished preparation. There, when they had shut in the consuls Veturius and Postumius and all the forces of the Romans by the narrowness of the places and by arms, Pontius their general took such advantage of the security of victory that he thought he should consult his father Herennius whether he should kill those shut in or spare them when subjugated; but he chose to keep them alive for disgrace. For it was established that the Romans had very often before been defeated and killed, but never could be captured or forced to surrender. So the Samnites, having won the victory, ordered the whole Roman army, shamefully captured and stripped too of arms and clothing (only single meaner coverings being allowed them to cover the shameful parts of their bodies), sent under the yoke and subjected to slavery, to march in a long procession. And having received six hundred Roman knights as hostages, they sent back the consuls loaded with ignominy and empty of everything else.

Why should I labour with words to enlarge the stain of this most shameful treaty, when I would have preferred to be silent? For today the Romans would either not exist at all or would be slaves under the dominion of Samnium, if they themselves, when subject to the Samnites, had kept the faith of the treaty that they wish to be kept to them by those subject to them.

In the following year the Romans broke the pact made firm with the Samnites and forced them into war, which, fought at the urging of the consul Papirius, caused great slaughter of both peoples. Since the anger of recent infamy goaded the fighters on one side and the glory of the last victory on the other, at last the Romans won by dying stubbornly; nor did they stop alike being slaughtered and slaughtering until, after the Samnites had been defeated and their general captured, they put the yoke back on them. Then the same Papirius stormed and took Satricum, having driven the Samnite garrison out of it. This Papirius was then held among the Romans to be so very warlike and energetic that when Alexander the Great was said to be planning to come down from the east, take Africa by force and from there cross into Italy, the Romans thought that, among the other generals who were then the best in their state, he would be the chief one who could withstand the attack of Alexander.

## 3.16

So in the 426th year from the founding of the city Alexander succeeded his father Philip in the kingdom. He gave the first proof of his spirit and valour by quickly crushing the movements of the Greeks, whose instigator in revolting from the rule of the Macedonians had been the orator Demosthenes, bribed with the gold of the Persians. So he remitted the war to the Athenians when they begged to be spared, and moreover freed them from the fear of a fine; he wiped out the Thebans together with their destroyed city, and sold the rest under the crown; he made the other cities of Achaea and Thessaly tributary; and soon carrying the war from here, he subdued the Illyrians and Thracians too. Then, about to set out for the Persian war, he killed all his kinsmen and nearest relatives. In his army there were thirty-two thousand foot, four thousand five hundred horse and a hundred and eighty ships. With so small a force, it is uncertain whether it is more wonderful that he conquered the whole world or that he dared to attack it. In his first encounter with King Darius there were six hundred thousand Persians in the battle line, who turned their backs, overcome no less by the skill of Alexander than by the valour of the Macedonians. So there was a great slaughter of the Persians. In Alexander's army a hundred and twenty horsemen and only nine foot soldiers were missing. Then he besieged, stormed and took Gordium, a city of Phrygia, which is now called Sardis, and gave it to plunder. Then, when the arrival of Darius with great forces was announced to him, fearing the narrows of the places in which he was, he crossed Mount Taurus with wonderful speed, and having covered five hundred stades at a run in one day he came to Tarsus. There, when he had gone down sweating into the very cold river Cydnus, he grew stiff and, with a contraction of the sinews, came very near to death. Meanwhile Darius advanced into battle with three hundred thousand foot and a hundred thousand horse. This multitude of enemies moved even Alexander, above all considering his own small numbers, although, having already overcome six hundred thousand enemies with the same small numbers, he had learned not only not to fear battle but even to hope for victory. So when both armies had halted within a spear's throw, and the leaders, running about, sharpened with various incitements the peoples intent on the signal for battle, the fight was joined with great spirit on both sides. In it both kings, Alexander and Darius, were wounded, and the contest was doubtful just as long as it took Darius to flee; from then on a slaughter of the Persians followed. Then eighty thousand foot and ten thousand horse were killed there, and forty thousand were captured. Of the Macedonians a hundred and thirty foot and a hundred and fifty horse fell. In the camp of the Persians much gold and other wealth was found. Among the captives of the camp were the mother of Darius, his wife, who was also his sister, and his two daughters. When Darius could not obtain their ransom even by offering half his kingdom, he gathered for the third time all the strength of the Persians and the help of their allies and renewed the war. But while Darius was doing this, Alexander sent Parmenion with forces to attack the Persian fleet, and himself set out for Syria. There, of the many kings who came to meet him of their own accord wearing the fillets of suppliants, he chose some, changed others and destroyed others. He crushed and took Tyre, a most ancient and flourishing city, which resisted him in confidence in the Carthaginians, its kinsmen. From there he passed through Cilicia, Rhodes and Egypt with stubborn fury. Then he went on to the temple of Jupiter Ammon, so that by a lie arranged for the occasion he might abolish the shame of an uncertain father and the infamy of an adulterous mother. For he summoned to himself the priest of the shrine himself and secretly told him what answer he wished to be given to him as if he were consulting it, as their historians say. So Alexander was sure, and has revealed to us, that, since the gods themselves are dumb and deaf, it is either in the power of the priest to invent what he wishes or in the will of the one consulting to hear what he prefers. Returning from Ammon for the third Persian war, he founded Alexandria in Egypt.

## 3.17

Darius, having lost hope of peace, set against Alexander, as he returned from Egypt, four hundred thousand foot and a hundred thousand horse, near Tarsus. Nor was there delay in battle: all rushed on the sword with blind fury, the Macedonians made bold by enemies so often defeated by them, the Persians preferring to die unless they won. Rarely has so much blood been shed in any battle. But Darius, when he saw his men being defeated, though ready to die in the battle, was compelled by the persuasion of his men to flee. In this battle the strength and the kingdoms of Asia fell, and the whole east passed into the power of the Macedonian empire; and all the confidence of the Persians was so worn away in this war that after it no one dared to rebel, and the Persians, after an empire of so many years, patiently accepted the yoke of slavery. Alexander spent thirty-four continuous days reviewing the booty of the camp. He seized Persepolis, the capital of the Persian kingdom, most famous and crammed with the wealth of the whole world. When he learned that Darius was being held by his own kinsmen, bound in golden fetters, he decided to pursue him. So, ordering the army to follow, he set out himself with six thousand horse and found him on the road, left alone, pierced with many wounds and breathing out the last of his life through them. When he was dead, with an empty pity, Alexander ordered him to be carried back to the tombs of his ancestors and buried; though he was holding in cruel captivity, I will not say his mother or wife, but even his little daughters.

Amid so great a multitude of evils, belief in what is said is most difficult: in three battles and as many years, fifteen hundred thousand foot and horse were consumed. And this from that kingdom and those peoples from which, not many years before, nineteen hundred thousand are reported to have been destroyed: though apart from these disasters, in the same three years very many cities of Asia were crushed, all Syria laid waste, Tyre destroyed, Cilicia emptied, Cappadocia subdued and Egypt made over; the island of Rhodes too, trembling, went into slavery of its own accord; very many provinces lying below Taurus, and Mount Taurus itself, tamed and conquered, accepted the yoke they had long refused.

## 3.18

And lest anyone should perhaps think that then either the east alone was subdued by the strength of Alexander or Italy alone was wearied by the restlessness of Rome: then too the war of Agis, king of the Spartans, was being waged in Greece, that of Alexander, king of Epirus, in Lucania, and that of Zopyrion, the prefect, in Scythia. Of these, Agis the Lacedaemonian, having roused all Greece and made it rebel with him, met the very strong forces of Antipater and fell himself amid great slaughter on both sides. Alexander, in Italy, aspiring to the empire of the west in rivalry with Alexander the Great, after numerous and heavy wars waged there, was overcome by the Bruttii and Lucanians, and his body was sold for burial. And Zopyrion, the prefect of Pontus, having gathered an army of thirty thousand, dared to make war on the Scythians, and was cut down to extermination and wiped out utterly with all his forces.

So Alexander the Great, after the death of Darius, subdued the Hyrcanians and the Mandi. There too, while he was still intent on war, Halestris or Minothea, a wanton Amazon, came to him with three hundred women, roused by the wish to have offspring by him. After this he attacked the Parthians in battle, who, long resisting, he almost destroyed before he conquered; then he subdued the Drangae, Euergetae, Parimae, Parapameni, Adaspii and the other peoples who lived at the foot of the Caucasus, founding there a city, Alexandria, on the river Tanais. But his cruelty towards his own was no less than his rage against the enemy. This is shown by his cousin Amyntas killed, his stepmother and her brothers put to death, Parmenion and Philotas massacred, Attalus, Eurylochus, Pausanias and many leading men of Macedonia done away with, and Clitus too, heavy with years, an old friend, wickedly killed. When, at a banquet, trusting in the king's friendship, Clitus defended the memory of the father against the king, who was putting his own deeds above those of his father Philip, he was run through with a hunting spear by the king, offended in vain, and as he died he bloodied the common banquet. But Alexander, insatiable of human blood, whether of enemies or even of allies, yet always thirsted for fresh gore. So, rushing into wars with stubborn force, he received the surrender of the Chorasmi and the Dahae, an untamed people. He killed the philosopher Callisthenes, his fellow pupil under Aristotle, together with very many other leading men, because, setting aside the custom of greeting, he did not adore him as a god.

## 3.19

After this he made for India, so as to bound his empire by the Ocean and the farthest east. He came to the city of Nysa; he stormed the Daedalian mountains and the kingdom of Queen Cleophylis, who, when she had surrendered, bought back her kingdom by lying with him. When Alexander, having traversed and subdued India, came to a rock of wonderful roughness and height, to which many peoples had fled, he learned that Hercules had been prevented from storming this same rock by an earthquake. Moved by rivalry to surpass the deeds of Hercules, he gained the rock with the greatest labour and danger and received the surrender of all the peoples of the place. He waged a most bloody war with Porus, the bravest king of the Indians. In it Alexander met Porus himself in single combat, and when his horse was killed and he was thrown, he escaped present death by the rush of his guards; Porus was pierced with many wounds and captured. Restoring him to his kingdom as a testimony to his valour, Alexander founded there two cities, Nicaea and Bucephala, which he ordered to be called by that name from the name of his horse. Then the Macedonians stormed the Adrestae, Catheni, Praesidae and Gangaridae, after cutting down their armies. When they came to the Cophides they joined battle there against two hundred thousand enemy horse; and since, worn down now by age, sick in spirit and weary in strength, they won with difficulty, they built a camp more magnificent than usual as a memorial. From there Alexander went on to the river Acesines; by it he was carried down into the Ocean. There he crushed the Gesonae and Sibi, whom Hercules founded. From here he sailed to the Mandri and Subagrae; these peoples met him with eighty thousand armed foot and sixty thousand horse. When battle was joined, a long, doubtful and bloody fight at last gave the Macedonians an almost sad victory. For when the enemy's forces had been routed, Alexander led his army to the city; and having been the first to climb the wall, thinking the city empty, he leaped down inside alone. When hostile enemies had surrounded him on every side, it is incredible to say how neither the multitude of the enemy, nor the great force of missiles, nor the great shouting of those attacking him terrified him: alone, he cut down and put to flight so many thousands. But when he felt that he was being overwhelmed by the multitude pouring round him, protecting his back with the barrier of the wall, he held off those in front more easily, until, at his danger and the shouting of the enemy, the whole army broke through the walls and burst in. In that battle he was pierced by an arrow below the breast, and fought on, on one knee, until he killed the man by whom he had been wounded. Then, boarding ships and travelling along the shores of the Ocean, he came to a certain city ruled by King Ambira. But in storming the city he lost a great part of his army to the enemy's arrows smeared with poison; and afterwards, when the others were helped by a herb shown to him in a dream and given as a drink to the wounded, he stormed and took the city.

## 3.20

Afterwards, having as it were rounded the turning post, he entered the river Indus from the Ocean and returned quickly to Babylon. There envoys from the terrified provinces of the whole world awaited him, that is from the Carthaginians and the cities of all Africa, but also from the Spaniards, the Gauls, Sicily and Sardinia, and moreover from the greater part of Italy. So great a fear of the general established in the farthest east had seized the peoples of the farthest west, that you could see from there an embassy travelling across the whole world to a place to which you would scarcely believe a rumour had reached. But Alexander, at Babylon, while still thirsting for blood with his greed ill restrained, drank poison through the plot of a servant and died.

O hard mind of men and heart ever inhuman! I myself, who review these things to establish the alternating calamity of all times, in relating so great an evil, at which the whole world trembled, either from his very death or from the fear of death it received: did I weep with my eyes? Did I grieve in my heart? Did I, going over these things, make the miseries of my ancestors my own, because of the common condition of living? Yet if ever I tell of myself, how I first saw unknown barbarians, how I avoided enemies, how I flattered those in power, how I guarded against the faithless, how I slipped away from those lying in wait, and lastly how, when they pursued me at sea and attacked me with stones and javelins, and were almost laying hands on me, I escaped, wrapped in a sudden mist, I would wish all who hear me to be moved to tears, and I would grieve silently for those who do not grieve, putting it down to the hardness of those who do not believe what they have not endured. The Spaniard and the Morinian came to Babylon to make supplication to Alexander, and of their own accord sought a bloody master through Assyria and India, so as not to receive him as an enemy, travelling round the ends of the earth and unhappily known to both Oceans: and yet the memory of so violent a necessity has either failed through forgetfulness or grown cheap with age. And do we think that it will cling to everlasting remembrance that, while the greatest part of the world was safe, a fleeing robber violated one corner? As if indeed, not to say the Indian or the Assyrian the other way round, but even the Spaniard himself, who suffers the enemy, had begged for peace from the Goths and Suebi. But if those times of Alexander are judged worthy of praise for the valour by which the whole world was won, rather than of loathing for the ruin by which the whole world was overthrown, very many will be found now too who think these things worthy of praise, because they have conquered much, and who count the miseries of others their own happiness. But someone may say: these are enemies of Romania. The answer will be: this too seemed so of Alexander to the whole east then, and the Romans too seemed such to others while they attacked peaceful peoples unknown to them with wars. But those sought to acquire kingdoms, these to overthrow them. The destructions of an enemy and the judgements of a victor are different things. For those too first afflicted with wars the peoples whom afterwards they ordered with their laws; and these now disturb in hostile fashion what, if they held it subdued (which God forbid), they would try to order after their own fashion, to be called great kings by posterity, who are now judged by us to be the most savage enemies. By whatever name these deeds of this kind are reckoned, that is whether they are called miseries or valour, both are smaller at this time compared with the former, and so both are in our favour in comparison with Alexander and the Persians: if it is now to be called valour, the enemy's is smaller; if misery, that of the Romans is smaller.

## 3.21

In the 450th year from the founding of the city, in the consulship of Fabius Maximus for the fifth time and Decius Mus for the fourth, four of the bravest and most flourishing peoples of Italy came together into one column and alliance. For the Etruscans, Umbrians, Samnites and Gauls, conspiring in one column, tried to destroy the Romans. The spirits of the Romans were terrified by this war and their confidence shaken, nor did they dare to hope for everything from their strength: they divided the enemy by a trick, thinking it safer to entangle themselves in more wars than in heavy ones. So, having sent some of their men ahead to lay waste the enemy's lands in Umbria and Etruria, and having forced the army of the Umbrians and Etruscans to return to protect their own, they hastened to engage in war with the Samnites and Gauls. In that war, when the Romans were pressed by the charge of the Gauls, the consul Decius was killed. Fabius, however, after great slaughter of Decius's part, at last won. In that battle forty thousand Samnites or Gauls are said to have been killed, while of the Romans seven thousand perished, from the part of Decius only, who was killed. Livy reports that, apart from the Etruscans and Umbrians, whom the Romans drew off from the war by cunning, there were 140,330 foot of the Gauls and Samnites and 47,000 horse, and that a thousand chariot fighters stood in arms against the Roman line.

But (as has often been said, the domestic quiet of the Romans was always interrupted by foreign wars, or their foreign successes weighed down by inward diseases, only so that their great spirits might be pressed in every way from every side) a pestilence of the city burdened this bloody and sad victory, and the funerals of the dead coming to meet them defiled the triumphal processions. Nor was there anyone who could be persuaded to rejoice at the triumph, when the whole city was sighing either for the sick or for the dead.

## 3.22

There follows a year in which the Romans, the war being renewed by the Samnites, were defeated and fled into their camp. But afterwards the Samnites, taking on a new appearance and spirit, that is with silvered arms and garments, and with minds prepared to die unless they won, offered themselves for battle. Against them the consul Papirius was sent with an army; when he was forbidden to engage by the chicken-keeping augurs, guessing at empty things, he laughed at them and finished the war as happily as he had steadfastly taken it up. For in this battle twelve thousand of the enemy are said to have been killed and three thousand captured. But this victory too, truly praiseworthy, which empty auspices could not hinder, was spoiled by diseases that suddenly arose. For so great and so intolerable a pestilence then seized the city that, in order to calm it by whatever means, they thought the Sibylline books should be consulted, and they brought over that dreadful Epidaurian snake together with the very stone of Aesculapius: as if indeed the pestilence had not been calmed before, or had not arisen afterwards.

Moreover, in the second year from this, the consul Fabius Gurges fought badly against the Samnites. For he lost his army and, defeated, fled back to the city. So when the senate was deliberating about removing him, his father Fabius Maximus, begging off his son's disgrace, offered of his own accord to go as his son's legate, if his son were given the opportunity of driving off the disgrace and waging the war again. When this was obtained and battle joined, when he suddenly saw his son the consul, while fighting, shut in by Pontius, the general of the Samnites, pressing on him and by the hostile missiles of the enemy, the loyal old man rode on his horse into the middle of the column. Stirred by this, the Romans threw their whole line in there, until, when the enemy army had been destroyed, they captured Pontius the general himself, defeated and overwhelmed. In that battle twenty thousand Samnites were killed and four thousand captured with their king; and at last the Samnite war, which had been dragged out for forty-nine years with much disaster to the Romans, was ended by the helplessness that came from the capture of their general.

In the following year a war was waged with the Sabines under the consul Curius, in which how many thousands of men were killed and how many captured the consul himself showed. For when he wished to report in the senate the size of the Sabine land acquired and the multitude of the people captured, he could not express the number.

In the 463rd year from the founding of the city, in the consulship of Dolabella and Domitius, when the Lucanians, the Bruttii and the Samnites too, having made an alliance with the Etruscans and the Senonian Gauls, were preparing a reviving war against the Romans, the Romans sent envoys to win over the Gauls. When the Gauls had killed them, the praetor Caecilius, sent with an army to avenge the murder of the envoys and to crush the rising of the enemy, was overwhelmed by the Etruscans and Gauls and perished. Seven military tribunes were also killed in that fight, many nobles slaughtered, and thirteen thousand Roman soldiers too were laid low in that war.

So whenever the Gauls blazed up, Rome was worn down in all its strength, so that under the present raiding of the Goths it ought rather to remember the Gauls.

## 3.23

But I am now called back, so that through these same times in which the Romans suffered these things I may unroll what wars the generals of the Macedonians waged among themselves, who, when Alexander was dead, drew by lot different provinces and consumed themselves in mutual wars. Their most tumultuous time I seem to myself to look upon as if, watching some immense camp by night from the lookout of a mountain, I could see nothing in the great space of the plain but countless fires. So throughout the whole kingdom of Macedonia, that is throughout all Asia and the greatest part of Europe and of Libya, dreadful balls of war suddenly blazed out together; and while they laid waste above all the places in which they flared up, they disturbed all the rest with the terror of rumour, as if with the darkness of smoke. But I shall by no means unfold the wars and destructions of so many kings and kingdoms unless I first set out the kingdoms themselves with their kings.

So Alexander for twelve years pressed with the sword the world trembling beneath him, and his leading men tore it apart for fourteen years, and, like greedy cubs over a rich prey laid low by a great lion, they pulled it to pieces and broke one another, roused to a quarrel by rivalry over the prey. So first Egypt and part of Africa and Arabia fell by lot to Ptolemy. Syria, bordering this province, was received by Laomedon of Mytilene, Cilicia by Philotas, the Illyrians by Philo. Atropatus was put over Greater Media, the father-in-law of Perdiccas over the lesser. The Susian people was assigned to Scynus, Greater Phrygia to Antigonus, son of Philip. Nearchus received Lycia and Pamphylia by lot, Cassander Caria, Menander Lydia. Leonnatus received Lesser Phrygia. Thrace and the regions of the Pontic Sea were given to Lysimachus, Cappadocia with Paphlagonia to Eumenes. The chief command of the camp fell to Seleucus, son of Antiochus; Cassander, son of Antipater, was put in charge of the king's bodyguards and attendants. In farther Bactriana and the regions of India the earlier prefects, who had begun to be there under Alexander, remained. Taxiles held the Seres, settled between the two rivers Hydaspes and Indus. Python, son of Agenor, was sent to the colonies founded among the Indians. Oxyarches received the Parapameni at the end of the Caucasus mountain. The Arachosians and Gedrosians were assigned to Sibyrtius. Statanor received the Drangae and Arei by lot, Amyntas the Bactrians, Scythaeus the Sogdians, Stacanor the Parthians, Philip the Hyrcanians, Phrataphernes the Armenians, Tleptolemus the Persians, Peucestes the Babylonians, Archon the Pelasgians, and Archelaus obtained Mesopotamia.

So the cause and origin of the wars was a letter of King Alexander, in which he ordered all exiles to be restored to their country and freedom. For the powerful men of the cities of Greece, fearing that the exiles, having recovered their freedom, would plan vengeance, revolted from the kingdom of the Macedonians. First the Athenians, having gathered an army of thirty thousand and two hundred ships, waged war with Antipater, to whom Greece had fallen by lot. Through the orator Demosthenes too they joined Sicyon, Argos, Corinth and the other cities to themselves as allies, and surrounded Antipater with a siege. There their general Leosthenes was pierced by a missile thrown from the walls and killed. The Athenians met Leonnatus, who was bringing help to Antipater, broke his forces and killed him.

Perdiccas made war on Ariarathes, king of the Cappadocians, and defeated him. In this victory he gained nothing but wounds and dangers; for all of them, before the storming of their city, set fire to their houses and burned themselves and all their possessions.

After this war arose between Antigonus and Perdiccas, with many provinces and islands most grievously torn apart because of help either refused or given. It was long debated whether the war should be carried into Macedonia or waged in Asia; at last Perdiccas himself made for Egypt with a great army. So Macedonia, with its generals running off in two directions, armed itself against its own vitals. Ptolemy, equipped with the strength of Egypt and the forces of the Cyrenians, prepared to meet Perdiccas in war. Meanwhile Neoptolemus and Eumenes fought it out with each other in a most bloody battle. Neoptolemus, defeated, fled to Antipater, whom he urged hard to crush Eumenes unexpectedly; Eumenes, thinking this would happen, caught those lying in ambush in an ambush of his own. In that war Polyperchon was killed; Neoptolemus and Eumenes were pierced by each other's wounds, but Neoptolemus died and Eumenes escaped as victor. Perdiccas, having met Ptolemy in a most bitter war, lost his forces and was himself killed too. Eumenes, Python, Illyrius and Alcetas, the brother of Perdiccas, were declared enemies by the Macedonians, and the war against them was assigned to Antigonus.

So Eumenes and Antigonus, bringing very great forces against each other, clashed. Eumenes, defeated, fled to a certain very strongly fortified castle, from which he asked through envoys for the help of Antipater, then very powerful; frightened by this news Antigonus withdrew from the siege. But not even so was Eumenes's hope firm or his safety certain. So, as a last resort, he asked for help from the Argyraspides, so called from their silvered arms, that is, the soldiers who had served under Alexander. They, listening disdainfully to their general in arranging the war, were defeated by Antigonus and deprived of their camp, and lost their wives and children together with everything they had acquired under Alexander. Afterwards, through envoys, they shamefully begged the victor that what they had lost be returned to them. Antigonus promised to return it if they would drag Eumenes to him in bonds. So, enticed by the hope of recovery, by a most disgraceful betrayal they, captives themselves, led their general, whose standards they had followed a little before, captured and chained; and soon, with the foulest ignominy, they were scattered through the army of Antigonus. Meanwhile Eurydice, the wife of Arridaeus, king of the Macedonians, did many wicked things under her husband's name through Cassander, whom she had known shamefully and had raised through all the ranks of honours to the highest summit; and he, at the woman's whim, afflicted many cities of Greece. Then Olympias, the mother of King Alexander, urged on by Polyperchon, when she was coming from Epirus into Macedonia escorted by Aeacides, king of the Molossians, and was kept from its borders by Eurydice, with the Macedonians' support ordered King Arridaeus and Eurydice to be killed. Yet Olympias herself also at once paid the deserved penalty for her cruelty. For when, with a woman's boldness, she carried out many killings of leading men, on hearing of the arrival of Cassander, distrusting the Macedonians, she withdrew with her daughter-in-law Roxane and her grandson Hercules into the city of Pydna, where she was soon captured by Cassander and killed. The son of Alexander the Great was sent with his mother to the citadel of Amphipolis to be guarded.

When Perdiccas, Alcetas, Polyperchon and the other generals of the opposing party, whom it would be long to recall, had been killed, the wars among the successors of Alexander seemed to be ended; but Antigonus, burning with lust for domination, pretended that Hercules, the king's son, must be freed from his confinement by war. Learning this, Ptolemy and Cassander, having made an alliance with Lysimachus and Seleucus, prepared war by land and sea with all their effort. Antigonus was defeated in that war together with his son Demetrius. Cassander, who had been made Ptolemy's partner in the victory, as he was returning to Apollonia, fell in with the Abienatae, who, because of an unbearable multitude of frogs and mice, had left their native soil and ancient dwellings and were migrating, seeking new homes under a show of peace for the time being. But Cassander, recognising both the valour and the number of the people, so that they should not, driven by necessity, shake and invade Macedonia with war, received them into alliance and settled them on the farthest borders of Macedonia. Then, when Hercules, Alexander's son, was already fourteen years old, fearing that all would prefer him as the lawful king, he secretly saw to it that he was killed together with his mother.

Ptolemy again clashed with Demetrius in a naval battle, and having lost almost his whole fleet and army, he fled back defeated to Egypt. Elated by this victory, Antigonus ordered himself to be called king together with his son Demetrius; all followed this example and took the royal name and dignity for themselves. So when Ptolemy, Cassander and the other generals of the other faction saw that they were being deceived by Antigonus one by one, confirming one another by letters they agreed on a time and place for coming together and prepared war against Antigonus with common forces. Cassander, entangled in wars with his neighbours, sent Lysimachus, the most famous general of all, with a great force to help his allies in his place. Seleucus too, coming down from Greater Asia, joined as a new enemy of Antigonus. For this Seleucus waged very many wars throughout the east among the partners of the Macedonian kingdom. At the beginning he stormed and took Babylon in war. He subdued the Bactrians, rising up in new movements. Then he crossed into India, which after the death of Alexander, as if the yoke had been pulled off and shaken from its neck, had killed his prefects, under a certain Androcottus as leader in claiming its liberty. Afterwards, acting cruelly towards his fellow citizens, he himself pressed into slavery those whom he had defended from foreign domination. Though Seleucus waged many heavy wars with this Androcottus, at last, the terms of the kingdom being settled and peace made, he withdrew.

So, when the forces of Ptolemy and his allies had been united, battle was joined: and the more powerful its preparation, the heavier its ruin; for in it the strength of almost the whole Macedonian kingdom then fell. Antigonus was killed in that war; but the end of this war was the beginning of another. For since the victors did not agree about the booty, they were again drawn apart into two factions: Seleucus joined Demetrius, Ptolemy Lysimachus; when Cassander died his son Philip succeeded. So new wars of Macedonia were born as if afresh.

Antipater, with his own hand, ran through his mother Thessalonice, the wife of Cassander, though she begged pitiably for her life. His brother Alexander, while he was preparing war against his brother to avenge their mother, was trapped and killed by Demetrius, whose help he had asked. Lysimachus, since he was pressed by a most hostile war with Dorus, king of the Thracians, could not fight against Demetrius. Demetrius, elated by the gain of Greece and of all Macedonia, arranged to cross into Asia. But Ptolemy, Seleucus and Lysimachus, having learned in the earlier contest how great is the strength of concord, again made an alliance, united their armies and carried the war into Europe against Demetrius. Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, joined them as companion and ally in the war, hoping that Demetrius could be driven out of Macedonia. Nor was the hope in vain: for when Demetrius's army had been corrupted and he himself put to flight, Pyrrhus seized the kingdom of Macedonia. Then Lysimachus killed his son-in-law Antipater, who was plotting against him, and killed his own son Agathocles, hating him beyond human custom.

In these days the city of Lysimachia was overthrown by a most dreadful earthquake, and was a cruel tomb for its people crushed beneath it. All his allies deserted Lysimachus, who was staining himself with continual murders of his kin, and, going over to Seleucus, urged that king, already inclined by rivalry for the kingdom, to make war on Lysimachus. It was a most foul spectacle: two kings, of whom Lysimachus was seventy-four years old and Seleucus seventy-seven, clashing to snatch each other's kingdoms, standing in the battle line, bearing arms. This was indeed the last war of the fellow soldiers of Alexander, but one that seems to have been kept as an example of human misery: for when, thirty-four of Alexander's generals being already dead, they alone possessed the world, and not looking at the very narrow limits of their old age and of their lives, they thought the limits of the whole world narrow for their empire. In that war Lysimachus, having lost or killed fifteen children before this battle, was himself killed last. So Lysimachus was the end of the Macedonian fighting. But not even Seleucus rejoiced in so great a victory with impunity. For neither did he find, after seventy-seven years, the rest of a natural death, but ended the life unhappily wrung from him as if by an untimely death: for at the instance of Ptolemy, whose sister Lysimachus had had in marriage, he was trapped in an ambush and killed.

Such were the dealings of kinship and alliance among parents, children, brothers and allies. So much was religion, divine and human, valued among them. Let those indeed blush at the remembrance of the past who now know that, by the intervention of the Christian faith alone and only by the mediation of a sworn oath, they live with enemies and do not suffer hostile treatment: by which it is proved beyond all doubt that, not as they did before, when they joined treaties over a slaughtered sow, but now, between barbarians and Romans, calling to witness their creator and Lord, with the Gospels brought for the oath, they keep a faith as great as nature could not then keep even between parents and children.

Now let the end of the Macedonian war be the end of the book too, especially since from here the wars of Pyrrhus begin and the Punic wars soon follow.

## 4.praef

Virgil tells that Aeneas, when after his own dangers and the shipwrecks of his people he was hardly comforting his remaining companions, said: "Perhaps one day it will please you to remember even these things." This saying, once aptly made, always brings back its threefold force with the most different effects. For past things are held the more pleasing in words the heavier they are told to have been in deeds; and future things, while they become desirable through weariness of the present, are always believed to be better; but to present things no just comparison of miseries can be applied in any part, for this reason: that these things that are, however small they may be, afflict us with much greater trouble than those that, whether past or to come, though they are called great, meanwhile do not exist at all. As if someone, tickled by fleas at night and anxious with wakefulness because of it, should perhaps remember other wakeful nights that he once long endured from the most burning fevers: beyond doubt he will bear the disquiet of these more impatiently than the remembrance of those. But though it may seem so to the feelings of all according to the capacity of the times, will anyone nevertheless be found who, even in that very anxiety, would declare that fleas are heavier than fevers? Or would take it more bitterly that he is awake and well than that, about to die, he could not sleep? Since this is so, I grant in some sort to those delicate and querulous people of ours that they may think these things, by which we are now sometimes admonished (because it is expedient so), heavy in the feeling; yet I do not wink at their also asserting that they are heavier by comparison. Just as if someone, going out in the morning from the softest coverlets and a most comfortable bedroom, should see that the surfaces of the pools had stiffened with the night's frost and the grass grown white, and, reminded by the unexpected sight, should say "it is cold today": he would seem to me in no way to be blamed, since he would have spoken either by common usage or by his own feeling. But if, running back trembling into his bedroom and covering himself, or rather hiding himself, in the coverlets, he should cry out that the cold was altogether as great as it had never been even in the Apennines, when Hannibal, shut in and overwhelmed by the snows, lost his elephants, horses and the greater part of his army: I would not only not bear him saying these things, sickening with childish indulgences, but I would even drag him from his very coverlets, the witnesses of his idleness, out to the people and the public, and, when he had been brought out of doors, I would show him children playing, delighting and sweating in that very frost and from it; so that his wordy triviality, spoiled by delicate nurture, might be taught that the violence is not in the time but the sluggishness in himself, and in the comparison of things to be judged, it might be proved, not that his elders bore small things, but that he himself is not able to bear even small things.

This I shall prove more plainly by recalling to their memory the disasters of the past, setting out first, as the order requires, the war of Pyrrhus. Its cause and origin was this.

## 4.1

In the 464th year from the founding of the city the Tarentines, seeing from the theatre where they watched the show a Roman fleet that happened to be passing by, attacked it as enemies; only five ships barely escaped by flight. The rest of the fleet was dragged into the harbour and destroyed; the captains of the ships were massacred, all those fit for war killed and the rest sold for a price. At once envoys were sent to Tarentum by the Romans to complain of the wrongs done; they were beaten by the same people and brought back wrongs increased besides. From these causes a great war arose. The Romans, measuring who and how great were the enemies clamouring round them, were driven by the last necessity to force the proletarii too into arms, that is, to enrol for military service those who were always left free in the city for the sake of supplying offspring: for care for offspring is in vain unless present affairs are seen to. So the Roman army, with the consul Aemilius, burst into the whole territory of the Tarentines, laid everything waste with fire and sword, stormed very many towns, and cruelly avenged the wrong insolently received. At once the Tarentines, supported by very many garrisons of their neighbours, were strengthened above all by Pyrrhus, who, because of the greatness of his strength and counsels, drew the supreme command and the name of the war onto himself. For, intending to defend Tarentum as a city founded by the Lacedaemonians and kindred to Greece, he brought into Italy the whole strength of Epirus, Thessaly and Macedonia, and was also the first to bring elephants, until then unseen by the Romans, twenty in number. He was terrible by land and sea, with men and horses, arms and beasts, and lastly with his own strength and tricks; except that, trapped by the ambiguous answer of that most empty spirit of Delphi, that most lying rascal whom they themselves call the great prophet, he came to the end of one who had not consulted it. So at Heraclea, a city of Campania, and the river Liris, the first battle was joined between King Pyrrhus and the consul Laevinus. The day was spent in a most heavy contest, all on both sides intent on dying and not knowing how to flee. But when the Romans saw the elephants brought in between the clashing columns, savage in appearance, heavy in smell, terrible in bulk, surrounded and terrified by a new kind of fighting, with the horses above all panicking, they scattered. But after Minucius, first hastatus of the fourth legion, cut off with his sword the trunk the beast stretched out at him, and forced it, maddened by the pain of the wound, to turn from the battle and rage against its own side, and they began to be thrown into disorder and confusion by its unrestrained running about, an end was put to the fight, with the help of night too. That the Romans had been defeated was shown by their shameful flight: of them there are said to have fallen then 14,880 foot, with 1,310 captured; of horse 246 were killed and 802 captured; twenty-two standards were lost. How great a number of Pyrrhus's allies on the other side was wiped out has not been handed down to memory, above all because it is the custom of the old writers not to record the number of the slain on the side that won, so that the losses of the victor should not stain the glory of the victory; unless perhaps so few fall that the smallness of the losses increases the wonder and the terror of their valour, as it was in the first encounter of the Persian war with Alexander the Great: of whom it is said that, among about four hundred thousand of the enemy killed, only nine foot soldiers were missing from his army. But Pyrrhus testified to his gods and to men the savagery of the disaster that he had received in this war by fixing an inscription in the temple of Tarentine Jupiter, in which he wrote this: "Men who were before unconquered, best father of Olympus, these I conquered in battle, and was conquered by them." And when he was reproached by his allies for calling himself conquered when he had conquered, he is said to have answered: "Truly, if I conquer again in the same way, I shall return to Epirus without a single soldier." Meanwhile the Roman army, after it had fled secretly from its camp, defeated, felt the wretched disaster of the war increased and heaped up by heavier portents. For a storm arose, as if hostile, with a horrible crashing of the sky, and seized the foragers who had happened to go out and burned them with dreadful thunderbolts. For the same whirlwind laid low thirty-four of them; twenty-two were left half-dead; very many beasts of burden were killed and taken; so that it may rightly be said to have happened not as a sign of a coming devastation but as the devastation itself.

The second battle between Pyrrhus and the Roman consuls was on the borders of Apulia. There the disaster of war fell to both, but above all to Pyrrhus, and the victory to the Romans. For when for a long time, with all stubbornly rushing into mutual slaughter, the outcome of the war hung in doubt, Pyrrhus, wounded with his arm pierced through, was the first to leave the battle. But Fabricius, the legate, was also wounded then. The elephants, found in the first battle to be able to be wounded and put to flight, were then harried with fires set beneath their hindquarters and soft parts, and carrying round the burning machines on their backs in their frightened fury, they were the destruction of their own side. In that battle five thousand Romans were killed, while of Pyrrhus's army twenty thousand were laid low. Fifty-three of the king's standards were taken, and eleven of the Romans' lost.

Pyrrhus, broken by the war, was summoned to the rule of Sicily on the death of Agathocles, king of Syracuse, and went to Syracuse.

## 4.2

But the misery of the Romans ceases with no truces; the interval of wars is consumed by the evils of diseases, and when there is a rest from battle abroad, the anger of heaven is at work within. For in the consulship of Fabius Gurges, for the second time, and C. Genucius Clepsina, a heavy pestilence invaded the city and its territory. It seized everyone, but above all women and cattle, and, killing the young in the womb, emptied them of future offspring; and abortions were wrenched out in untimely births, with danger to the mothers, and cast away, so that it was believed that the succession would fail and the race of living things would come to an end, the lawful order of birth taken away.

Meanwhile the consul Curius met Pyrrhus on his return from Sicily, and this third war against the Epirotes was fought in Lucania on the Arusine plains. So at the first clash, when the soldiers of Pyrrhus were trembling at the charge of the Romans and, looking round for flight, were trying to give way in the battle, Pyrrhus ordered the elephants to be brought up from the reserves. The Romans, now used to fighting with the beasts, had prepared fire-darts wrapped in tow and smeared with pitch, made to cling besides by hooked barbs; and when they hurled these, set alight, at the backs of the beasts and the towers, without difficulty they turned the raging and burning beasts back to the destruction of those whose support they had been. They say the king had eighty thousand foot in that battle and six thousand horse. Of these 33,000 are said to have been killed and 1,300 captured. Pyrrhus, in only the fifth year after he had come, fled from Italy defeated. After many very heavy wars that he waged, he fell in Greece at Argos, a most flourishing city of Achaea, struck by a stone, led on by greed for the Spartan kingdom.

Then too among the Romans the Vestal virgin Sextilia, convicted and condemned of unchastity, was buried alive at the Colline Gate.

## 4.3

In the 475th year from the founding of the city the Tarentines, having learned of the death of Pyrrhus, again stirred up new arms against the Romans, and asked, through envoys, for help from the Carthaginians and received it. When battle was joined the Romans won: and in it the Carthaginians, though not yet judged enemies, already then felt that they could be defeated by the Romans.

In the following year Roman severity cut down a great part of its own vitals. For long before, at the approach of Pyrrhus, the eighth legion, distrusting the hope of Rome, dared a new crime: it killed all the people of Rhegium, whom it was guarding as a garrison, and claimed all the booty and the town itself for itself. The punishment of this crime in such wicked deserters was ordered to the consul Genucius. He besieged the city of the Rhegians and captured all of them; he himself inflicted fitting punishments on the rest, the deserters and robbers, while he sent to Rome the Roman soldiers of the whole legion, who by order of the people were beaten with rods in the middle of the forum and struck with the axe.

Rome seemed to itself then to conquer, when it killed its own whole legion, which would beyond doubt have been counted defeated if it had lost it in a battle with an enemy.

## 4.4

In the 478th year from the founding of the city foul and dreadful prodigies were either seen at Rome or reported. The temple of Salus was destroyed by a stroke of lightning, and part of the wall below the same place was, as they say, struck from heaven. Three wolves, entering the city before dawn, brought in a half-eaten corpse, and, scattered limb by limb in the forum, they left it themselves, terrified by the noise of men. At Formiae the walls were burned and destroyed on every side by many strokes of lightning. In the territory of Cales a flame, suddenly belched out through a gap split in the earth, boiling up terribly for three days and three nights, dried five iugera of land into ashes, the sap of its fertility utterly exhausted, so that it is said to have consumed not only the crops but also the trees with their deepest roots.

In the following year the consul Sempronius led an army against the Picentes. And when both lines, drawn up, had halted within a spear's throw, suddenly the earth trembled with such a horrible crashing that both columns, terrified with amazement at the wonder, grew dull. For a long time the peoples on both sides hesitated, astonished, with a foreboding consciousness of their undertaking; at last, with a swift charge, they entered the contest. That war was so sad that it may rightly be said that the earth then trembled with a horrible groan, about to receive so much human blood. Of the Romans in that battle the very few who escaped were victors.

## 4.5

In the 480th year from the founding of the city, among many prodigies, blood was seen to flow from the earth and from the sky. For in very many places gore flowed, welling up from the springs; and from the clouds, with milk let fall in drops in the likeness of rain, showers, dreadful as it seemed to them, watered the earth. At that time the Carthaginians, when they were charged by the senate through envoys for having given help to the Tarentines against the Romans, heaped a shameful perjury, taken in advance, on the most shameful stain of a broken treaty.

Then too the Volsinienses, the most flourishing of the Etruscans, almost perished through luxury. For when, with licence extended into habit, they freed their slaves everywhere, enrolled them in their banquets and honoured them with marriages, the freedmen, admitted to a share of power, planned to usurp the whole of it by crime; and, freed from the yoke of slavery, they burned with ambition for domination; and those whom as subjects they had loved with an even mind as masters, they now, as free men, cursed because they remembered they had been masters. So the freedmen, conspiring in the crime (and their band was so great that they gained what they dared without dispute), seized the city and claimed it for their own kind alone, usurped by crime the estates and the wives of their masters, and drove the masters far off as exiles. These, wretched, exiled and destitute, betook themselves to Rome, where, having displayed their misery and wept out their complaint, they were both avenged and restored through the severity of the Romans.

In the 481st year from the founding of the city a great pestilence blazed up at Rome, whose savagery I am content to indicate, since I cannot fill it out in words. For if the length of time it lasted is asked, it stretched on, laying waste, beyond two years; if the depopulation it caused, a census was declared which asked not how many men had perished but how many had survived; if the violence with which it afflicted, the Sibylline books are witnesses, which replied that it had been imposed by the anger of heaven. But lest it offend anyone as a temptation to cavil, that, while the Sibyl said that the gods were angry, we seem to have said the anger of heaven, let him hear and understand that these things, though they are mostly done through the powers of the air, yet are by no means done without the judgement of almighty God.

At the same time Caparronia, a Vestal virgin, accused of unchastity, perished by hanging; her seducer and the slaves who knew of it were punished.

See how many and how great things we number as having happened in succession in single years, among which certainly rarely or almost never was nothing sad done; and this although the same writers, having set themselves rather the business of praising, avoided the multitude of miseries, so as not to offend those for whom and about whom these things were written, and so as not to seem to frighten their hearers with examples of the past rather than to instruct them. Further, we, placed at the end of time, cannot know the evils of the Romans except through those who praised the Romans. From this it may be understood how great were those things that were deliberately suppressed because of their horror, when such great things are found that could slip out thinly among praises.

## 4.6

And since from here on the Punic wars follow, the matter itself demands that at least a few things be told of Carthage, which is found to have been founded by Elissa seventy-two years before the city of Rome, and of its disasters and domestic evils, as Pompeius Trogus and Justin set them out. The Carthaginians always had among themselves a native and inward evil, discord, which unhappily driving them on, they never passed any times either prosperous abroad or quiet at home. But when, among other evils, they were also suffering from pestilence, they used murders as remedies: for they sacrificed men like victims, and brought to the altars children of an age below puberty, which would provoke the pity even of enemies.

About this kind of sacred rites, or rather sacrileges, I do not find what should best be discussed. For if any demons dared to command rites of this kind, that the deaths of men should be satisfied by the killing of men, it should have been understood that they were hired as workers and helpers of the pestilence, so as themselves to kill those whom it had not seized: for it is the custom that sound and unblemished victims be offered, so that they did not calm the diseases but anticipated them.

So the Carthaginians, the gods being turned away because of rites of this kind (as Pompeius Trogus and Justin confess, but, as is established among us, God being angry because of their own presumption and impiety), after fighting long and unhappily in Sicily, carried the war into Sardinia and were again defeated, more unhappily. For this they ordered their general Mazeus and the few soldiers who had survived to go into exile. The exiles, asking for pardon through envoys and being refused, surrounded their country with war and siege. There Mazeus, the leader of the exiles, hung on a cross, under the eyes of his country, just as he was, with his purple and his fillets, his son Carthalo, a priest of Hercules, because he had come to meet him in purple as if insulting him. A few days later he took the city itself; and when, having killed very many of the senators, he was ruling bloodily, he was killed. These things were done in the times of Cyrus, king of the Persians.

After this Himilco, king of the Carthaginians, while he was waging war in Sicily, suddenly lost his army through a horrible plague. Nor was there delay: as the people fell in crowds from the diseases, each one was quickly seized, soon dead, and now was not even buried. When news of this evil had filled Carthage, stunned with sudden mourning, the city was thrown into confusion just as if it had been captured. Everything resounded with wailing; doors were shut everywhere; all public and private business was given up; all ran down to the harbour and questioned the few who had survived the disaster, as they came out of the ships, about their own people. After the wretched people understood about the disaster of their own, while those men were silent or groaning, then the voices of mourners were heard along the whole shore, then the wailing and tearful complaints of unhappy mothers. Amid this the general himself came forward from his ship, ungirt, in a dirty, slave's tunic; at the sight of him the mourning columns joined together; he himself too, stretching his hands to heaven, accused and bewailed now his own, now the public misfortune; at last, crying aloud through the city, he finally entered his house, dismissed with a last address all who followed him weeping, and then, the doors barred and even his sons shut out, he ended his grief and his life with the sword. These things were done in the times of Darius.

After this Hanno, a certain Carthaginian, who surpassed the strength of the state with his private wealth, drank in the desire to seize domination. He thought it a useful plan for this to pretend the marriage of his only daughter and to kill with poison, over the cups, all the senators whose rank he judged would stand in the way of his undertakings. This was betrayed by the servants and avoided without vengeance, so that, in a powerful man, the thing known should not cause more trouble than the thing planned. Thwarted in this plan, Hanno prepared to attack the crime by another device: he roused the slaves, by whom he might suddenly crush the city off its guard. But when he understood that he had been betrayed and forestalled before the day fixed for the slaughter, he seized a certain fort with twenty thousand armed slaves. There, while he was rousing the Africans and the king of the Moors, he was captured, and first beaten with rods, then, with his eyes dug out and his hands and legs broken, as if a penalty were exacted from each limb, killed in the sight of the people. His body, torn by the blows, was fixed to a cross; his sons and all his kin were handed over to punishment, so that no one of the same family should ever plan to imitate or avenge him. These things were done in the times of Philip.

After this the Carthaginians, when they had learned that the city of Tyre, the author of their origin, had been taken and overthrown by Alexander the Great, fearing that his crossing into Africa would follow, sent a certain Hamilcar, surnamed Rhodanus, a man outstanding in eloquence and skill, to investigate the acts of Alexander. He was received through Parmenion as if a deserter, then admitted into the king's service, and reported everything to his fellow citizens by tablets written on and afterwards smeared over with wax. When he returned to Carthage after Alexander's death, they killed him, as if he had sold the city to the king, not only with an ungrateful spirit but with a cruel envy. Then, when they were waging continual and never sufficiently prosperous wars against the Sicilians, and had surrounded Syracuse, then the most flourishing city of Sicily, with a siege, they were trapped by the wonderful wit of Agathocles, king of Sicily, and brought to the extremity of despair. For when Agathocles was being besieged at Syracuse by the Carthaginians and saw that he was neither equal to them in war in the equipment of his forces nor able to endure a siege with sufficient supplies, by a plan well foreseen and better concealed he crossed into Africa with his army. There he revealed to his men what he was planning, then taught them what needed to be done. At once, with one mind, they first burned the ships in which they had come, so that there would be no hope of fleeing back. Then, while he was laying low everything towards which he directed his march and burning villas and forts, he met a certain Hanno with thirty thousand Carthaginians. He killed him with two thousand of his men, while he himself lost only two in that war. By this battle, the spirits of the Africans being incredibly broken and those of his own immensely increased, he stormed cities and forts, drove off great booty and slaughtered many thousands of the enemy. Then he pitched his camp at the fifth milestone from Carthage, so that they might watch from the walls of the city itself the losses of their richest possessions, the devastation of their fields and the burning of their villas. A sadder report was added to the present evils. For it was also reported that in Sicily the army of the Africans had been destroyed with its general; Andro, the brother of Agathocles, had in truth overwhelmed it while it was off its guard and almost idle. When this rumour had spread through all Africa, not only the tributary cities but even allied kings revolted from them. Among them Ophellas too, king of Cyrene, made a pact with Agathocles for partnership in the war, while he ardently aspired to the kingdom of Africa. But after they had joined their armies and camps into one, he was trapped by Agathocles with flatteries and ambushes and killed. The Carthaginians, gathering forces from every side, blazed out into war. Agathocles, having with him the forces of Ophellas, met them and overcame them in a heavy battle, with much blood of both armies. By the crisis of this contest such despair was brought on the Carthaginians that, had not a mutiny arisen in the army of Agathocles, Hamilcar, the general of the Carthaginians, would have deserted to him with his army. For this offence he was fixed to a gibbet in the middle of the forum by order of the Carthaginians and provided a cruel spectacle for his people. Then, when after the death of Agathocles the Carthaginians were laying waste Sicily with an equipped fleet, they were often overcome in land and naval contests by Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, summoned from Italy, and at last turned to the Roman wars.

Oh, the pity of it! Do those who complain about recent things read these things about the old? Indeed they read them, and they judge them not with fairness but with rivalry. For they are pricked by that very great and unspeakable goad, which they themselves do not discern, not because of the bad times but because of the Christian times; and it is the overflow of an envious ulcer, that whatever is done under what is cursed seems more dreadful. So too among us it is often wont to seem to the eyes of enemies that those whom they curse do nothing in word or deed that is not crooked, nothing that is not worthless, nothing that is not to their hurt: and this quite simply too, for envy so twists the captured heart that it does not see what is by nature straight. Of their number are these, but much more wretched, because they are enemies of God and so enemies of the truth (of whom we say these things weeping, and whom, if they allow it, we reprove in mercy so as to heal them): who see these things with a faulty eye, and so what they see seems double to them; and, confused by the darkness of wickedness, they fall into this, that by seeing less they see more, while yet they cannot see what is as it is. They think the scourges of a father heavier than the fires of an enemy; they call God, who coaxes, admonishes and redeems, harsher than the devil, who persecutes, lords it and slaughters. Yet if they understood it of a father, they would rejoice at the chastisement; and if the fruit of instruction were foreseen, the discipline would be bearable, and because of the hope that is now given to the nations, but before was not, they would think things lighter, even if they suffered heavier ones. And yet they can learn the scorning of miseries even from their own people, among whom the greatest evils were valued as the greatest goods, only so that they might attain a celebrated and illustrious glory of fame: from whom it may be gathered how much we, to whom a blessed eternity is promised, must bear for life, when they could bear so much for fame.

## 4.7

In the 483rd year from the founding of the city, that is in the consulship of Appius Claudius and Q. Fabius, the Romans sent help to the Mamertines, whose city was Messana, a famous city of Sicily, against Hiero, king of Syracuse, and the forces of the Carthaginians joined to Hiero, and sent the consul Appius Claudius with an army. He overcame the Syracusans and Carthaginians so quickly that the king himself, terrified by the greatness of events, confessed that he had been defeated before he had engaged. Then, his strength broken and his confidence lost, when he asked as a suppliant for peace, he received it, fined two hundred talents of silver by order of the consuls. The consuls surrounded Agrigentum, a city of Sicily, and the garrisons of the Carthaginians there with siege works and a rampart. And when the elder Hannibal, general of the Carthaginians, shut in by that siege, had been reduced to the utmost want, Hanno, a new general of the Carthaginians, with fifteen hundred horse and thirty thousand foot, and also thirty elephants, came between unexpectedly and put off the storming of the city for a little. But soon the city was taken: the Carthaginians were defeated and routed in a very great battle, eleven elephants were brought into their power, and all the people of Agrigentum were sold under the crown; the elder Hannibal made a sally with a few and fled.

In the consulship of Cn. Cornelius Asina and C. Duilius, when the elder Hannibal, with an equipped fleet of seventy ships, was laying waste the sea coast of Italy, the Romans too ordered a fleet to be built and equipped. The consul Duilius quickly carried this out: for within sixty days after the trees had been felled a fleet of a hundred and thirty ships had been launched and stood at anchor. Cornelius Asina, the other consul, made for the island of Lipara with sixteen ships. There, called out by Hannibal as if to a conference about peace, he was captured by Punic treachery and killed in chains. When Duilius, the other consul, heard this, he set out against Hannibal with thirty ships. When a naval battle was joined Hannibal, having lost the ship in which he was carried, slipped away in a skiff and fled; thirty-one of his ships were captured and thirteen sunk, three thousand men are said to have been killed and seven thousand captured.

Afterwards, in the consulship of C. Aquilius Florus and L. Cornelius Scipio, the Carthaginians put Hanno, appointed in the place of Hannibal, in command of a naval battle to defend the Sardinians and Corsicans. Defeated by the consul Scipio, having lost his army, he threw himself among the thickest of the enemy and was killed there. In the same year three thousand slaves and four thousand naval allies conspired for the destruction of the city of Rome, and had not a timely betrayal forestalled the plan, the city, bare of defence, would have perished by a band of slaves.

## 4.8

In the next year from this the consul Calatinus, making for Camerina, a city of Sicily, rashly led his army into narrows that the forces of the Carthaginians had long since blocked. When he had absolutely no means either of resisting or of escaping, he was freed by the valour and work of Calpurnius Flamma, who, with a picked band of three hundred men, seized a hill occupied by the enemy and, by fighting, turned all the Carthaginians on himself, until the Roman army passed through the blocked narrows with the enemy not pressing. All three hundred were killed in that action; only Calpurnius, though pierced with many wounds and covered with corpses, escaped. The elder Hannibal, again put in command of the fleet by the Carthaginians, met the Romans unhappily in a naval battle and, defeated, died covered with stones by his army in a mutiny that arose. The consul Atilius roamed through and overthrew Lipara and Melita, famous islands of Sicily. The consuls, ordered to carry the war into Africa, made for Sicily with three hundred and thirty ships. Hamilcar, the general of the Carthaginians, and Hanno, the commander of the fleet, met them. When a naval battle was joined, the Carthaginians were put to flight and lost sixty-four ships. The victorious consuls crossed into Africa and first of all received the city of Clupea in surrender. Then, making for Carthage, they laid waste three hundred forts or more and carried their hostile standards round Carthage. The consul Manlius, leaving Africa with the victorious fleet, brought back to Rome twenty-seven thousand captives with great spoils. Regulus, having drawn the Carthaginian war as his lot, while marching with his army, pitched his camp not far from the river Bagrada. There, since a serpent of wonderful size was devouring very many soldiers as they went down to the river from the need to draw water, Regulus set out with his army to overpower the beast. But since the javelins made no progress on its back, and every stroke of missiles was in vain (they slid off its horrid hurdle of scales as if off a slanting tortoise of shields, and in a wonderful way were driven back by the body itself so as not to wound it), and moreover he saw a great multitude crushed by its bite, trampled by its charge and even killed by its pestilent breath, he ordered ballistas to be brought, by which a wall-stone struck on its spine loosened the joining of its whole body. For such is the nature of the serpent that, though it seems to lack feet, it is yet so equipped with ribs and scales, which it has set in an equal fashion from the top of its throat to the bottom of its belly, that it leans on its scales as on claws and on its ribs as on legs. For it does not, like a worm, which has no stiffness of spine, unfold its motion by stretching forward in a straight line, step by step, the parts of its little body that were drawn together, and drawing together those that were stretched; but, straining with alternate efforts, it carries its winding sides round, so as to stretch the stiff line of its ribs along the outer curve of its spine, and with its ribs, which are by nature straight at the top, to fix the claws of its scales; and by doing this in turn and quickly it not only glides over level ground but even climbs slopes, furnished with as many footholds as it has ribs. This is the reason why, if it is crushed by any blow in any part of the body from the belly to the head, it is made weak and cannot move; because wherever that blow falls it loosens the spine, through which the feet of the ribs and the motions of the body were worked. Hence this serpent too, which had so long withstood so many javelins unwounded, gave way, weakened, at the blow of one stone, and soon, surrounded by missiles, was easily overwhelmed. Its skin, brought to Rome, which they say was a hundred and twenty feet in length, was for some while a wonder to all.

Regulus waged a most savage war against three generals, that is the two Hasdrubals and Hamilcar, summoned from Sicily, in which seventeen thousand Carthaginians were killed and five thousand captured, eighteen elephants were taken away, and eighty-two towns surrendered to the Romans.

## 4.9

The Carthaginians, broken by wars and emptied by disasters, asked Regulus for peace. But when they heard the intolerable and harsh terms of peace, thinking it safer to die armed than to live wretched, they thought they should buy with money not only the help of the Spaniards and Gauls, of whom they already had very many, but also of the Greeks. So they summoned Xanthippus, king of the Lacedaemonians, with auxiliaries, and put him in command of the war as general. Xanthippus, having inspected the forces of the Carthaginians and led them down into the plain, with their equipment changed far for the better, joined battle with the Romans. Great was the ruin of the Roman strength there: for thirty thousand Roman soldiers were laid low in that encounter. That famous general Regulus was captured with five hundred men, and, thrown into chains, in only the tenth year of the Punic war provided the Carthaginians with a famous triumph. Xanthippus, conscious of so bold a deed and fearing the change of unstable things, at once left Africa for Greece.

So the consuls Aemilius Paulus and Fulvius Nobilior, hearing of the captivity of Regulus and the disaster of the Roman army, and ordered to cross into Africa with a fleet of three hundred ships, made for Clupea. The Carthaginians came there at once with an equal fleet, nor could the naval contest be put off. A hundred and four ships of the Carthaginians were sunk and thirty captured with their fighting men, and besides thirty-five thousand of their soldiers were killed; of the Romans, nine ships having been sunk, eleven hundred soldiers perished. The consuls pitched camp at Clupea. The two Hannos, generals of the Carthaginians, came there again with a great army, and when battle was joined they lost nine thousand soldiers. But, as there was then never lasting good fortune among the Romans, and successes of whatever kind were at once buried under great masses of evils, when the Roman fleet was returning to Italy loaded with booty it was overturned in an unspeakable shipwreck: for of three hundred ships two hundred and twenty perished, and eighty were barely saved by throwing their cargoes overboard. Hamilcar, the general of the Carthaginians, sent with an army into Numidia and Mauretania, after he had acted in hostile and bloody fashion towards all because they were said to have received Regulus gladly, fined the rest a thousand talents of silver and twenty thousand oxen, and fixed the leading men of all the peoples to the gibbet.

In the third year (as unconquered fury always quickly forgets dangers) the consuls Servilius Caepio and Sempronius Blaesus crossed to Africa with two hundred and sixty ships, laid waste the whole sea coast that lies around the Syrtes, and, advancing inland, captured and overthrew very many cities and carried off great booty to the fleet. Then, when they were returning to Italy, near the promontory of Palinurus, which runs out into the deep from the Lucanian mountains, they were dashed on the rocks and unhappily lost a hundred and fifty transport ships and the famous booty cruelly acquired. At last, among the Romans, the enormity of miseries overcame a most wicked greed: for the fathers, who were now weary of seafaring, decreed that no larger fleet than sixty ships should be kept for the defence of Italy; a decree that they soon broke, driven by untamed greed. Moreover the consul Cotta, crossing into Sicily, fought very many battles by land and sea against the Carthaginians and Sicilians, and left unburied heaps of slain throughout all Sicily, partly of enemies and partly also of allies.

In the consulship of L. Caecilius Metellus and C. Furius Placidus, Hasdrubal, a new general of the Carthaginians, came from Africa to Lilybaeum with a hundred and thirty elephants and more than thirty thousand horse and foot, and at once joined battle with the consul Metellus near Panormus. But Metellus, fearing the great force of the beasts, first, using a great plan, drove them either to flight or to death, and so easily overcame the force of the enemy, great as it was. Twenty thousand Carthaginians were killed in that battle; twenty-six elephants too were killed and a hundred and four captured, and, led through Italy, they provided the peoples of Italy with a very great spectacle. Hasdrubal fled to Lilybaeum with a few, and was condemned to death in his absence by the Carthaginians.

## 4.10

After this the Carthaginians, wearied by so many evils, decided that peace must be sought from the Romans. For this they thought that Atilius Regulus, formerly a Roman general, whom they had now held captive for five years, should be sent above all among the other envoys. When he returned from Italy without having obtained peace, they cut off his eyelids, bound him in a machine and killed him by keeping him awake. Then another Atilius Regulus and Manlius Vulso, both consuls for the second time, set out for Lilybaeum with a fleet of two hundred ships and four legions. When the Romans tried to besiege this town, set on a promontory, they were defeated by Hannibal, who was the son of Hamilcar, coming up, and, having lost the greater part of their army, they themselves barely escaped. After them the consul Claudius set out against the enemy with a fleet of a hundred and twenty ships for the harbour of Drepana, where he was soon met by the fleet of the Carthaginians and overcome. He himself fled with thirty ships to the camp at Lilybaeum; all the rest, that is ninety, were either captured or sunk; eight thousand soldiers are said to have been killed and twenty thousand captured. Gaius Junius too, the colleague of Claudius, lost his whole fleet by shipwreck.

In the following year too the Punic fleet crossed into Italy and laid waste very many parts of it far and wide. Meanwhile Lutatius, carried over to Sicily with a fleet of three hundred ships, while he was stirring up a fight among the foremost near the city of Drepana, was pierced through the thigh and, when he was already being overwhelmed, was snatched away with the greatest difficulty. Then the Carthaginians, with four hundred ships and great forces, under Hanno as general, hurried to Sicily. Nor was Lutatius slower; rather, he forestalled the plans of the Carthaginians with wonderful speed. After the fleets of both had halted very near each other at the Aegates islands through the whole night, with their anchors almost interwoven, at dawn Lutatius first gave the signal for battle. As the fight grew cruel, Hanno, defeated, turned his ship and was the first leader of the flight. A considerable part of his army made for Africa with him; others fled to Lilybaeum. Sixty-three Punic ships were captured and a hundred and twenty-five sunk; thirty-two thousand men were captured and fourteen thousand killed; of the Romans twelve ships were sunk. Lutatius then came to the city of Eryx, which the Carthaginians held, and there, joining battle, killed two thousand Carthaginians.

## 4.11

Then the Carthaginians, in headlong haste, sent to the consul Lutatius and then to Rome; they begged for peace, which they at once obtained on the terms proposed before. The terms were that they should withdraw from Sicily and Sardinia and, for the costs of the war, pay three thousand Euboic talents of pure silver in equal instalments over twenty years. This peace treaty was made after the twenty-third year from the beginning of the first Punic war.

Who, I ask, could set out in speech one war of two cities waged for twenty-three years: how many kings of the Carthaginians, how many consuls of the Romans, how many columns of armies, how great a number of ships it gathered, routed and overwhelmed? And then at last, if those things seem fully weighed, let judgement be given about the present.

In the 507th year from the founding of the city a sudden overthrow of Rome itself forestalled the triumph of the Romans. For I would not say it rashly, since there was no time when even a moderate joy came on Rome without a most grievous mourning suddenly crushing it. For in the consulship of Q. Lutatius Catulus and A. Manlius various disasters of fire and water almost consumed the city. For the Tiber, swollen by unusual rains and overflowing beyond belief in either duration or size, destroyed all the buildings of Rome set on the level ground. Different qualities of places came together to one ruin, since both what the slower flood held it soaked and dissolved, and what the course of the torrent found it struck and threw down. The most grievous disaster of the waters was followed by a heavier devastation of fire. This fire, it is uncertain where it rose from, roamed through very many parts of the city and both made a wretched slaughter of men and houses and consumed in one burning as much wealth as very many foreign victories could not bring in. Then, while it was laying waste everything round the forum, it seized the temple of Vesta, and, without even the gods helping themselves, a temporary fire overwhelmed that fire which was thought eternal; so that Metellus too, while he was snatching away the gods who were about to burn, barely fled, with his arm half burned.

## 4.12

In the consulship of T. Sempronius Gracchus and C. Valerius Falto the Romans made war on the Falisci, and in that battle fifteen thousand Falisci were killed. In the same year the Cisalpine Gauls rose up as new enemies. Against them they fought with varied fortune. For in the first conflict, under the consul Valerius, three thousand five hundred Romans fell; in the second fourteen thousand Gauls were killed and two thousand captured, but because of the earlier disaster a triumph was refused to the consul.

In the consulship of T. Manlius Torquatus and C. Atilius Bulbus the island of Sardinia rebelled at the instigation of the Carthaginians. So the Sardinians were soon subdued and crushed; and it was decreed that war be made on the Carthaginians as violators of the peace that they themselves had asked for. The Carthaginians, on the other hand, asked as suppliants for peace; and when they had achieved nothing by envoys sent twice, and afterwards did not obtain it even when ten leading men likewise begged twice, at last they earned it by the speech of Hanno, the least man among the envoys. In this year the gate of Janus Geminus was shut, because there was no war anywhere in that year; which had happened only under King Numa Pompilius.

Here at last we should be silent, and it is expedient to pass over in silence the times with which ours can in no way be compared, so that we do not rouse by this din the detractors of the days of their own life to insult themselves rather. See, the gates of Janus were shut, there was no war of the Romans abroad, Rome, holding all its offspring resting in its lap, did not sigh. And when was this? After the first Punic war. After how long? After 440 years. For how long? For one year. And what followed in the next? To say nothing of the rest, the Gallic war, and Hannibal with the second Punic war. Woe is me, that I should have learned and laid bare things of which even I am ashamed! Was that peace of one year, or rather shadow of peace, a soothing of miseries or an incentive to evils? Did that drop of oil falling into the midst of a great flame put out the fuel of so great a fire, or feed it? Did a little cold water drunk in the most burning fevers heal the sick man, or rather inflame him? For almost seven hundred years, that is from Tullus Hostilius to Caesar Augustus, only in one summer did the Roman vitals not sweat blood; and among the very many ages of great centuries, the wretched city, truly a wretched mother, scarcely at one time rested from the fear of mourning, not to say from mourning itself. If any man had had so little quiet in his life, would he even be said to have lived? Or if anyone is driven by pains and tortures for a whole year, but in the middle of the year spends only one day tranquil and without struggles, will he take relief from his evils from this, and not count the whole year among miseries? But they, it is said, set this year as a glorious sign of untiring valour. And would that they had passed it over, so as to forget the continuous calamity! For as in the body of a man leprosy is recognised only if a different colour appears here and there among the sound parts of the skin, but if it spreads itself everywhere so as to make everything of one colour, though a false one, that distinction perishes: so, if a continuous labour had flowed on with an equal endurance and without desire for breathing space, it would be called an intent of the will and a choice of custom. But when either the joys of the elders or the studies of the younger lean so much on this tiny bit of leisure, it is certainly discerned both what delight this brevity had and what bitterness that length had: that is, how welcome that quiet would have been if it had remained lasting, and how this unceasing misery should even have been avoided, if it could have been avoided in any way.

## 4.13

In the 517th year from the founding of the city Hamilcar, the general of the Carthaginians, was killed by the Spaniards in war while he was secretly preparing another war against the Romans.

In the following year envoys of the Romans were killed by the Illyrians. Afterwards a most savage war was waged with the Illyrians themselves, in which, after many towns and peoples had been destroyed, the rest surrendered to the consuls Fulvius and Postumius.

In the third year after that, the evilly powerful pontiffs defiled the wretched city with sacrilegious sacrifices. For the decemvirs, going beyond the custom of ancient superstition, buried alive in the cattle market a Gaulish man and a Gaulish woman together with a Greek woman. But this magical binding was at once turned to the contrary: for they expiated those dreadful deaths of foreigners that they had caused by the foulest slaughter of their own. For in the consulship of L. Aemilius Catulus and C. Atilius Regulus the senate was struck with great dread by the revolt of Cisalpine Gaul, when a great army was also reported to be approaching from farther Gaul, above all of the Gaesatae, which is the name not of a people but of Gaulish mercenaries. So the consuls, alarmed, gathered the strength of all Italy for the protection of the empire. When this was done there are said to have been eight hundred thousand armed men in the army of the two consuls, as the historian Fabius, who was present at that war, wrote. Of these there were 299,200 foot and 26,600 horse of the Romans and Campanians; the rest of the multitude were allies. When battle was joined at Arretium the consul Atilius was killed; eight hundred thousand Romans fled when a part of them was killed not even so great as ought to have frightened them: for the historians record that three thousand of them were then killed. This is the more ignominious and shameful, that such great columns scattered when so few were lost, because it shows that in other victories they had prevailed not by strength of spirit but by the fortunes of war. For who, I ask, would believe that this number was even in the Roman army, I do not say that it fled? After this a second battle was fought with the Gauls, in which fully forty thousand Gauls were slaughtered.

In the following year the consuls Manlius Torquatus and Fulvius Flaccus were the first to lead the Roman legions across the Po. There they fought with the Insubrian Gauls, of whom twenty-three thousand were killed and five thousand captured.

Then in the year next to this dreadful prodigies terrified the wretched city: wretched indeed, since it was terrified on one side by the roar of enemies and on the other by the wickedness of demons. For in Picenum a river flowed with blood, among the Etruscans the sky was seen to burn, and at Ariminum late at night a clear light shone out and three moons appeared risen in distant regions of the sky. Then too, by a great earthquake, Caria and the island of Rhodes were so shaken that, as houses fell everywhere, that huge Colossus too fell. In the same year the consul Flaminius, scorning the auspices by which he was forbidden to fight, engaged the Gauls and won. In that war nine thousand Gauls were killed and seventeen thousand captured.

After this the consul Claudius destroyed thirty thousand Gaesatae, where he himself, advancing into the front line, also killed King Viridomarus; and among the many towns of the Insubres whom he had forced to surrender, he took also Mediolanum, a most flourishing city.

Then the Histri were roused as new enemies, whom the consuls Cornelius and Minucius subdued, with much Roman blood indeed. Here that ancient appetite of the Romans for wicked praise, even from the murder of kin, comes up a little. For Fabius Censorius killed his son Fabius Buteo, accused of theft: a deed worthy, no doubt, that a father should think it must be punished even by the murder of his son, for which not even the laws decreed anything against any man but a money fine or at most exile.

## 4.14

In the 534th year from the founding of the city Hannibal, the general of the Carthaginians, destroyed Saguntum, a most flourishing city of Spain and a friend of the Roman people, in only the eighth month: having first attacked it with war, then surrounded it with siege and tortured it with famine, while it bravely endured everything, worthy and unworthy, out of regard for the loyalty it had pledged to the Romans. He also most insultingly kept from his sight the envoys of the Romans sent to him. Then, from hatred of the Roman name, which he had sworn before the altars to his father Hamilcar when he was nine years old (most faithful in this, though otherwise most faithless), in the consulship of P. Cornelius Scipio and P. Sempronius Longus he crossed the Pyrenees mountains, opened a way with the sword among the fiercest peoples of the Gauls, and came in only the ninth day from the Pyrenees to the Alps. There, while he overcame in war the mountain Gauls, who strove to drive him back from the ascent, and cut through the pathless rocks with fire and iron, he spent four days and only on the fifth, with the greatest toil, came to the plains. They put his army then at a hundred thousand foot and twenty thousand horse. The consul Scipio was the first to meet Hannibal, and when battle was joined at the Ticinus he himself was gravely wounded and escaped, freed from death itself by his son Scipio, then quite a boy in the purple-bordered toga, who was afterwards surnamed Africanus. Almost the whole Roman army was slaughtered there. Then a battle was fought, under the same consul, at the river Trebia, and again the Romans were overcome with an equal disaster. The consul Sempronius, learning of his colleague's misfortune, returned from Sicily with his army; he likewise engaged at the same river, lost his army and escaped almost alone. In that war, however, Hannibal too was wounded. Afterwards, when he was crossing into Etruria at the beginning of spring, he was seized by a storm on the top of the Apennines and for two days on end was shut in and weighed down by the snows with his army, motionless and stiff. There a great number of men, very many beasts of burden and almost all the elephants perished from the bitterness of the cold. But the other Scipio of that time, the brother of the consul Scipio, waged very many wars in Spain, and also defeated and captured Mago, a general of the Carthaginians.

## 4.15

Then too the Romans were terrified by dreadful prodigies. For the disc of the sun was seen to grow smaller; at Arpi shields were seen in the sky; the sun was also seen to fight with the moon; at Capena two moons rose by day; in Sardinia two shields sweated blood; among the Falisci the sky was seen to split as if with a great gap; at Antium bloody ears of grain fell into the basket of the reapers. So Hannibal, knowing that the consul Flaminius was alone in his camp, so as to overwhelm him the more quickly while unprepared, advanced at the beginning of spring and took the nearer but marshy road, at a time when the Sarnus, widely overflowing, had left the plains soft and loosened, of which it is said: "and the levels that the Sarnus waters." Advancing into them with his army, Hannibal, chiefly because of the mists that were breathed out from the marsh and took away the view, lost a great part of his allies and beasts of burden. He himself, riding on one elephant, the only one that had survived, barely escaped the difficulty of the road; but he lost an eye, with which he had long been ailing, through the violence of the cold, the wakefulness and the toil. When he was very near the camp of the consul Flaminius, he roused Flaminius to war by laying waste the places round about. This battle was fought at Lake Trasimene. There the Roman army, most unhappily surrounded by the skill of Hannibal, was slaughtered utterly; the consul himself was also killed. Twenty-five thousand Romans are said to have been killed in that battle and six thousand captured. Of Hannibal's army two thousand fell. This contest at Lake Trasimene was famous for so great a Roman disaster, above all since the ardour of the fighters was so intent that they did not feel at all a most grievous earthquake, which happened then to be so violent that it is said to have destroyed cities, moved mountains, split rocks and forced rivers backwards. The ruin at Trasimene was followed by the battle of Cannae, though the time of the dictator Fabius Maximus came between, who slowed Hannibal's attack by delaying.

## 4.16

In the 540th year from the founding of the city the consuls L. Aemilius Paulus and P. Terentius Varro, sent against Hannibal, through the impatience of the consul Varro most unhappily lost, at Cannae, a village of Apulia, almost all the strength of Roman hope. For in that battle forty-four thousand Romans were killed, though a great part of Hannibal's army was also cut down. Yet in no Punic war were the Romans brought so near the extremity of extermination. For there perished in it the consul Aemilius Paulus; twenty men of consular or praetorian rank were killed; thirty senators were captured or killed, three hundred noble men, forty thousand foot soldiers and three thousand five hundred horse. The consul Varro fled to Venusia with fifty horsemen. Nor is it doubtful that that would have been the last day of the Roman state if Hannibal had pressed on soon after the victory to break into the city. Hannibal, in testimony of his victory, sent to Carthage three measures of gold rings, which he had pulled from the hands of the slain Roman knights and senators. The last despair of the state was so great among the remaining Romans that the senators thought they should take counsel about leaving Italy and seeking homes elsewhere. This would have been confirmed on the motion of Caecilius Metellus, had not Cornelius Scipio, then a military tribune, the same who was afterwards Africanus, deterred them with drawn sword and rather forced them to swear in his words for the defence of their country. The Romans, daring to breathe again in hope of life as if from the underworld, created Decimus Junius dictator. He held a levy from the age of seventeen, of unripe and untrained soldiery, and gathered four legions from wherever he could. Then too he bound to the military oath, with the promise of freedom, slaves of proven strength and will, either offered or, if need be, bought at public cost. The arms that were lacking they took from the temples; private wealth was poured back into the needy treasury. So the order of knights, so the anxious plebs, forgetting their interests, took counsel for the common good. Junius the dictator too, recalling an ancient deed of Roman misery, by an edict, opened as if an asylum to make up the army, enrolled for military service, with impunity promised, whatever men were liable for crimes or debts; their number came to six thousand men. Campania, or rather all Italy, utterly despairing of the restoration of the Roman state, went over to Hannibal. After this the praetor L. Postumius, sent to fight against the Gauls, was cut down with his army.

Then, in the consulship of Sempronius Gracchus and Q. Fabius Maximus, Claudius Marcellus, appointed proconsul after being praetor, routed Hannibal's army in battle and, first after such great ruins of the state, gave hope that Hannibal could be overcome. The Scipios in Spain crushed in a very heavy war Hasdrubal, the general of the Carthaginians, who was preparing an army for Italy: for they reduced his army by thirty-five thousand soldiers, either killed or captured. Celtiberian soldiers, the first foreign force that the Romans began to have in their camp, were enticed by pay from alliance with the enemy and brought into their own camp. Sempronius Gracchus, the proconsul, was drawn into an ambush by a certain Lucanian, his host, and killed. Centenius Paenula, a centurion, asked of his own accord that a war against Hannibal be assigned to him; he was cut down by Hannibal with the eight thousand soldiers he had led out into battle. After him the praetor Cn. Fulvius was defeated by Hannibal, lost his army and barely escaped.

I am ashamed to recall it. For what shall I call it, the wickedness or the misery of the Romans? Or rather, more truly, a wicked misery or a wretched wickedness. Who would believe that at that time, when the treasury of the Roman people was begging a meagre pittance from private contributions; when the soldier in camp was nothing but a boy, a slave, a criminal or a debtor, and not even so sufficient in number; when the senate in the senate house seemed almost all new men; lastly when, with everything so diminished and broken, there was such despair that a plan to leave Italy was considered: at that time, when, as we have said, one war at home could in no way be borne, three wars overseas were taken on besides? One in Macedonia against Philip, the most powerful king of Macedonia; another in Spain against Hasdrubal, Hannibal's brother; a third in Sardinia against the Sardinians and the other Hasdrubal, a general of the Carthaginians; and besides this a fourth, that of Hannibal, by which they were pressed in Italy. And yet a brave despair in each turned out for the better, for in all these they fought by despairing and conquered by fighting. From which it is plainly shown that the times then were not calmer with leisure, but that the men were stronger in miseries.

## 4.17

In the 543rd year from the founding of the city Claudius Marcellus barely took, at the second assault, Syracuse, the wealthiest city of Sicily, which, when he had long besieged it, he could not storm, driven back by the machines of Archimedes, a Syracusan citizen gifted with wonderful genius. In the tenth year after Hannibal had come into Italy, in the consulship of Cn. Fulvius and P. Sulpicius, Hannibal moved his army from Campania and, setting out with great disaster to all through the territory of Sidicinum and Suessa by the Latin Way, encamped by the river Anio three miles from the city, to the incredible fear of the whole city. While the senate and people were anxious with various cares, the matrons too, mad with terror, ran along the battlements and were eager to carry stones onto the walls and to fight foremost in front of the walls. He himself with light-armed horse came in hostile fashion right up to the Colline Gate, and then drew up all his forces in battle line; but the consuls and Fulvius the proconsul did not refuse battle either. But when the lines, set out on both sides, had halted, in sight of Rome, the prize of the victor to be, so great a rain mixed with hail suddenly poured from the clouds that the disordered columns, barely keeping hold of their arms, gathered themselves into their camps. Then, when fair weather had returned and the forces had gone back into the plain and into line, a still more violent storm poured down again, restrained the boldness of mortals with greater fear and forced the terrified armies to flee back into their tents. Then Hannibal, turning to religion, is said to have said that the possession of Rome was not given him, now the will, now the power.

Let the detractors of the true God answer me at this point: was it Roman bravery or divine compassion that kept Hannibal from seizing and overthrowing Rome? Or perhaps those who were preserved disdain to confess what Hannibal both feared as victor and proved by withdrawing. If it is plain that that divine protection came through rain from heaven, and that rain itself is not supplied at fitting and needful times except through Christ, who is the true God, I think this too is known with sufficient certainty from such things and cannot be denied: above all now, when, as a proof of His power, it often happens that rain is asked for when drought is troubling, and in alternate turns now the gentiles, now the Christians pray, and never, by their own testimony too, has it happened that the longed-for rains came except on the day on which it was permitted that Christ be prayed to and Christians pray. Beyond doubt it is established that the city of Rome, through this same true God, who is Jesus Christ, ordering things according to the pleasure of His unspeakable judgement, was both preserved then for the belief of the faith to come and is now chastised in that part of it that is unbelieving.

But in Spain both Scipios were killed by the brother of Hasdrubal. In Campania Capua was taken by the proconsul Q. Fulvius; the leading men of the Campanians brought death on themselves by poison; Fulvius put to death by torture the whole senate of Capua, even though the Roman senate forbade it. When the Scipios had been killed in Spain, and everyone hesitated, struck with fear, Scipio, though quite a young man, offered himself of his own accord; and since the treasury was in a shameful want, on the motion of Claudius Marcellus and Valerius Laevinus, who were then consuls, all the senators openly brought their gold and silver, coined, to the quaestors for public use, so that they left nothing for themselves and their sons but a ring each and the bullae, and then for their daughters and wives only an ounce of gold each and no more than a pound of silver each.

## 4.18

Scipio, twenty-four years old, having drawn proconsular command in Spain, intent in his mind above all on vengeance for his father and uncle, crossed the Pyrenees and at the first attack took New Carthage, where the Carthaginians kept the greatest stores of pay, strong garrisons and great quantities of gold and silver; there too he captured Mago, the brother of Hannibal, and sent him to Rome with the rest. The consul Laevinus, returning from Macedonia, stormed Agrigentum, a city of Sicily, and there captured Hanno, the general of the Africans; he received forty cities in surrender and stormed twenty-six. Hannibal in Italy killed the proconsul Cn. Fulvius, and besides eleven tribunes and seventeen thousand soldiers. The consul Marcellus fought with Hannibal for three days on end. On the first day they parted in an equal fight; on the next the consul was defeated; on the third, victorious, he killed eight thousand of the enemy and forced Hannibal himself to flee with the rest into his camp. The consul Fabius Maximus again stormed and took Tarentum, which had revolted from the Romans, and there destroyed great forces of Hannibal with their general Carthalo himself; he sold thirty thousand captives and paid the proceeds into the treasury.

In the following year in Italy the consul Claudius Marcellus was killed by Hannibal with his army. Scipio in Spain defeated Hasdrubal, the general of the Carthaginians, and stripped him of his camp; moreover he brought eighty cities into his power either by surrender or by war; the Africans he sold under the crown, the Spaniards he let go without ransom. Hannibal trapped both consuls, Marcellus and Crispinus, in ambushes and killed them.

In the consulship of Claudius Nero and M. Livius Salinator, when Hasdrubal, Hannibal's brother, was coming from the Spains through the Gauls to Italy, and, ordered by the Carthaginians to join his brother with his forces, was bringing great auxiliaries of Spaniards and Gauls with him, he was betrayed to the consuls as having already come down from the Alps by a hastened arrival; forestalled by the Roman army without Hannibal's knowledge, he was killed with his whole army. For a long time indeed the outcome of the battle was uncertain, with the elephants above all troubling the Roman line. They were driven back by the Roman soldiers called velites, from flitting about. This kind of soldiery had been devised a little before: young men chosen for their nimbleness sat with their arms behind the backs of the horsemen, and soon, when they had come to the enemy, leaped down from the horses and at once, themselves on foot, drove the enemy back, while on the other side the horsemen by whom they had been carried fought. So by these velites the elephants were driven back, and when they could no longer be controlled by their own men, they were killed by a carpenter's chisel driven in between the ears. This way of killing the beast, when it was necessary, the same general Hasdrubal had been the first to devise. In this battle the river Metaurus, where Hasdrubal was defeated, was to the Carthaginians as Lake Trasimene, and Caesena, a city of Picenum, as the village of Cannae: for fifty-eight thousand of Hasdrubal's army were killed there and five thousand four hundred captured; and four thousand Roman citizens were found among them and recovered, which was a consolation to the victorious consuls. For eight thousand of their army also fell. The head of his brother Hasdrubal was thrown before Hannibal's camp. When he saw it, and at the same time learned of the disaster of the Carthaginians, in the thirteenth year after he had come into Italy he fled back to the Bruttii. After this, for a whole year, a rest from the tumult of wars seemed to come between Hannibal and the Romans, because there was a disquiet of diseases in the camps and both armies were driven by a most grievous pestilence. Meanwhile Scipio, having reduced all Spain from the Pyrenees to the Ocean to a province, came to Rome; made consul with Licinius Crassus, he crossed into Africa, killed Hanno, son of Hamilcar, the general of the Carthaginians, and destroyed his army partly by slaughter and partly by captivity: for he killed eleven thousand Carthaginians in that battle. The consul Sempronius engaged Hannibal and, defeated, fled back to Rome. Scipio in Africa attacked the winter quarters of the Carthaginians and others of the Numidians, both of which were not far from Utica, and at dead of night had them set on fire. The Carthaginians, in alarm, thinking that the fire had happened by chance, ran together unarmed to put it out, and so were easily overwhelmed by armed men. In both camps forty thousand men were consumed by fire and sword and five thousand captured; the generals themselves, wretchedly burned, barely escaped. Hasdrubal the general came as a fugitive to Carthage. So Syphax and Hasdrubal soon restored a very large army and engaged Scipio again, and, defeated, fled. Laelius and Masinissa captured Syphax as he fled; the rest of the multitude fled to Cirta, which Masinissa attacked and received in surrender; he led Syphax, bound in chains, to Scipio, who handed him over to Laelius to be taken to Rome with great spoils and very many captives.

## 4.19

Hannibal, ordered to return to Africa to help the weary Carthaginians, left Italy weeping, having killed all the soldiers of Italian race who were unwilling to follow. As he drew near the African shore, one of the sailors, ordered to climb the ship's mast and look out what region they were reaching, answered that he saw a ruined tomb; Hannibal, abhorring the omen of the words, changed course and landed his forces at the town of Leptis. He soon restored his multitude and came to Carthage, and then asked for a conference with Scipio. There, when the two most famous generals had long looked at each other, astonished with mutual admiration, the business of peace was left undone and battle was joined. Long arranged with great skill by the generals, carried on with great masses of forces and completed with the great strength of the soldiers, it brought victory to the Romans. Eighty elephants were either captured or killed there, and twenty thousand five hundred Carthaginians were killed. Hannibal, having tried everything both before the battle and in the battle, slipped away amid the tumult with a few, that is barely four horsemen, and fled to Hadrumetum. Afterwards he came to Carthage, thirty-six years after he had left it as a child with his father, and persuaded the senate, which was taking counsel, that there was no hope left except in seeking peace.

In the consulship of C. Cornelius Lentulus and P. Aelius Paetus peace was granted to the Carthaginians through Scipio by the will of the senate and people. However, more than five hundred ships were brought out to sea and burned in sight of the city. Scipio, already then surnamed Africanus, entered the city in triumph; and Terence, who was afterwards the comic poet, one of the noble Carthaginian captives, wearing the cap of freedom, which was the sign of the liberty granted him, followed behind the chariot of the man in triumph.

## 4.20

In the 546th year from the founding of the city the second Punic war, which was waged for seventeen years, was ended. It was followed at once by the Macedonian war. The consul Quinctius Flamininus, who drew it as his lot, after many and very heavy battles in which the Macedonians were defeated, gave peace to Philip. Then he fought with the Lacedaemonians, and, having defeated Nabis their leader, led before his chariot the noblest hostages, Demetrius, son of Philip, and Armenes, son of Nabis. The Roman captives who had been sold through Greece under Hannibal were all recovered, and, their heads shaved as a sign that slavery had been wiped away, they followed the chariot of the man in triumph. At the same time the Insubres, Boii and Cenomani, uniting their strength under Hamilcar the Carthaginian as leader, who had remained in Italy, laid waste Cremona and Placentia, and were overcome in a most difficult war by the praetor L. Fulvius. Afterwards Flamininus, as proconsul, subdued in war King Philip and with him the Thracians, Macedonians, Illyrians and many peoples besides who had come to help him. The defeated Macedonians lost their camp; Polybius writes that eight thousand of the enemy were killed on that day and five thousand captured; Valerius says that forty thousand were slaughtered; while Claudius records that thirty-two thousand were killed.

But this variety among the writers is certainly a falsehood; and the cause of the falsehood is surely flattery, while they strive to heap up the praises of the victor and to extol the valour of their country, either for those present or for posterity: otherwise, if the number had not been sought, it would not have been stated in any form. But if it is glorious for a general and a country to have destroyed very many of the enemy, how much more joyful for a country and happy for a general it can seem to have lost none or very few of its own. So it is most clearly evident that with the same shamelessness of lying by which the number of enemies killed is added to, the losses of allies lost are also diminished, or even passed over in silence altogether.

So Sempronius Tuditanus, crushed in war in Hither Spain, was killed with the whole Roman army. The consul Marcellus, crushed by the Boii in Etruria, lost a great part of his army; afterwards Furius, the other consul, came to his help, and so, laying waste the whole people of the Boii with fire and sword, they destroyed them almost to nothing.

In the consulship of L. Valerius Flaccus and M. Porcius Cato, Antiochus, king of Syria, preparing war against the Roman people, crossed from Asia into Europe. Then too Hannibal, ordered by the senate to be produced at Rome because of rumours spread about him among the Romans that he was stirring up war, set out secretly from Africa and went over to Antiochus. Finding him hesitating at Ephesus, he soon drove him into war. Then too the law that had been passed by Oppius, tribune of the plebs, that no woman should have more than half an ounce of gold or use a many-coloured garment or a carriage through the city, was repealed after twenty years.

In the consulship of P. Scipio Africanus, for the second time, and T. Sempronius Longus, ten thousand Gauls were killed near Mediolanum, and in the following battle eleven thousand Gauls and five thousand Romans were killed. The praetor Publius Digitius lost almost his whole army in Hither Spain. The praetor M. Fulvius defeated the Celtiberians with the neighbouring peoples and captured their king. Minucius, brought into the utmost danger by the Ligurians and trapped by the enemy's ambush, was barely freed by the energy of the Numidian horsemen. Scipio Africanus, sent among other envoys to Antiochus, even had a friendly conversation with Hannibal. But he left Antiochus with the business of peace undone. In both Spains very horrid and bloody wars were waged by the praetors Flaminius and Fulvius, for both peoples.

In the consulship of P. Cornelius Scipio and M. Acilius Glabrio, Antiochus, although he had seized Thermopylae, so as to be safer by its fortification because of the doubtful outcome of war, nevertheless, when battle was joined, was overcome by the consul Glabrio and barely fled from the battle with a few and reached Ephesus. He is said to have had sixty thousand armed men, of whom forty thousand are said to have been killed and more than five thousand captured. The other consul, Scipio, clashed with the people of the Boii, and in that battle killed twenty thousand of the enemy.

In the following year Scipio Africanus, having Eumenes, son of Attalus, as an ally, waged a naval war against Hannibal, who then commanded the fleet of Antiochus. Antiochus, when Hannibal had been defeated and put to flight and at the same time his whole army lost, asked for peace, and of his own accord sent back the son of Africanus, whom he had captured, it is uncertain whether while he was scouting or in battle. In Farther Spain the proconsul L. Aemilius was cut down by the Lusitanians with his whole army and perished. L. Baebius, setting out for Spain, was trapped by the Ligurians and killed with his whole army; it is established that not even a messenger survived from there, so that the people of Massilia took care to announce the slaughter itself at Rome. The consul Fulvius crossed from Greece into Gallograecia, which is now Galatia, and came to Mount Olympus, to which all the Gallograeci had fled with their wives and children, and there waged a most bitter war. For the Romans were heavily worn down from the higher places by arrows, sling-bullets, stones and other missiles, but at last broke through to close quarters with the enemy: forty thousand Gallograeci are said to have been killed in that battle. The consul Marcius, setting out against the Ligurians and overcome, lost four thousand soldiers, and had he not, defeated, fled quickly back into his camp, he would have suffered the same disaster of extermination that Baebius had lately received from the same enemies.

In the consulship of M. Claudius Marcellus and Q. Fabius Labeo, King Philip, who had killed envoys of the Roman people, earned pardon through the most modest prayers of his son Demetrius, whom he had sent as envoy; and soon, as if he were a friend of the Romans and a traitor to himself, his brother too serving as the agent of the murder, the father killed him with poison, while the wretched youth suspected no evil from either. In the same year Scipio Africanus, long an exile from a city ungrateful to him, died of illness at the town of Liternum. In the same days too Hannibal, at the court of Prusias, king of Bithynia, when he was being demanded back by the Romans, killed himself with poison; Philopoemen, general of the Achaeans, was captured by the Messenians and killed. Then in Sicily the island of Vulcan, which had not been there before, suddenly rose from the sea, to the wonder of all, and remains to this day. The praetor Q. Fulvius Flaccus routed twenty-three thousand men in a very great battle in Hither Spain and captured four thousand. Ti. Sempronius Gracchus in Farther Spain forced a hundred and five towns, emptied and shaken by wars, to surrender. In the same summer too L. Postumius killed forty thousand of the enemy in war in Hither Spain. Gracchus the praetor in the same place again stormed and took two hundred towns.

In the consulship of Lepidus and Mucius the most savage people of the Bastarnae, enticed at the instigation of Perseus, son of Philip, by the hope of booty and the chance of crossing the river Hister, was destroyed without a single battle or any enemy. For then the Danube, which is also the Hister, happened to be covered with thick ice and easily allowed a crossing on foot. So when an incalculable multitude of men and horses was crossing improvidently, all together in one very great column, the frost, cracking under the enormity of the weight and the shaking of the marchers, and the crust of ice sprang apart, and, overcome at last and broken, it abandoned in the middle of the whirlpools the whole column that it had long borne, and, drawn over it again, with the fragments hindering, drowned it. A few out of the whole people barely escaped by either bank, with their bodies cut to pieces.

In the consulship of P. Licinius Crassus and C. Cassius Longinus the Macedonian war was waged, which deserves to be counted among the greatest wars. For on the side of the Romans there were first all Italy, then Ptolemy, king of Egypt, Ariarathes of Cappadocia, Eumenes of Asia and Masinissa of Numidia. Perseus and the Macedonians were followed by the Thracians with King Cotys and all the Illyrians with King Gentius. So Perseus met the consul Crassus on his arrival, and when battle was joined the Romans, wretchedly defeated, fled. In the following fight, with almost equal disaster to both sides, they withdrew into winter quarters. Then Perseus, having routed the Roman army in many battles, crossed into Illyricum and took by fighting the town of Sulcamum, defended by Roman garrisons; there he partly killed, partly sold under the crown and partly led away with him into Macedonia a great multitude of the Roman garrisons.

Afterwards the consul L. Aemilius Paulus fought with him and won: for he killed twenty thousand foot in that war. The king slipped away with his cavalry, but was soon captured and led with his sons before the chariot in the triumph, and afterwards wasted away in custody at Alba. His younger son learned the craft of a coppersmith at Rome to bear his want, and was worn out there. Very many wars besides, with quite different outcomes, were waged everywhere among many peoples, which I have passed over for the sake of brevity.

## 4.21

In the 600th year from the founding of the city, in the consulship of L. Licinius Lucullus and A. Postumius Albinus, when a great fear of the Celtiberians had seized all the Romans and there was no one among them all who dared to go to Spain either as tribune or as legate, P. Scipio, who will afterwards be Africanus, offered of his own accord to serve in Spain, though he had already been assigned to Macedonia by lot. So, setting out for Spain, he caused great slaughter of the peoples, often performing the duty of a soldier rather than of a general; for he also met in single combat and killed a barbarian who challenged him. But the praetor Sergius Galba was defeated by the Lusitanians in a great battle, and, having lost his whole army, he himself barely slipped away and escaped with a few. At the same time the censors decided that a stone theatre should be built in the city. Scipio Nasica opposed its being done then in a most weighty speech, saying that this device for nourishing idleness and wantonness would be most hostile to a warlike people; and he so moved the senate that it not only ordered everything prepared for the theatre to be sold but even forbade benches to be set up for the games.

Therefore let our people (to whom whatever comes in their way apart from the delight of lust is an offence) understand that, for this reason, that they themselves feel and confess that they are weaker than their enemies, it is the theatres that must be blamed, not the times; and that it is not the true God, who forbids them to this day, who must be blasphemed, but their own gods or demons that must be abominated, who sought these things, demanding such a sacrifice as a deep enough proof of their greatness, since they fed no more on the blood of cattle poured out than on the valour of men struck down. For certainly then there was no lack of enemies or famine or diseases or prodigies; rather, there were very many then; but there were no theatres, in which (incredible to say) the victims of the virtues are slaughtered at the altar of luxury. It once seemed good to the Carthaginians to sacrifice men, but that wickedly presumed persuasion was soon given up; but from the Romans it was exacted that they spend themselves on their own destruction. It was done, it is done, it is loved and clamoured for to be done: those who would perhaps be offended at sacrificing a beast of their flock rejoice at killing the valour of their own heart. Let them rather blush before Nasica, those who think it a reproach to be cast at the Christians; and let them complain, not to us about the enemies they have always had, but to him about the theatre he had forbidden to be had.

So in Spain the praetor Sergius Galba, having received in surrender the Lusitanians who lived on this side of the river Tagus, who had come over of their own accord, killed them by a crime: for, pretending that he would deal with their interests, he set soldiers round them and struck all of them down, unarmed and off their guard. This afterwards, because of the treachery of the Romans, was the cause of the greatest uprising throughout all Spain.

## 4.22

In the 602nd year from the founding of the city, in the consulship of L. Censorinus and M. Manilius, the third Punic war arose. So, when the senate had decreed that Carthage must be destroyed, the consuls set out for Africa, and Scipio, then a military tribune, and they occupied the camp of the elder Africanus near Utica. There the Carthaginians were summoned and ordered to hand over their arms and ships; and, without delay, so great a quantity of arms was suddenly handed over that all Africa could easily have been armed from them. But the Carthaginians, after they had handed over their arms and had been ordered to leave the city and withdraw ten miles from the sea, turned their grief into despair, either to defend their city or to be buried with it and by it, and created two Hasdrubals as their generals. They set first to making arms, and made up the lack of bronze and iron with the metals of gold and silver. The consuls decided to attack Carthage, whose site is said to have been of this kind: embraced by a wall of twenty-two miles, it was almost wholly surrounded by the sea, except for an isthmus that opened for three miles. This place had a wall thirty feet broad, of squared stone, forty cubits in height. The citadel, whose name was Byrsa, covered a little more than two miles. On one side the wall of the city and of the Byrsa was common, overhanging the sea, which they call the Pool, because it is calmed by the barrier of a tongue of land stretched out before it. So the consuls, though they had destroyed a considerable part of the wall, shaken by machines, were nevertheless defeated and driven back by the Carthaginians. As they fled, Scipio protected them, driving the enemy back within the walls. Censorinus returned to the city. Manilius, leaving Carthage, turned his arms against Hasdrubal. Scipio, on the death of Masinissa, divided the kingdom of Numidia among the three sons of Masinissa. When he had returned to the neighbourhood of Carthage, Manilius stormed and sacked the city of Tezaga; twelve thousand Africans were killed there and six thousand captured. Hasdrubal, a general of the Carthaginians and grandson of Masinissa, was killed by his own men in the senate house with fragments of the benches, because of a suspicion of treachery. The praetor Juventius, engaging the Pseudo-Philip in Macedonia, was killed with the greatest disaster of the whole Roman army.

## 4.23

In the 606th year from the founding of the city, that is in the fiftieth year after the second Punic war, in the consulship of Cn. Cornelius Lentulus and L. Mummius, P. Scipio, consul of the previous year, setting about destroying Carthage by a final stroke, entered the Cothon. There, while the fighting went on for six continuous days and nights, the last despair drew the Carthaginians to surrender, asking that those whom the disasters of war had left might at least be allowed to be slaves. First a column of women came down, pitiable enough, then a still more unsightly one of men. For it has been handed down to memory that there were twenty-five thousand women and thirty thousand men. King Hasdrubal surrendered of his own accord. The deserters, who had seized the temple of Aesculapius, threw themselves down of their own will and were consumed by fire. The wife of Hasdrubal, with a man's grief and a woman's fury, threw herself and her two sons with her into the middle of the burning, the last queen of Carthage now making the same end in death that the first had once made. The city itself burned for seventeen continuous days and provided its victors with a wretched spectacle of the changeableness of the human condition. Carthage was destroyed, with every stone of its walls ground to dust, in the seven hundredth year after it was founded. The whole multitude of captives, except for a few leading men, was sold. So the third Punic war was ended in the fourth year after it was begun.

But to me, inquiring however studiously, yet a man of rather slow wit, the cause of the third Punic war, which Carthage kindled to such a degree that it was decreed it should justly be overthrown, has nowhere at all become clear; and this moves me above all: that if, as in the earlier wars, an evident cause and grievance had been inflaming them against a rising enemy, there would have been no need of deliberation. But since some of the Romans decreed that it must be destroyed for the perpetual security of Rome, while others thought that Carthage should be left unharmed in its condition for the perpetual care of Roman valour, which they would always spend on themselves out of suspicion of a rival city, so that Roman vigour, always exercised by wars, should not be loosened into languid sluggishness by security and leisure: I find that the cause arose not from any wrong done by the provoking Carthaginians but from the inconstancy of the Romans growing torpid. Since this is so, why do they charge to the Christian times their own dullness and rust, by which they are coarse outside and eaten away within? They who, almost six hundred years ago, as their own prudent and fearful men had foretold, destroyed Carthage, that great whetstone of their brightness and sharpness.

So I shall make an end of the volume, lest perhaps, by striking more violently, when the rust has been knocked off for the time, and I cannot draw out the needed sharpness, I find a superfluous roughness. Though I should by no means be afraid of a roughness in my way, if I found the hope of a sharpness within.

## 7.17

In the 944th year from the founding of the city Severus, an African by birth, a Tripolitanian from the town of Lepcis, who wished to be called Pertinax after the name of the emperor whose murder he had avenged, seventeenth from Augustus, obtained the empire, which had been left without a ruler, and held it eighteen years. Savage by nature and always harried by many wars, he ruled the state very bravely indeed, but with very great toil. He defeated and killed at Cyzicus Pescennius Niger, who had aspired to tyranny in Egypt and Syria. He put down with the sword the Jews and Samaritans when they tried to rebel. He overcame the Parthians, the Arabs and the Adiabeni. In the fifth persecution after Nero he tortured the Christians, and a great many of the saints in various provinces were crowned with martyrdom. This profane presumption of Severus against the Christians and the Church of God was followed at once by the vengeance of heaven. For immediately Severus was snatched, or rather dragged back, from Syria into Gaul to a third civil war. He had already waged one at Rome against Julianus, and another in Syria against Pescennius; the third was stirred up by Clodius Albinus, Julianus's partner in the killing of Pertinax, who had made himself Caesar in Gaul. In this war much Roman blood was shed on both sides. Albinus, however, was crushed and killed near Lugdunum. Severus, the victor, was drawn into the Britains by the defection of almost all the allies. There, after fighting many great and heavy battles, he decided to separate the part of the island he had recovered from the other, unconquered peoples by a wall. So he drew a great ditch and a very strong rampart, fortified besides with frequent towers, for one hundred and thirty-two miles from sea to sea. And there, at the town of Eboracum, he died of illness. He left two sons, Bassianus and Geta. Of these Geta was judged a public enemy and perished; Bassianus took the surname Antoninus and gained the throne.

## 7.18

In the 962nd year from the founding of the city Aurelius Antoninus Bassianus, also called Caracalla, eighteenth from Augustus, obtained the principate and remained in it not quite seven years. He lived more harshly than his father, and more intemperate in lust than any man, for he even married his stepmother Julia. While he was preparing war against the Parthians he was surrounded by the enemy between Edessa and Carrhae and killed.

After him Opilius Macrinus, nineteenth from Augustus, who was praetorian prefect, seized the empire with his son Diadumenus, but when a year had passed he was at once killed at Archelais in a mutiny of the soldiers.

In the 970th year from the founding of the city Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, twentieth from Augustus, obtained the empire and held it four years. This priest of the temple of Heliogabalus left no memory of himself but one infamous enough for debauchery, outrages and every obscenity. When a mutiny of the soldiers broke out, he was killed at Rome together with his mother.

In the 974th year from the founding of the city Aurelius Alexander, twenty-first from Augustus, was made emperor by the will of the senate and the soldiers, and for thirteen years he deserved the praise of justice. His mother Mamaea, a Christian, took care to hear the presbyter Origen. For at once, making an expedition against the Persians, he overwhelmed their king Xerxes, victor in a very great war. Using Ulpian as his assessor, he showed the state the greatest moderation; but he was killed at Mogontiacum in a mutiny of the soldiers.

## 7.19

In the 987th year from the founding of the city Maximinus, twenty-second from Augustus, was made emperor by the army without any will of the senate, after he had waged a successful war in Germany, and carried out a persecution of the Christians, the sixth from Nero. But at once, that is in the third year of his reign, he was killed by Pupienus at Aquileia and made an end of both his persecution and his life. He had launched the persecution against the priests and clergy, that is the teachers, chiefly because of the Christian household of Alexander, whom he had succeeded, and of his mother Mamaea, and above all because of the presbyter Origen.

In the 991st year from the founding of the city Gordian, twenty-third from Augustus, was made emperor and remained in it six years. For Pupienus, the killer of Maximinus, and his brother Balbinus, who had usurped the empire, were soon killed in the Palace. Gordian, still quite a boy, about to set out to the east for the Parthian war, opened the gates of Janus, as Eutropius writes. Whether anyone closed them after Vespasian and Titus I do not remember anyone having written, although Cornelius Tacitus records that they were opened by Vespasian himself a year later. So Gordian, after fighting great battles successfully against the Parthians, was killed through the treachery of his own men not far from Circesium, above the Euphrates.

## 7.20

In the 997th year from the founding of the city Philip, twenty-fourth from Augustus, was made emperor, made his son Philip his partner in the rule, and remained in it seven years. He was the first of all the emperors to be a Christian, and after the third year of his reign the thousandth year from the founding of Rome was completed. So this birthday year, the most august of all that had gone before, was celebrated with magnificent games by a Christian emperor. Nor is there any doubt that Philip referred the thanks and honour for so great a devotion to Christ and the Church, since no writer shows either that there was a procession up to the Capitol or that victims were sacrificed according to custom. Both, however, though in different places, were killed in a mutiny of the soldiers and by the treachery of Decius.

## 7.21

In the 1004th year from the founding of the city Decius, the instigator and the suppressor of a civil war, when the Philips had been killed, seized the empire, twenty-fifth from Augustus, and held it three years. He at once, to show that he had killed Philip for this reason too, sent out deadly edicts for the persecution and killing of the Christians, seventh after Nero, and sent a great many of the saints from their crosses to the crowns of Christ. He also chose his son as Caesar, and was killed together with him at once, in the very midst of the barbarians.

In the 1007th year from the founding of the city Gallus Hostilianus, twenty-sixth from Augustus, obtained the throne and held it with his son Volusianus for barely two years. The vengeance for the violated name of Christian breaks out, and as far as the edicts of Decius had run for the ruin of the churches, so far the plague of unbelievable diseases spreads. There was scarcely a Roman province, scarcely a city, scarcely a house that was not seized and emptied by that general pestilence. Gallus and Volusianus, notable for this disaster alone, were killed while they were preparing a civil war against Aemilianus, who was planning revolution. Aemilianus, however, was dead in the third month of the tyranny he had seized.

## 7.22

In the 1010th year from the founding of the city two emperors were made in the twenty-seventh place after Augustus: Valerian was hailed Augustus by the army in Raetia, while at Rome Gallienus was made Caesar by the senate. Gallienus remained on the throne unhappily for fifteen years, while the human race breathed again for a little from that pestilence, more continuous and heavier than usual. Forgetful malice provokes its own punishment. For impiety, when it is scourged, feels the lashes indeed, but hardened, it does not feel by whom it is scourged. To say nothing of earlier times, when Decius had made his persecution of the Christians, a great pestilence afflicted the whole Roman Empire. Wickedness lied to itself, caught by its own crooked judgement to its ruin, that the pestilence was a common chance, and that death from disease was the end set by nature and not a punishment. So once again, and in a short time, it provokes the anger of God by wicked deeds, about to receive a blow that it would be forced to remember for some while. For Valerian, eighth from Nero, as soon as he had seized the empire, ordered Christians to be forced by torture into idolatry and those who refused to be killed, and the blood of the saints was shed through the whole breadth of the Roman realm. At once Valerian, the author of the wicked edict, was captured by Shapur, king of the Persians, and the emperor of the Roman people grew old among the Persians in a most shameful slavery. As long as he lived he was condemned to this continual degrading office: bending to the ground, he always raised the king when he was about to mount his horse, not with his hand but with his back. And Gallienus, frightened by so clear a judgement of God and shaken by the wretched example of his colleague, restored peace to the churches with anxious amends. But the captivity of one impious man, however lasting and however beyond measure abominable, does not make up the full measure of the wrong and of the vengeance for so many thousands of saints tortured; and the blood of the righteous, crying to God, asks to be avenged in the same land where it was shed. For it was not of the maker of the order alone that punishment was demanded by just judgement, but it was just that the executors, informers, accusers, spectators and judges, and last of all everyone who assented to that most unjust cruelty even by silent goodwill (for God knows what is hidden), should be struck by the same blow of vengeance; and these were the greater part of mankind, in every province. Suddenly, by God's leave, the peoples who had been placed round about and left for this purpose are let loose on every side, and with the reins slackened they burst into all the territories of the Romans. The Germans pass through the Alps, Raetia and all Italy and come as far as Ravenna; the Alamanni roam through the Gauls and cross into Italy as well; Greece, Macedonia, Pontus and Asia are wiped out by a flood of Goths; for Dacia beyond the Danube is taken away for ever; the Quadi and Sarmatians lay waste the Pannonias; the farther Germans strip Spain and take possession of it; the Parthians take away Mesopotamia and scrape Syria bare. There still stand in various provinces, among the ruins of great cities, small and poor dwellings that keep the signs of the miseries and the evidence of the names; among them we too can show our own Tarraco in Spain, for consolation in a recent misery. And so that no part of the Roman body should be spared this tearing apart, tyrants conspire within, civil wars arise, and a great deal of Roman blood is shed everywhere, as Romans and barbarians rage. But soon the anger of God is turned to mercy, and the vengeance begun is reckoned more as a warning than as a punishment in its full measure. So first Genuus, who had taken up the purple of empire, is killed at Mursa. Postumus seized tyranny in Gaul, to the great benefit of the state indeed: for ten years, using great courage and moderation, he both drove out the enemies who were lording it and restored the lost provinces to their former appearance; he was, however, killed in a mutiny of the soldiers. Aemilianus was crushed at Mogontiacum when he was plotting revolution. After the death of Postumus, Marius seized the empire in the same place but was at once killed. Then Victorinus was made emperor by the Gauls of their own accord and was killed after a little while. He was succeeded by Tetricus, who was then governing as praeses of Aquitania. He endured many mutinies of the soldiers. But in the east, by a certain Odenathus, who gathered a band of countrymen, the Persians were defeated and driven back, Syria was defended and Mesopotamia recovered, and the countrymen of Syria, with their Odenathus, came conquering as far as Ctesiphon. Gallienus, however, when he had abandoned the state and was a slave to his lusts at Mediolanum, was killed.

## 7.23

In the 1025th year from the founding of the city Claudius, twenty-eighth, took up the empire by the will of the senate, and at once attacked the Goths, who had been laying waste Illyricum and Macedonia for fifteen years already, and destroyed them with incredible slaughter. The senate decreed him a golden shield in the senate house and an equally golden statue on the Capitol; but at once, at Sirmium, before he had completed two years in the empire, he was seized by illness and died.

When Claudius was dead his brother Quintillus was chosen emperor by the army, a man of unique moderation and the only one to be preferred to his brother; he was killed on the seventeenth day of his reign.

In the 1027th year from the founding of the city Aurelian, twenty-ninth, obtained the empire and held it five years and six months, a man most excellent in military energy. Taking up an expedition to the Danube, he routed the Goths in great battles and established Roman sway at its ancient boundaries. Then, turning to the east, he brought under his power Zenobia, who after the killing of her husband Odenathus was claiming for herself the Syria he had recovered, more by the terror of battle than by battle. Tetricus, who in Gaul was quite unable to withstand the mutinies of his soldiers and was even writing, "Rescue me, unconquered one, from these evils", and so was the betrayer of his own army, he overcame without effort. So, the recoverer of the east and the north, he celebrated a triumph with great glory. He surrounded the city of Rome with stronger walls. At the last, when he was decreeing a persecution against the Christians, ninth from Nero, a thunderbolt fell before him, to the great terror of those standing about, and not long after he was killed on a journey.

## 7.24

In the 1032nd year from the founding of the city Tacitus, thirtieth, obtained the empire and was killed in Pontus in the sixth month. After him Florianus, who had an equal lot in the rule, was killed at Tarsus in only the third month.

In the 1033rd year from the founding of the city Probus, thirty-first, obtained the throne and held it six years and four months. Through many heavy battles, the enemies at last destroyed, he completely freed the Gauls, which had long been occupied by barbarians. Then he waged two civil wars, with a great deal of blood: one in the east, in which he crushed and captured Saturninus, who was relying on a tyranny; the other in which he defeated Proculus and Bonosus in great battles at Agrippina and killed them. He himself, however, was killed at Sirmium in an iron tower in a mutiny of the soldiers.

In the 1039th year from the founding of the city Carus of Narbo, thirty-second, took up the empire and held it two years. When he had made his sons Carinus and Numerian partners in the rule, in the Parthian war, after he had taken Coche and Ctesiphon, two very famous cities of the Parthians, he was struck by lightning in his camp above the Tigris and died. Numerian, who had been with his father, was killed on the way back through the treachery of his father-in-law Aper.

## 7.25

In the 1041st year from the founding of the city Diocletian, thirty-third, was chosen emperor by the army and was emperor twenty years. As soon as he had the means of power he killed with his own hand Aper, the killer of Numerian. Then, in a very difficult war and with very great toil, he overcame Carinus, whom Carus had left as Caesar in Dalmatia, and who was living shamefully. Next, when in Gaul Amandus and Aelianus had gathered a band of country people, whom they called Bacaudae, and stirred up destructive disorders, he made Maximian, surnamed Herculius, Caesar and sent him into the Gauls; and he easily settled the unskilled and disorderly band of rustics with military valour. Then a certain Carausius, of the lowest birth indeed but quick in counsel and in action, had been posted to watch the shores of the Ocean, which the Franks and Saxons were then infesting. He was acting more to the ruin than to the advancement of the state: the booty he took from the pirates he gave back in no part to its owners but claimed for himself alone, and so he roused the suspicion that he was letting the enemies themselves raid the frontiers by a crafty negligence. When for this reason Maximian ordered him to be killed, he took up the purple and seized the Britains. So the crash of sudden disorders sounded through all the frontiers of the Roman Empire: Carausius was rebelling in the Britains, Achilleus in Egypt, while the Quinquegentiani were harassing Africa, and Narseh too, king of the Persians, was pressing the east with war. Disturbed by this danger, Diocletian made Maximian Herculius Augustus from Caesar, and chose Constantius and Maximian Galerius as Caesars. Constantius took as his wife Theodora, the stepdaughter of Maximian Herculius, and by her had six sons, the brothers of Constantine. Carausius, after claiming and holding Britain for himself very bravely for seven years, was at last killed through the treachery of his partner Allectus. Allectus afterwards held for three years the island he had taken from Carausius. Asclepiodotus, the praetorian prefect, crushed him and recovered Britain after ten years. Constantius Caesar, in Gaul, when his army was routed by the Alamanni in the first battle, was himself barely snatched away; but in the second a quite favourable victory followed, for sixty thousand Alamanni are reported to have been cut down in a few hours. And Maximian Augustus tamed the Quinquegentiani in Africa. Then, too, Diocletian besieged Achilleus at Alexandria for eight months, took him and killed him. But using his victory without restraint, he gave Alexandria over to plunder and defiled all Egypt with proscriptions and killings. Besides, Galerius Maximian, when he had already fought two battles against Narseh, met him a third time between Callinicum and Carrhae and was defeated; having lost his forces, he fled back to Diocletian. Diocletian received him most arrogantly, so that he is said to have run for some miles in his purple before his carriage. Yet he used this insult as a whetstone for courage: by it the rust of royal pride was rubbed away and he sharpened the edge of his mind. So he soon gathered forces from all over Illyricum and Moesia, swiftly returned against the enemy and overcame Narseh with great strategy and strength. When the forces of the Persians had been destroyed and Narseh himself put to flight, he attacked his camp, captured his wives, sisters and children, plundered an immense quantity of Persian treasure, and led away a great many captives from among the Persian nobles. Returning to Mesopotamia he was received by Diocletian with the greatest honour. Afterwards the same commanders fought vigorously against the Carpi and the Bastarnae. Then they defeated the Sarmatians, and a very great multitude of captives from them they scattered among the garrisons of the Roman frontiers.

Meanwhile Diocletian in the east and Maximian Herculius in the west ordered the churches to be laid waste and the Christians to be afflicted and killed, in the tenth place after Nero. This persecution was longer and more savage than almost all those carried out before, for for ten years it went on without ceasing, with the burning of churches, the proscription of the innocent and the killing of martyrs. There followed an earthquake in Syria, by which, as roofs fell everywhere at Tyre and Sidon, many thousands of people were crushed. In the second year of the persecution Diocletian required of Maximian, against his will, that they should lay down the purple and the empire together, and that, when younger men had been put in their place over the state, they themselves should grow old in private leisure. So on one day Diocletian at Nicomedia and Maximian at Mediolanum laid down together the power of empire and its trappings.

Galerius and Constantius, the first Augusti to do so, divided the Roman Empire into two parts. Galerius Maximian held Illyricum, Asia and the east; Constantius, Italy, Africa and the Gauls. But Constantius, a most peaceable man, content with Gaul only [and Spain], gave up the other parts to Galerius. Galerius chose two Caesars: Maximinus, whom he set up in the east, and Severus, to whom he entrusted Italy, while he himself was based in Illyricum. Constantius Augustus, a man of the greatest mildness and civility, died in Britain. He left Constantine, his son born of the concubine Helena, as emperor of the Gauls.
